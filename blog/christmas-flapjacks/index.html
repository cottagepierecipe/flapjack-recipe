Christmas Flapjacks: Festive Recipes to Bake and Share

By Michael

A Christmas flapjack is the classic golden syrup oat bar wearing its festive clothes: mincemeat stirred through the oats, warm spice, orange zest and a snowy drizzle of white chocolate. It uses the same tin, the same oven temperature and the same press-and-bake method as the classic, so if you can make the plain version you can make this one.

The short answer: Melt 175g butter with 100g golden syrup and 100g light brown sugar — less syrup and sugar than the classic, because mincemeat brings its own sweetness and moisture. Stir in 200g mincemeat, 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt, press into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes. Cool in the tin, cut into 12 bars, and drizzle with white chocolate if you like. The bars keep for about 5 days in an airtight tin and freeze well, so they suit baking ahead for gifts.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Mincemeat flapjacks: why the classic formula changes

The main recipe on this page is a mincemeat flapjack, and it is not simply the classic flapjack with a jar tipped in. Mincemeat — the sweet mixture of dried fruit, peel, spices and suet sold in jars for mince pies — is both wet and very sweet. If you added 200g of it to the classic base without changing anything else, the slab would carry far more liquid than the oats can absorb and far more sugar than it needs. The result would be a soft centre that struggles to set and edges that catch and darken before the middle is done.

So the syrup drops from 175g to 100g and the sugar from 175g to 100g. The butter stays at 175g, because the fat is what coats the oats and carries the mincemeat evenly through the mixture, and the oats stay at 350g so the slab keeps its structure. What you get is a bar that tastes of Christmas — dark fruit, spice, a little sharpness from the peel — while still cutting cleanly and holding together in the hand. If you already make our fruit flapjack recipe, think of this as its festive cousin: there the fruit is soaked and drained, here the fruit arrives ready-mixed in the jar.

One practical point before you start: mincemeat varies. Some jars are loose and juicy, others are firm and packed. If yours looks very wet, hold back a spoonful of the golden syrup; if it looks dry and crumbly, the full amount is right. Traditional mincemeat contains suet, which may be beef suet or a vegetarian alternative — check the jar if that matters to anyone who will eat the bars.

IngredientAmountNotes

White chocolate (optional)75gFor the drizzle; melt gently and use once the bars are cold

Everything else is equipment you already own for the classic: a 20cm square tin, baking paper, a large saucepan and a spoon for pressing. The method is the classic method with the mincemeat stirred in with the oats, and the whole batch makes 12 bars.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Heat the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving paper overhanging two opposite sides so the cooled slab lifts out in one piece. Put the shelf in the middle of the oven.

  2. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put the butter, golden syrup and light brown sugar in a large saucepan and warm over a low heat, stirring, until everything has melted into a smooth, glossy mixture. Do not let it boil — a boiled mixture sets hard and can make the finished bars tough.

  3. Stir in the mincemeat. Take the pan off the heat and stir in the mincemeat until it is loosened and evenly mixed through the butter mixture. The mixture will look darker and thicker than the classic base; that is expected.

  4. Add the oats and salt. Tip in the porridge oats and the pinch of salt. Stir until every oat is coated and glossy and there are no dry patches hiding at the bottom of the pan. Check that pieces of fruit are spread through the mixture rather than sitting in one clump.

  5. Press into the tin. Tip the mixture into the lined tin and spread it into the corners. Press down firmly with the back of a spoon or the base of a glass, working across the whole surface so the slab is the same thickness throughout. Firm pressing is what stops the bars crumbling when they are cut.

  6. Bake until the edges are golden. Bake for 30–35 minutes until the edges are golden and the centre looks set, with the surface deepened in colour. Fruit on the surface should look rich and dark, not blackened. The centre will still feel slightly soft — it firms as it cools.

  7. Cool in the tin, then cut. Leave the slab to cool in the tin on a wire rack. Cutting while warm drags the fruit and tears the bars. Once cold, lift the slab out with the paper and cut into 12 bars with a large knife, pressing straight down.

  8. Drizzle with white chocolate, if using. Melt the white chocolate gently and drizzle it over the cold bars in thin lines from a spoon or a small piping bag. Leave until set before stacking or wrapping. Warm bars will melt the drizzle into a smear, so patience matters here.

Three more festive variations: spice, gingerbread and a white-chocolate finish

The mincemeat version is the headline recipe, but a tin of flapjacks is an easy thing to vary once the base method is familiar. The three variations below all start from the tested classic base — 175g butter, 175g golden syrup, 175g light brown sugar, 350g porridge oats, a pinch of salt — and change only the flavouring, so the tin, temperature and pressing stay the same. Only the mincemeat version needs the reduced syrup and sugar, because only mincemeat adds liquid.

VariationWhat changesBakeCharacter
Spiced fruitClassic base plus 2 tsp mixed spice, 100g mixed dried fruit and the zest of 1 orange, stirred in with the oats160°C / 140°C fan / Gas Mark 3, 30–35 minThe closest to a Christmas pudding flavour; soak the fruit for 10 minutes and drain well if it is very dry
Gingerbread spiceClassic base plus 2 tsp ground ginger, 1 tsp ground cinnamon and ½ tsp ground mixed spice; swap 50g of the light brown sugar for 50g dark brown sugar for a deeper colour160°C / 140°C fan / Gas Mark 3, 30–35 minWarm and fiery rather than fruity; good cut small, as the ginger builds
White-chocolate drizzleAny of the versions above, finished with 75g melted white chocolate drizzled over the cold barsAs the base version; add the drizzle only after coolingThe festive finish for gift boxes — thin lines look generous and use less chocolate than a full topping

A note on the spiced fruit version: dried fruit at the surface of a flapjack is exposed to the oven heat for the whole bake, so very dry fruit can scorch. The soaking habit from our fruit flapjack — 10 minutes in warm water, then drain and shake dry — protects it. The mincemeat version does not need this because the fruit in the jar is already moist.

Gifting and sharing: wrapping, keeping and freezing festive bars

Flapjacks suit Christmas gifting better than most bakes. They are sturdy, they travel without slumping, they need no refrigeration and a batch cuts into a dozen bars, which is a generous present from one tin. Bake them plain or drizzled, let them cool completely, and wrap them the same day they are cut so the edges stay fresh.

For wrapping, greaseproof paper is the reliable first layer: wrap each bar, or stack three or four with paper between them, then tie with string or pack into a small tin or a cellophane bag. Keep white-chocolate-drizzled bars in a single layer or separate them with paper, because the drizzle sticks to whatever touches it. A handwritten label with the flavour and the baking date is the useful finishing touch — the person receiving them may be saving them for a quiet moment in January, and the date tells them what they need to know.

For keeping times, the bars keep for about 5 days in an airtight tin at room temperature, in line with the guidance in our guide to how to store flapjacks. The mincemeat version keeps at least as well as the plain one because the fruit holds moisture. For longer keeping, freeze them: wrapped bars freeze well for up to 3 months, and our guide to freezing flapjacks covers wrapping and thawing in full. Freezing is the practical answer to December's crowded calendar — bake in a quiet week, freeze, and the gift boxes assemble themselves later.

Two last things worth planning around. First, portion size: Christmas flapjacks are rich, and a 12-bar cut gives substantial bars. For a mixed gift box alongside other bakes, cut the slab into 16 smaller squares instead — the bake is the same, only the knife work changes. Second, posting: flapjacks are one of the few bakes that survive the post well. Wrap tightly, pack the box so the bars cannot move, and choose the plain or spiced fruit versions, which travel better than the chocolate drizzle.
