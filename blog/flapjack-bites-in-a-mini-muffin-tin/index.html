Flapjack Bites in a Mini Muffin Tin

By Michael

Flapjack bites are the classic flapjack mixture — unchanged, gram for gram — baked in a mini muffin tin instead of a square one. You get 24 small round bites from one batch: crisp-edged, chewy in the middle, and exactly the size for a lunchbox or a child's hand. The mixture does not change. The shape does all the work.

The short answer: Make the classic mixture — 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado sugar melted together, with 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt stirred in. Spoon about 1 heaped tablespoon into each hole of a well-greased 24-hole mini muffin tin, press down firmly, and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 12–15 minutes. Cool in the tin for 10 minutes, then turn out. Makes 24 bites.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Flapjack bites: the same mixture, a smaller tin

There is no new recipe here, and that is the point. The mixture is our classic flapjack recipe exactly as tested — 175g each of butter, golden syrup and light muscovado sugar to 350g of porridge oats — divided into the holes of a mini muffin tin instead of being pressed into a 20cm square tin. Everything you already know about the classic applies: the gentle melt, the firm press, the golden edges. What changes is what the shape gives you. A bite is small, round and self-contained, with a much higher proportion of crisp edge to soft middle than a square cut from a slab. For lunchboxes, party plates and small children, that turns out to matter more than any change to the ingredients would.

Portioning is the other honest advantage. A slab cut into 12 gives you 12 bars whether you wanted 12 or not, and they are big bars. A batch of bites gives you 24 pieces, each roughly half a bar, which suits how people actually eat flapjack outside a pudding bowl: one piece with a cup of tea, two in a lunchbox, three if a small person has spotted the tin. Our post on packing flapjacks for lunchboxes and picnics covers wrapping and carrying them once they are made.

Why the bites bake faster: small pieces, hot metal all round

The bites take 12–15 minutes against the classic slab's 30–35, and the reason is simple geometry. A square of flapjack in a big tin is heated mainly from above and below, and its middle is a long way from any hot surface. A bite sits in its own metal cup: the tin conducts heat into it from the base and all round the sides, and no part of the mixture is more than about a centimetre from hot metal. Heat arrives faster and more evenly, so the bake is done in under half the time.

That speed cuts both ways. Small bakes go from golden to overdone quickly, because there is so little mixture in each hole to buffer the heat. Start checking at 12 minutes: the bites are ready when the edges are golden and the tops look set and dry, even if the centres still look fractionally soft — they firm up in the tin as they cool. And because the edges crisp more than a slab's do, a bite baked to the slab's colour will be too hard; pull them a shade paler than you would the classic.

You will also need a 24-hole mini muffin tin. Grease it properly: flapjack mixture is mostly sugar and syrup, and it welds itself to bare metal as it cools. Brush every hole with melted butter or oil, into the corners, or use mini paper cases, which make turning out foolproof and double as wrapping. Silicone mini muffin trays work well here too — our post on making flapjacks in a silicone mould covers the differences — but a metal tin gives the crispest edges.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and prepare the tin. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Grease a 24-hole mini muffin tin thoroughly with butter or oil, or line the holes with mini paper cases.
  2. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado in a large pan over a low heat. Stir until everything has melted into one smooth liquid. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil — boiled syrup sets like toffee and makes hard bites.
  3. Add the oats and salt. Take the pan off the heat, tip in 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt, and stir until every oat is coated and shiny.
  4. Fill the holes. Spoon about 1 heaped tablespoon of mixture into each hole. Work through the whole tin before you start pressing, so the mixture is divided evenly — the last holes should not be the thin ones.
  5. Press each bite down. Press the mixture firmly into each hole with the back of a teaspoon, or with a clean thumb. Pressing matters more in small holes than in a big tin: a loosely filled bite has nothing holding it together but hope, and it will crumble when it comes out.
  6. Bake for 12–15 minutes. The edges should be golden and the tops set and dry-looking. Take them out a shade paler than you would a slab — small bakes harden quickly, and they firm up as they cool.
  7. Cool in the tin for 10 minutes. This is part of the setting, not a rest you can skip. The bites come out of the holes fragile while hot and firm up as the syrup cools.
  8. Turn out onto a rack. Run a small knife or the handle of a teaspoon round any bite that sticks, tip the tin over a wire rack, and leave the bites to cool completely. In cases, lift them straight out once the tin is cool enough to handle.

Flavoured bites: small changes that suit the small tin

Because the mixture is the classic one, every variation that works in the classic works here — with one caution. Additions need to be small or finely chopped: a whole dried apricot or a big chocolate chunk is a lump in a bar but a structural fault in a bite, taking up half the hole. Chop everything to pea-size or smaller, and keep the total additions to about 100g so the oats still bind. A teaspoon of ground ginger or cinnamon stirred through suits the size well, as does the finely grated zest of an orange. Fifty grams of chocolate chips — the mini kind, if you can get them — fold through neatly. A few bites can also be finished differently from the same batch: press a single raspberry or a pinch of desiccated coconut into the top of each hole before baking, and one tin becomes a small selection box. For bigger flavoured batches, the same ideas are written up at bar size in our recipe collection.

Storing flapjack bites: about 5 days in an airtight tin

The bites keep about 5 days in an airtight tin at room temperature, like any flapjack — though their higher proportion of edge means they dry out a touch faster than a slab, so keep the tin sealed and somewhere cool. Paper cases, if you used them, can stay on in the tin; they keep the bites from sticking to each other. For lunchboxes, wrap bites in pairs in a little baking paper or pop them into a small tub — our guide to packing flapjacks for lunchboxes and picnics has the details. They freeze well for up to 3 months, sealed in a bag or box, and defrost at room temperature in about half an hour — the one size of flapjack that is ready by break time if you take it out at breakfast. The general rules are in our guide to how to store flapjacks and how long they last.
