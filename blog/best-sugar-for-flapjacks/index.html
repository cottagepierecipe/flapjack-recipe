Which Sugar Is Best for Flapjacks?

By Michael

Brown, white, caster, demerara, muscovado — the sugar shelf offers half a dozen plausible choices, and the one you pick changes a flapjack's chew, colour and flavour more than any other single swap. Here is what each sugar actually does in the pan and in the oven, and the one that earns its place in the classic recipe.

The short answer: Light brown sugar is the best all-round sugar for flapjacks. It melts smoothly into the butter, holds a little moisture so the bars stay chewy rather than dry, and brings a gentle caramel flavour that suits oats. It is the sugar in our classic recipe — 175g of it to 350g of porridge oats. Caster sugar makes a paler, cleaner-tasting bar; demerara adds crunch; dark muscovado is the one to choose when you want a deep, treacly flavour.

What Sugar Does in a Flapjack: Binder, Moisture and Set

Sugar in a flapjack is not there only for sweetness. Melted with the butter and golden syrup, it becomes part of the liquid that coats every oat, and as the slab cools that liquid sets and glues the bars together. Take too much sugar away and the mixture loses its binding — the bars dry out and crumble. That is why flapjack recipes keep their sugar quantities so steady, and why the choice of sugar changes the finished texture as much as the flavour.

The sugars differ in three ways that matter here. The first is molasses: brown sugars are white sugar with molasses left in or added back, and molasses brings colour, a caramel or treacle flavour, and a slight acidity that deepens browning. The second is moisture: molasses is hygroscopic, meaning it holds on to water, so brown sugars keep a bar softer and chewier for longer. The third is crystal size: fine sugars dissolve completely in the melted butter, while coarse crystals such as demerara may survive the short melting stage partly intact, and you taste them in the finished bar as crunch — or, if they never dissolve at all, as graininess.

Keep those three differences in mind and every sugar on the shelf becomes predictable. The rest of this page takes them one at a time, judged on the same job: sweetening 350g of porridge oats in a 20cm tin, baked at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3).

Light Brown Sugar for Flapjacks: The 175g in the Classic Recipe

Our classic flapjack recipe uses 175g of light brown sugar — the same weight as the butter and the golden syrup, against 350g of porridge oats. That is not an accident. Light brown sugar melts quickly and completely into the butter mixture without any risk of graininess, so the coating on the oats is smooth and even. In the oven it colours the slab to an even mid-gold, and its small amount of molasses gives the flavour a rounded caramel note that supports the oats and butter without shouting over them.

The moisture point matters most over a few days. A classic flapjack made with light brown sugar is still pleasantly chewy on day three or four, where a white-sugar version is already drying towards biscuit. If you bake flapjacks to eat across the week — and most people do — that slow drying is the strongest argument for light brown sugar as the default.

Light muscovado is its closest relation and swaps like-for-like by weight. Muscovado is less refined, slightly damper and a touch more flavourful; some bakers find it clumps in the bag, in which case rub the lumps out with your fingers before it goes in the pan. In the finished bar, light brown sugar and light muscovado are close enough that most people cannot tell them apart blind.

Demerara Sugar in Flapjacks: Coarse Crystals That Stay Crunchy

Demerara is the coarse, golden crystal sugar usually stirred into coffee. Its large crystals are the point and the problem. In the few minutes the butter mixture spends on the hob, demerara does not fully dissolve; what survives into the tin bakes into distinct crunchy flecks through the bar. Baked into a flapjack on purpose, that is a deliberate texture — a chewy bar with a crisp, sugary bite — and many people love it.

The risk is graininess. A bar made with 100% demerara can eat sandy, and the undissolved crystals also bind less well, so the slab is more likely to crumble at the edges. The reliable way to use demerara is as a part-swap: replace about half the light brown sugar with demerara — say 85g of each in the classic — and you keep the smooth binding and moisture of the brown sugar with a measured crunch on top. Demerara also makes an excellent topping: a spoonful scattered over the pressed mixture before baking gives a crisp, sparkling crust while the sugar inside the bar stays the dependable sort.

One more caution: because demerara dissolves slowly, give the pan mixture an extra stir and a few extra seconds on a low heat, and never let it boil to force the crystals down. Boiling the butter mixture is the classic route to a hard flapjack, whatever sugar you use.

Caster Sugar in Flapjacks: The Pale, Clean-Tasting Option

Caster sugar is white sugar ground finer, and it behaves accordingly: it disappears into melted butter almost at once, leaving no graininess at all. A flapjack made with caster is paler in colour and cleaner in flavour — the taste is of butter, oats and golden syrup, with the sugar simply sweetening and setting. If you find brown sugar's caramel note muddies a delicate version — a lemon flapjack, say, or one with white chocolate — caster is the right tool.

The trade-off is texture and keeping. With no molasses, caster holds less moisture, so the bars set a little firmer and dry a little sooner. Eaten on the day or the day after, you would barely notice; by day four the difference from a brown-sugar bar is clear. Caster also gives you less colour in the oven, which makes judging the bake slightly harder: go by the edges firming and the centre still looking soft, not by a deep golden top that will never arrive.

Dark Muscovado in Flapjacks: A Strong, Treacly Flavour

Dark muscovado sits at the far end of the brown sugar range: damp, intensely flavoured, and tasting of liquorice and treacle as much as of sugar. Used for the full 175g in a classic batch it can overwhelm the oats and turn the whole slab almost bitter at the edges, and its extra moisture makes for a softer, darker bake that browns quickly.

Used with restraint, it is superb. Swap a third to a half of the light brown sugar for dark muscovado — 60g to 85g — in a batch with ginger, or at Christmas, or alongside dark chocolate, and you get depth without heaviness. Watch the oven: the dark sugars colour fast, so check a few minutes early and trust the soft centre rather than the shade of the top. If a recipe is already giving you trouble with hardness, this is not the sugar to experiment with — read why flapjacks turn out hard first and fix the bake, then play with flavours.

White Granulated Sugar in Flapjacks: It Works, but It Is the Plainest Choice

White granulated sugar is what most cupboards hold, and the honest verdict is that it works — with reservations. Its crystals are larger than caster's, so it needs a gentle, patient melt to dissolve fully; rushed, it can leave a faint grain in the set bar. It brings sweetness and binding and nothing else: no caramel depth, no moisture-holding molasses, the palest colour of any sugar here.

If granulated is what you have, use it without worry and borrow two tricks from the other sugars: melt it slowly and completely with the butter and syrup before the oats go in, and eat the batch within a couple of days while it is at its best. A spoonful of demerara scattered on top restores some of the character the white sugar lacks. What granulated cannot do is improve with keeping — of all the sugars here, its bars stale fastest.

Swapping Sugars in the Classic Recipe: Weight for Weight, With Two Warnings

Because the sugars are all sucrose at heart, swaps in the classic recipe are made by weight: 175g of caster or light muscovado replaces 175g of light brown sugar without touching anything else, and the bake stays at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes. Two warnings cover almost every failed swap. First, treat demerara and dark muscovado as part-swaps only, for the reasons above — half at most. Second, do not cut the sugar back sharply to make the bars "healthier": sugar is structural here, and removing a third of it gives you a dry, crumbly slab rather than a virtuous one. If a lighter bar is the goal, our healthy flapjack recipe takes a different route altogether, sweetening with fruit instead of asking the classic to be something it is not.

One question worth settling: why does the classic use sugar and golden syrup? Because they do different jobs. The syrup's job is binding with a smooth, non-crystallising set — granulated sugars on their own can crystallise as they cool, one cause of a grainy bar — while the sugar brings the body and the caramel flavour. If you are adjusting one, read how much golden syrup a flapjack needs before you adjust the other; the two are balanced against each other.
