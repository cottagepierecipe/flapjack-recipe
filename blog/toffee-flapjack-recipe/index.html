Toffee Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

A toffee flapjack is the classic oat bar pushed towards bonfire night: dark muscovado sugar and a spoonful of black treacle in the base, and chopped hard toffee folded through the oats so it melts into pockets as the bars bake. The base tastes of toffee before the toffee even goes in, and the pieces give you chewy, dark-edged pockets all the way through.

The short answer: Melt 175g butter, 150g dark muscovado sugar, 3 tablespoons of golden syrup and 1 tablespoon of black treacle in a pan, stir in 350g porridge oats, a pinch of salt and 100g hard toffee chopped small, press into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes. Cool completely in the tin and cut into 12 bars.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Toffee flapjacks: a dark base with toffee melted through it

Most flapjacks taste of butter and syrup first and everything else second. This one reverses that. The base of our classic flapjack recipe — butter, golden syrup, sugar and oats — is adjusted so the toffee flavour is there before any toffee pieces are added: dark muscovado instead of light, black treacle alongside the syrup, and slightly less sugar overall because the toffee pieces bring their own. The result is a darker, deeper bar, closer to bonfire toffee than to a golden tea-time flapjack, with a slight bitterness at the edges that stops it being simply sweet.

The chopped toffee is the second layer of the idea. Hard toffee does not dissolve into the mixture the way chocolate chips do. In the heat of the oven it slumps and softens into pockets — chewy, almost liquid in a warm bar, firm and snapping once the bars are cold. Cut a bar across and you can see them: dark amber pools threaded through the oats. That contrast, soft oat crumb against chewy toffee, is the whole reason for making this version rather than stopping at a treacle base.

Toffee is not caramel: why the pieces hold their shape

Toffee and caramel start from the same short list — sugar, butter, sometimes syrup or cream — and the difference between them is temperature. Caramel is cooked to a lower temperature and stays a sauce or a soft layer. Toffee is cooked hotter, until much more of the water has boiled off, and it sets hard and brittle. That is why a bag of toffee pieces can be folded raw into a flapjack mixture without disappearing: the pieces were made to be hard, and the oven only just undoes that work. They soften at the edges, slump into the oats around them and keep their identity as pockets instead of melting away into the base.

It is also why the pieces must be chopped small — pea-sized, roughly. A big chunk of toffee has a long way to soften, and in a 30-minute bake it may not get there, leaving you with a lump that is hard enough to be a dental hazard in an otherwise soft bar. Small pieces soften through. The other practical point is the tin: molten toffee is determined to weld itself to bare metal. Line the tin properly, with paper up the sides, and nothing sticks. Our caramel flapjack takes the layered route instead — a separate caramel poured over the base — and the comparison table below sets out how the two sweets behave differently in a bake.

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper, with the paper coming up the sides — this is not optional with toffee in the mixture — and a large pan for the oat mixture. For chopping the toffee, put the pieces in a strong bag and hit them with a rolling pin, or work through them with a large knife; toffee shatters rather than slices, and the bag keeps the shards on the board instead of the floor.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides and making sure the paper covers the sides of the tin as well as the base.
  2. Chop the toffee. Break 100g of hard toffee into pieces about the size of a pea. A rolling pin and a strong plastic bag is the easiest way. Small pieces soften through in the bake; large ones stay hard. Set the pieces aside.
  3. Melt the butter, sugar, syrup and treacle. Put 175g butter, 150g dark muscovado sugar, 3 tablespoons of golden syrup and 1 tablespoon of black treacle in a large pan over a low heat. Stir until everything has melted into one dark, smooth liquid. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil — boiled syrup sets hard and makes hard bars.
  4. Add the oats, salt and toffee. Take the pan off the heat and tip in 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt. Stir until every oat is coated and glossy, then fold through the chopped toffee and stir a few more turns so the pieces are spread through the mixture rather than gathered in one place.
  5. Press into the tin. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working well into the corners. Firm pressing is what holds the bars together once cut, and it presses the toffee pieces down into the mixture where they belong.
  6. Bake for 30–35 minutes. The top should be a deep golden brown with the edges a shade darker, and any toffee showing at the surface should look melted and glossy. The centre will still feel slightly soft when pressed — it firms up as it cools.
  7. Cool completely in the tin. Leave the slab in the tin until it is cold, at least an hour. This matters more here than in a plain flapjack: cut too soon and the softened toffee is still liquid, and it will smear and pull instead of slicing.
  8. Lift out and cut into 12. Use the paper overhang to lift the cold slab onto a board and cut with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down. If a toffee pocket resists the knife, a gentle sawing motion gets through without tearing the bar.

Getting the balance right: treacle, dark sugar and salt

Three small decisions decide whether this recipe tastes of toffee or merely of sweet oats. The first is the sugar. Dark muscovado has a deep, almost liquorice-like flavour of its own, and it is doing real work here; light muscovado or light brown sugar will still make a good flapjack, but a paler and less interesting one. The second is the treacle. One tablespoon darkens and deepens the whole batch; two tablespoons push it towards bitterness and overwhelm the oats. Resist adding more — the toffee pieces bring plenty of intensity of their own.

The third is the salt, which looks like the least important ingredient on the list and is not. A flapjack is a sweet bake by design, and a toffee flapjack is sweeter than most. A pinch of salt stirred through the oats does not make the bars taste salty; it makes them taste more of everything else, and it keeps the finish from cloying. If your toffee is a salted toffee, the pinch in the mixture is still worth keeping — the two are doing different jobs, one in the crumb and one in the pockets.

Storing toffee flapjacks: about 5 days in an airtight tin

Keep the cut bars in an airtight tin at room temperature, with baking paper between the layers — the toffee pockets can make the bars slightly tacky where they touch, and the paper stops them sticking to each other. They keep about 5 days. The toffee pockets firm up over the first day, so the bars are at their chewiest on day one and snappier after that; both stages are good, just different. The bars freeze well for up to 3 months, wrapped individually and sealed; defrost in the wrapping at room temperature. The general rules are in our guide to how to store flapjacks and how long they last.
