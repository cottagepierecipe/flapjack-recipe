Can You Bake Two Trays of Flapjacks at Once?

By Michael

Two trays of flapjack mixture and one oven: it feels like the obvious way to double a batch, and it is — with two adjustments. An oven with two trays in it is a different oven from the one the recipe was written in. Trays block heat, interrupt airflow and shade each other, so the pair bake less evenly than either would alone. Swap them round halfway and give them a little longer, and the difference mostly disappears.

The short answer: Yes, especially in a fan oven. Put the trays on two shelves, leave gaps around each tin for the hot air to move, and swap the trays between shelves — turning each one round as you do — halfway through the bake. Expect the pair to need a few extra minutes over the single-tray time, and judge each tray separately at the end, because they rarely finish together. In a conventional oven without a fan the top shelf runs hotter, so the halfway swap matters even more.

What a second tray does to the oven

A recipe time assumes one tray in an oven full of moving hot air. Our classic flapjack recipe — baked at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes — assumes the heat reaches the tin from every side and the air circulates freely around it. Add a second tray and three things change. The trays shade each other: the lower tray is partly shielded from the top element by the tray above it, and the upper tray sits in the lower one's rising heat. The air has furniture in its way, so circulation slows and pockets of cooler air form. And there is simply twice as much cold, dense mixture drinking the oven's heat, so the temperature dips further when the trays go in and takes longer to settle. None of this stops the bake working. All of it means the two trays bake unevenly relative to each other, which is why the management below — spacing, swapping, judging separately — is about evening things out rather than rescuing anything.

Fan ovens: two shelves, one swap

A fan oven is built for exactly this. The fan moves the hot air around the trays, which evens most of the difference the second tray creates, and both shelves bake at close to the same rate. Put one tray on the middle shelf and one below it — or one above; the fan makes the order far less important than in a conventional oven. Use the fan temperature from the recipe, 140°C fan for our classic. Then, halfway through the bake, swap the trays: top to bottom, bottom to top, and turn each tray front-to-back as you move it, because most ovens are a little hotter at the back. The swap takes twenty seconds and costs a small puff of heat; it repays it by making the pair finish within a minute or two of each other instead of five apart.

Conventional ovens: the top shelf runs hotter

Without a fan, heat rises and stays risen: the top of a conventional oven is genuinely hotter than the bottom, and a tray on the top shelf will race ahead of one at the bottom — darker edges, faster set, the works. The halfway swap is not optional in this oven; it is the whole technique. Swap shelves and turn the trays at the midpoint without fail, and check the tray that spent its second half on top a few minutes early, because it had the hotter berth last. If your oven has a known fierce spot — most do — the turning matters as much as the swapping. Some bakers of very uneven conventional ovens prefer to bake the two trays one after the other instead, and for a small, old, hot-spotted oven that is a respectable choice: two even single bakes beat one lopsided double.

Spacing: leave the air somewhere to go

However the trays are arranged, they need room around them. Hot air that cannot pass a tray cannot heat what is behind it, so leave a clear gap between the two tins and between each tin and the oven walls — a few centimetres all round is the working rule. Two tins side by side on one shelf of a wide oven works well if the gaps are honest; two tins touching each other, or wedged against the walls, bake as one badly shaped lump of heat shadow. Never stack one tray directly on top of another, and do not put a tray on the oven floor. Deep tins crowd a small oven faster than shallow ones; if your two tins only just fit, that is the oven telling you it is a one-tray oven for this job, and the honest answer is two batches.

Timing: a few extra minutes, and judge each tray separately

Expect the pair to take a little longer than the single tray's time — a few extra minutes is the usual experience, because of the greater load of cold mixture and the interrupted airflow. Do not raise the temperature to compensate; hotter air darkens the exposed edges while the shaded parts lag further behind. Instead, treat the recipe time as the moment to start looking. And judge the trays as individuals: they have had different shelves and different airflow, and they will not finish in the same minute. Take out whichever tray is done — golden top, edges set, centre just set with the slight softness that firms on cooling, as our guide to telling when flapjacks are done sets out — and give the other its extra minutes. A tray held in a cooling oven "to keep together" with its slower partner just dries out; flapjacks are forgiving about many things, but not about waiting around in heat. If both trays are for the same occasion, none of this matters — our post on how to cut flapjacks will even out any differences in portioning anyway.

One practical note from the last row: if you do bake in two batches, the second tray of mixture waits while the first bakes. Half an hour in the tin on the worktop does it no harm at all. What does not help is pressing it and leaving it for hours, or leaving the mixture in a warm kitchen next to the oven — keep the wait short and the mixture somewhere cool and it bakes as well as the first.

What about three trays?

Three trays is where a domestic oven usually stops coping. With a tray on every shelf, the middle one is shielded from above and below at once, the airflow has almost nowhere to go, and the spread between the fastest and slowest tray grows past what one swap can repair. It can be done in a large fan oven with a swap and a shuffle at the halfway point and very patient, separate judging at the end — but the honest advice for a home oven is that two trays is the practical limit. The third batch bakes better, and barely later, going in as the first two come out.

What not to do

Three arrangements fail reliably, and all three are tempting when the oven looks small. Do not stack trays — a tray sitting on another tray shields the lower bake almost completely and the pair come out as one done, one raw. Do not cover one tray with foil "to protect it" while the other browns; the covered tray steams, sets late and comes out pale and soft in the way our post on why flapjacks are too soft describes. And do not open the door every few minutes to check on them — each opening dumps heat the oven then has to replace, which widens the gap between the trays. One confident swap halfway, a check near the end, and that is the whole job.
