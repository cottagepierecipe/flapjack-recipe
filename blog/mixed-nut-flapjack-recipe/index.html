Mixed Nut Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

This is the classic flapjack with a bowl of chopped nuts folded through it: 150g of mixed nuts, roughly chopped, in place of part of the oats. The bars come out knobbly and crunchy, with toasted nut edges against the soft caramel oats — but nuts change how the mixture presses and cuts, so the pressing is firmer and the slab must be properly cold before the knife goes in.

The short answer: Melt 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado sugar. Stir in 250g porridge oats, 150g mixed nuts roughly chopped and a pinch of salt. Press very firmly into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes. Cool completely in the tin — cold, not just cool — and cut into 12 bars. Please read the allergy note below before making these for anyone else.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Mixed nut flapjacks: 150g of nuts in place of 100g of the oats

The base is our classic flapjack recipe, kitchen-tested at 175g each of butter, golden syrup and light muscovado sugar with 350g of porridge oats. The one real change is the dry ingredients: 250g of oats plus 150g of mixed nuts, roughly chopped. That swap is not weight for weight, and the reason matters. Oats soak up syrup and swell slightly as they bake, which helps the mixture bind. Nuts do neither — a chopped nut stays a chopped nut, coated in syrup but never part of the glue. Replace too many oats with nuts and there is not enough binding mixture to hold the pieces together. At 250g of oats to 150g of nuts the balance still works: there is plenty of oat mixture to fill the gaps between the nut pieces, and the bars press, bake and set like the classic.

What the nuts give you in return is texture and a second flavour. The oat part stays soft and caramel-sweet; the nuts stay firm, and their exposed edges toast gently in the oven, so each bar is soft and crunchy at once. It is the same principle as our seeded flapjack recipe, which folds seeds through the same base — solid pieces scattered through a soft bar — but nuts are bigger than seeds, which is why the chopping, pressing and cutting below get more attention than they do in the classic.

Nut allergy: these bars are not for nut-free lunchboxes

This needs saying plainly, before the recipe goes any further. Nuts are a major allergen, and these bars are full of them — not a trace, but 150g in a batch of twelve. They are not suitable for anyone with a nut allergy, they should never go into a lunchbox or onto a table where nuts are banned or where someone allergic might take one unknowing, and a home kitchen that handles nuts cannot honestly offer them as safe for an allergic person. Peanuts count too: peanuts and tree nuts are listed separately on UK labels, and most bags of mixed nuts contain both. If you are baking for a mixed group or for school, use our nut-free flapjack recipe instead — it is written for exactly that situation.

Choosing the nuts: a mix of firm and soft works best

A bag of mixed nuts is the easy route and a good one — most contain some combination of walnuts, hazelnuts, almonds, cashews, pecans and peanuts, which is already a sensible mix. Building your own mix works too, and the principle is simple: combine firm nuts that keep their bite (almonds, hazelnuts) with softer, sweeter ones (walnuts, cashews, pecans), rather than using all of one sort. All-almond bars are hard work to bite; all-cashew bars are rich but soft, with no real crunch. A blend gives you both.

NutFlavour and textureHow it behaves in a flapjack
WalnutSoft, rich, slightly bitter skinChops easily and stays tender in the bake. The bitterness balances the syrup; a reliable backbone for a mix
HazelnutSweet and firmKeeps a real crunch. Skinned hazelnuts look and taste cleaner; cut each nut into two or three pieces
AlmondFirm and mildThe hardest nut in the mix — chop it well or the bars are tough to bite. Blanched or skin-on both work
CashewSoft and butteryBreaks up as you chop it, and the pieces stay tender. Lovely flavour, but use as part of a mix, not the whole 150g
PecanSweet and softClose to walnut but sweeter. Often the most expensive in the bag, so many mixes lean on walnut instead
PeanutStrong, savoury-sweetTechnically a legume, not a tree nut. Its flavour dominates, so keep it to part of the mix unless you want a peanut bar

Buy plain, raw nuts for this recipe where you can — unsalted and unroasted gives you a clean starting point, and the oven in the method does the toasting. Salted snack nuts and dry-roasted nuts both work in a pinch; there is a question about them at the end of the page. Avoid honey-roasted or flavoured coatings: the extra sugar catches and darkens in the oven, and a chilli coating in a flapjack is a surprise nobody asked for.

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper, a large pan, and a big sharp knife or a food processor for the nuts — a knife is slower but gives you control over the size, and size is the thing that decides whether these bars cut cleanly or break apart. A spare baking tray is needed only if you choose the optional toasting step.

Rough chopping: pieces big enough to notice, small enough to cut through

The chop decides how these bars behave. Aim for rough pieces — each nut in two to four pieces, most of them somewhere between a pea and a whole hazelnut. Pieces left whole or halved become fault lines: the knife meets a whole almond, the bar splits around it, and the break runs through the slab. Dust and tiny fragments are not a problem — they fill gaps between the oats and actually help the binding — but a mixture chopped to powder is just expensive oat flour, and you lose the crunch that is the point of the recipe.

Pressing matters more here than in any other flapjack on this site. Nut pieces do not squash down and knit together the way oats do, so the mixture goes into the tin looser and with more air in it. Press it down hard — the back of a spoon, worked into every corner, or the flat base of a glass — until the surface looks level and compact. Then, at the other end, be patient: a nut flapjack cut while warm will break at the nuts, because the syrup around each piece is still soft. Cut it fully cold and the same slab slices into clean bars.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides for lifting the slab out later.
  2. Chop the nuts roughly. Cut 150g of mixed nuts into rough pieces — each nut in two to four pieces, mostly pea-to-hazelnut sized. Tip the whole lot, dust and all, into a bowl; the small bits help fill the gaps.
  3. Toast the nuts if you want to (optional). Spread the chopped nuts on a baking tray and put them in the oven as it heats for about 5 minutes, until they smell nutty and the cut edges are a shade darker. Tip them onto a cold plate straight away so they stop cooking, and see the toasting section below before you decide.
  4. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado in a large pan over a low heat. Stir until everything has melted into one smooth liquid. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil — boiled syrup sets like toffee and makes hard bars.
  5. Add the oats, nuts and salt. Tip in 250g porridge oats, the chopped nuts and a pinch of salt. Stir until everything is coated and shiny, and check the nuts are spread through the mixture rather than clumped in one corner of the pan.
  6. Press into the tin — harder than usual. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down very firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon or the base of a glass, working well into the corners. Nut pieces leave gaps that only firm pressing closes, and firm pressing is what stops these bars crumbling.
  7. Bake for 30–35 minutes. The top should be golden brown, the nut pieces on the surface a shade darker and smelling toasted, and the centre set but still slightly soft when pressed — it firms up as it cools.
  8. Score while warm. About 5 minutes after the tin comes out, mark the portion lines with a table knife — 12 bars — while the top is soft enough not to crack. Try to run the lines between nut pieces rather than through the biggest ones.
  9. Cool completely in the tin. Leave the slab until it is fully cold and set — an hour at least, and longer does no harm. This is the step nut flapjacks punish you for rushing: a warm slab breaks at the nuts when you cut it.
  10. Lift out and cut. Use the paper overhang to lift the cold slab onto a board and cut through along the scored lines with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down in one firm motion rather than sawing. Any small pieces that do break off are the cook's share.

Toasting the nuts first: optional, and what it changes

Raw nuts bake perfectly well inside a flapjack, and if you skip the toasting step the bars are still good. What toasting adds is depth: heat drives moisture out of the nut, concentrates its oils and browns its cut surfaces, so a toasted walnut tastes more walnut-like and a toasted hazelnut more hazelnut-like than either does raw. The difference is real but not enormous, because the nuts get half an hour in the oven inside the bars anyway — pre-toasting simply starts that process and takes it further than the insulated middle of a flapjack ever reaches.

Two cautions if you do toast. Chopped nuts toast fast and scorch faster: the small pieces and the dust brown well before the big pieces, which is why the time in the method is short — go by smell and colour, not the clock. And tip the nuts off the hot tray the moment they are done; left sitting on it, they carry on cooking in the tray's heat and the smallest bits burn while you are melting the butter.

Why nut flapjacks break at the nuts

If a bar snaps, it almost always snaps through a nut. The reason is structural: the set syrup binds oat to oat in a continuous sheet, but around a nut piece the binding is only a coating, and a big piece is a hole in the sheet with hard edges. Three things fix it, all already in the method: chop the pieces smaller so the holes are smaller, press the mixture harder so there are fewer gaps around each piece, and cut the slab fully cold so the coating is set hard before the knife loads it. If your bars still crumble, our troubleshooting guide to why flapjacks fall apart works through the other causes — underbaking, too little syrup for the dry ingredients, and cutting warm among them.

Storing mixed nut flapjacks

Keep the cut bars in an airtight tin at room temperature with baking paper between the layers. They keep about 5 days — the same as the classic, though nuts bring their own oils to the bar, and nut oils are the first flavour to stale, so these are at their best in the first three or four days. They freeze well for up to 3 months, wrapped individually and sealed in a bag or box; defrost at room temperature in the wrapping. There is more detail in our guide to how to store flapjacks and how long they last.

The one to avoid as a solo act is cashew: an all-cashew batch is rich and tasty but soft, and you lose the crunch that makes a nut flapjack worth making.
