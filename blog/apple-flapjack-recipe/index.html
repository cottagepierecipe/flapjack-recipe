Apple Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

Grated Bramley apple and a teaspoon of cinnamon turn the classic flapjack into something that smells like apple crumble and eats like a proper oat bar. The one thing this recipe has to solve is moisture — fresh apple is mostly juice — and the fix is simple: squeeze the grated apple dry before it goes anywhere near the oats.

The short answer: Melt 150g butter with 150g golden syrup and 150g light brown sugar, stir in 325g porridge oats, a squeezed-dry grated Bramley apple, a teaspoon of cinnamon and a pinch of salt, press into a lined 20cm tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30 to 35 minutes. Cooling in the tin is what sets it — cut too soon and it will crumble.

Not yet kitchen-tested: this recipe adapts our tested classic flapjack and has not been cooked by us yet. Michael tests every new recipe himself, and this page will be updated once he has cooked it. Times marked (estimate) are estimates.

Why Bramley apple: the cooking apple that flapjacks need

Use a Bramley. A cooking apple does two things an eating apple cannot: it softens completely into the mixture during the bake, so you get apple flavour through every bite instead of chewy raw shreds, and its sharpness stands up to the syrup and brown sugar instead of disappearing into sweetness. One large Bramley — about 200g before peeling — is the right amount for this tray.

An eating apple such as a Gala or Braeburn will work at a push, but grate it and expect a sweeter, less distinct result, and be even more careful with the squeezing step below, because eating apples give up their juice just as readily. What you should not use is tinned apple or apple sauce: both arrive already wet, and extra liquid is exactly what this recipe is designed to keep out.

Ingredients

IngredientAmount
Butter150g
Golden syrup150g
Light brown sugar150g
Porridge oats325g
Bramley apple1 large (about 200g), peeled, coarsely grated and squeezed dry
Ground cinnamon1 tsp
SaltA pinch

Two numbers here differ from our tested classic flapjack, and both differences are the apple's fault. The syrup drops from 175g to 150g, and a little of the oat weight goes too, because grated apple — even squeezed — brings some moisture of its own. Add the full classic quantities on top of a wet apple and the syrup balance tips: the bars come out loose, soft in the middle and reluctant to set.

The method, step by step

  1. Line the tin and heat the oven. Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides so the slab lifts out easily. Heat the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3).
  2. Grate and squeeze the apple. Peel the Bramley and grate it coarsely. Pile the gratings into a clean tea towel, gather the towel up and twist hard over the sink until the shreds feel dry and crumbly rather than wet. This step decides whether the bars set — do not rush it.
  3. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put the butter, golden syrup and light brown sugar in a large saucepan over a low heat on the hob. Stir until everything has melted together and the sugar has dissolved. Let it bubble gently for a moment if you like, but do not boil it hard.
  4. Take the pan off the heat and stir in the dry ingredients. Add the porridge oats, cinnamon and salt, then the squeezed apple shreds. Stir well until every oat is coated and the apple is spread evenly through the mixture — clumps of apple in one corner mean a soggy patch in the finished tray.
  5. Press the mixture into the tin. Tip the mixture into the lined tin and press it down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working it into the corners. A level, well-packed surface bakes evenly and cuts cleanly.
  6. Bake for 30 to 35 minutes (estimate). Bake until the top is golden and the edges are just starting to colour more deeply. The centre will still look a little soft — it firms as it cools, exactly as the classic does.
  7. Cool in the tin, then cut. Leave the tray to cool in the tin for at least 20 to 30 minutes; score the cuts while it is still warm if you like, then lift the slab out on the paper and cut into 12 bars once properly cool.

Why the apple is squeezed dry: juice is the enemy of the set

This is the single most important paragraph in the recipe. A flapjack sets because a thick syrup of butter, sugar and golden syrup cools and firms around the oats. Apple juice thins that syrup. Grated fresh apple can give up several spoonfuls of juice as it heats, and every spoonful loosens the set of the finished bars — they stay soft in the middle, crumble at the cut and never quite firm up, however long you bake them.

Squeezing the gratings in a tea towel removes most of that free juice before it reaches the pan, leaving the apple's flavour and soft flesh behind. The slightly reduced syrup in the ingredients does the rest of the balancing. If your previous fruit flapjacks have turned out soggy, this — not the oven temperature — is almost always the reason, and the same squeeze-first approach improves our fruit flapjack when you use fresh fruit in it.

Keeping apple flapjacks: 3 to 4 days, and the fridge helps

Fresh fruit shortens a flapjack's life. The classic keeps about a week in an airtight tin; these apple bars keep 3 to 4 days (estimate) at their best, and the fridge stretches that a little if your kitchen is warm. Store them in a single layer or with baking paper between layers, and let a fridge-cold bar sit at room temperature for ten minutes before eating so the butter softens. For the full storage picture across every version, see how to store flapjacks and how long they last.

Variations that work with apple: walnuts, sultanas and a crumble top

The base recipe is deliberately plain, because apple and cinnamon carry it — but it takes additions well, as long as they are dry ones. Fifty grams of chopped walnuts stirred in with the oats is the classic partner and adds a slight crunch against the soft bars. A handful of sultanas works too, though it pushes the sweetness up, so hold back a tablespoon of the sugar if you add them. A pinch of ground cloves alongside the cinnamon gives a more wintry, mincemeat-adjacent warmth.

For a crumble effect, reserve two tablespoons of the oat mixture before pressing — made with a teaspoon of the melted butter mixture rubbed back in — and scatter it loosely over the top instead of pressing the surface flat. The top bakes rougher and crisper while the bars underneath stay compact. What not to add is more fresh fruit: a second grated apple overloads the moisture balance this recipe is built around, and chopped raw apple pieces stay firm and watery in the finished bar.

Troubleshooting apple flapjacks: the three things that go wrong

The bars crumble at the cut. They were cut too soon, most likely — the syrup finishes setting as the tray cools, and apple bars need the full cooling time more than the classic does. Next time, score while warm and finish cutting when the slab is completely cold. If a cooled tray still crumbles, the apple was squeezed a little too dry or the bake ran long; a minute less next time, or a slightly gentler squeeze.

The edges darken too fast. Apple sugars caramelise keenly, so the corners of an apple tray colour faster than a plain one. If your oven runs hot, drop it by 10 degrees and keep the time; a piece of foil laid loosely over the top for the last ten minutes also protects a tray that is colouring ahead of schedule.

The apple tastes raw in the finished bar. The shreds were too thick, or an eating apple was used — Bramley breaks down completely in the bake, eating apples do not. Grate on the coarse side of the grater but not in long ribbons, and spread the shreds through the mixture rather than leaving them in clumps, so the heat reaches all of them evenly.

Serving apple flapjacks: warm with yoghurt, cold from the tin

Cold, these are a straightforward lunchbox or walking bar — the cinnamon makes them taste more considered than the plain classic, and the apple keeps them softer. Warm, they change character: ten minutes in a low oven or a few seconds in the microwave softens the syrup, the cinnamon comes forward, and a bar served with a spoonful of natural yoghurt or a little custard is recognisably a pudding built from the same tray. If you are serving them warm, cut the bars a fraction larger, because warm flapjack is richer than it looks.
