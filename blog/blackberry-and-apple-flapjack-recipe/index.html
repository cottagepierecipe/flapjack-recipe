Blackberry and Apple Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

A blackberry and apple flapjack is the classic oat bar with two lots of fresh fruit folded through it: 150g of blackberries and one diced eating apple. The fruit brings sharpness and juice against the butter, syrup and sugar, and the blackberry juice stains the crumb a deep purple as it bakes. The method is the classic one with two changes — the blackberries go in last and gently, so they stay whole, and the bake runs a few minutes longer to deal with the fruit's moisture.

The short answer: Melt 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado sugar in a pan, stir in 325g porridge oats and a pinch of salt, fold in one diced eating apple (about 150g) and then 150g blackberries, press into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 35–40 minutes. Cool completely in the tin and cut into 12 bars.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Blackberry and apple: the crumble pairing, in a bar

Blackberry and apple is the pairing British puddings are built on. It fills pies and crumbles every autumn because the two fruits correct each other: blackberries are dark, jammy and a little wild-tasting, apples are sharp and clean, and together they taste like more than either one does alone. A flapjack is a sensible place to put the pairing. The base — our classic flapjack recipe, butter, golden syrup, light muscovado and oats — is sweet and rich, and sweet, rich bases are exactly what sharp fruit is good at cutting through. Each bite of these bars runs sweet first, then fruity, then sharp at the end.

The arrangement is borrowed from the crumble in the photograph above: fruit under oats. Here the oats are bound with butter and syrup instead of rubbed in dry, and the whole thing is pressed flat and cut into bars rather than spooned out of a dish, but the logic is identical — the fruit softens and gives up its juice while the oats on and around it turn golden. The fruit flapjack page in our fruit flapjack recipe collection works on the same principle with dried fruit; this version is the fresh-fruit one, and fresh fruit asks for a little more care, which the next two sections cover.

The apple: firm and sharp, diced small, skin left on

The apple has one job: to soften in the bake without disappearing. That rules the choice. A Bramley, the classic cooking apple in the photograph below, is bred to collapse — put it in a pie and it cooks down to a sharp purée, which is what you want there. In a flapjack it does the same thing, melting into the crumb and leaving sharp, apple-flavoured patches rather than pieces of apple. That is not a failure, and if a Bramley is what you have, use it and accept the softer result. But if you are buying an apple for this recipe, buy a firm eating apple with some sharpness to it — a Braeburn, a Cox or a Granny Smith — and it will hold its dice through the bake, so the finished bar has actual pieces of apple in it.

Whatever the variety, the dice matters more than the name. Core the apple and cut it into pieces of about 1cm, skin on. Much bigger and the pieces are still firm when the oats are done; much smaller and they vanish. The skin stays on for two reasons: it helps each piece hold together in the heat, and peeling one apple is fiddly work that buys you nothing — the skin softens completely in 35 minutes in the oven and nobody will notice it in the finished bar. Dice the apple just before it goes into the pan, so it does not sit around browning, and fold it through the oat mixture while the mixture is still warm, which starts the apple softening before the tin is even filled.

The blackberries: fold them in last, and fold gently

Blackberries are the fragile half of this recipe. A ripe blackberry is mostly juice held together by a thin skin, and the warm oat mixture is heavy. Stir the berries through the way you stir the oats and they burst — the juice bleeds into the mixture in the pan, the bars bake up grey-purple and steamy, and the fruit that was supposed to sit in pockets ends up spread thin everywhere. So the order matters: oats first, apple second, blackberries absolutely last, folded in with a spoon in a few slow turns, just enough to distribute them. Some will still burst. A few is fine and unavoidable; the aim is that most arrive in the tin whole.

Pick the berries over before they go in — shop-bought blackberries hide the odd mouldy one in the middle of the punnet, and picked ones come with leaves and the occasional insect. Rinse them if you must, then dry them on kitchen paper; wet berries add water the recipe has not allowed for. This is also the point to be honest about what fresh fruit does to a flapjack generally: it brings moisture, and moisture is the enemy of a firm set. Our guide to why fruit flapjacks turn soggy covers the causes in full. The defences here are the slightly reduced oats, the longer bake and cooling the slab completely before it is cut — all three are built into the method below.

Ingredients

IngredientAmountNotes
Butter175gSalted or unsalted both work
Golden syrup175gThe full classic amount — it is the glue that holds the fruit in
Light muscovado sugar175gLight brown sugar is the nearest swap
Porridge oats325gReduced from the classic 350g to make room for the fruit
Fine sea saltA pinchSharpens the fruit as well as the base
Eating apple1 (about 150g)Firm and sharp — Braeburn, Cox or Granny Smith. Cored, diced about 1cm, skin on
Blackberries150gFresh, picked over and dry. Folded in last, gently

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides so the slab lifts out cleanly, and a large pan — the fruit makes this mixture bulkier than the classic, and a small pan makes the gentle folding at the end hard to do without crushing the berries.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides.
  2. Prepare the fruit. Core the apple and dice it into pieces of about 1cm, skin on. Pick over the blackberries, throw out any that are mouldy or mushy, and leave the good ones dry on a plate. Do not wash them unless they need it, and dry them on kitchen paper if you do.
  3. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado in a large pan over a low heat. Stir until everything has melted into one smooth liquid. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil — boiled syrup sets like toffee and makes hard bars.
  4. Add the oats and salt. Take the pan off the heat and tip in 325g porridge oats and a pinch of salt. Stir until every oat is coated and shiny.
  5. Fold in the apple. Add the diced apple and stir it through the warm mixture until the pieces are spread evenly. The warmth starts the apple softening, which is what you want.
  6. Fold in the blackberries, last and gently. Tip the blackberries over the mixture and fold them in with a spoon in a few slow turns — just enough to distribute them. Some will burst; most should stay whole. Stop as soon as they are spread through.
  7. Press into the tin. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working into the corners. Press steadily rather than hard in one spot, so the berries under the spoon survive.
  8. Bake for 35–40 minutes. Five minutes longer than the classic, because the fruit brings moisture. The top should be golden with darker edges, the surface should look dry rather than wet, and there may be purple juice bubbling at the edges — that is the blackberries, and it is right.
  9. Cool completely and cut into 12. Leave the slab to cool in the tin until it is completely cold — warm fruit flapjack cuts ragged and falls apart. Lift it out by the paper overhang and cut into 12 bars with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down.

When fresh fruit goes wrong: soggy middles and burst berries

Nearly every failure with this recipe is a moisture failure or a handling failure, and both show up in predictable ways. The table below covers the usual ones. The crumble in the photograph is a useful reminder of the target: fruit that has softened and slumped a little under golden oats, not fruit that has boiled itself to jam.

What happenedLikely causeThe fix next time
Soft, soggy middle that will not setThe fruit's moisture had nowhere to goBake the full 35–40 minutes, dry the berries before they go in, and cool the slab completely before cutting
Bars grey-purple all through, with no whole berriesThe blackberries were stirred rather than folded, or added too earlyFold them in last, off the heat, in a few slow turns of the spoon
Apple pieces still firm in the finished barThe dice was too bigCut to about 1cm — bigger pieces do not cook through in the bake
Apple vanished into the crumbA Bramley or another collapsing cooking appleUse a firm eating apple if you want pieces; keep the Bramley if you like it melted in
Bars too sharpUnderripe blackberries or a very tart appleUse ripe, black, glossy berries; the syrup can only do so much against unripe fruit

Storing blackberry and apple flapjacks: best within about 3 days

Fresh fruit shortens the keeping time. These bars are at their best within about 3 days, kept in an airtight tin at room temperature with baking paper between the layers; after that the fruit starts to make the crumb damp and the flavour dulls. If your kitchen is warm, keep the tin in the fridge instead — the bars firm up in the cold and soften again after a few minutes out of it. They can be frozen for up to 3 months, wrapped well, but be honest with yourself about the result: frozen and thawed fruit is juicier than fresh, so a defrosted bar is softer and damper than the day it was baked. This is a recipe to make in the quantity you will eat in a few days. The general rules are in our guide to how to store flapjacks and how long they last, and our apple flapjack recipe keeps longer if you want the apple without the blackberries' short clock.
