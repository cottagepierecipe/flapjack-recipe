How to Bake Flapjacks in a Round Tin

By Michael

Flapjacks are usually square because the tin is square — the mixture itself has no opinion about shape. A round cake tin works perfectly well, and a good many kitchens have one when they have no square tin. The one real difference is area: a 20cm round tin holds less than the 20cm square the classic recipe is written for, and everything in this guide follows from that single fact.

The short answer: A 20cm round tin has about 314cm² of base against the 20cm square's 400cm² — roughly four-fifths the area. Either make about 0.8 of the classic mixture (140g butter, 140g golden syrup, 140g light muscovado sugar, 280g porridge oats) for the same thickness of bar, or press in the full batch for a deeper slab and give it a few extra minutes. Line the base with a circle of baking paper, bake at the same 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3), cool completely, and cut wedges instead of squares.

A round tin changes the area, and the area changes everything else

Flapjack recipes are really written in depth, not in tin sizes. Our classic flapjack recipe spreads 875g of mixture over the 400cm² base of a 20cm square tin, and that depth — a couple of centimetres — is what bakes in 30–35 minutes at 160°C and sets into a bar that is chewy rather than crunchy. Spread the same weight over a smaller area and the layer is deeper: the centre takes longer to heat through and longer to set. Spread it over a larger area and the layer is thin, quick and prone to drying out. Tin shape is just the most common way the area changes without the cook noticing.

A round tin makes the difference bigger than people expect because the comparison sounds equal. Twenty centimetres is twenty centimetres — but a square's corners count. The 20cm square is 20 × 20 = 400cm². The 20cm round is a circle of 10cm radius: π × 10², about 314cm². A fifth of the base has quietly gone missing, and the full batch in a 20cm round tin sits roughly a quarter deeper than it should. That is entirely manageable — but it is a change to manage, which is what the next two sections are for. Our flapjack tin sizes guide covers square and rectangular tins in the same terms.

These are area comparisons, not new recipes — flapjack mixture is forgiving about a few percent either way. What you are avoiding is the two bad outcomes: a small tin so full that the centre is still wet when the top is done, and a large tin spread so thin that the bars bake hard and dry. Anywhere near the standard depth, the ordinary recipe and ordinary timing work.

Option one: scale the mixture down to about 0.8

Scaling keeps the bars exactly the thickness of the classic, which keeps the timing and the texture exactly the classic's too. Four-fifths of each ingredient is the whole calculation: 140g butter, 140g golden syrup, 140g light muscovado sugar and 280g porridge oats, with the same pinch of salt. Those are tidy numbers to weigh, which is part of the pleasure — no calculator needed at the scales. Melt, stir and press exactly as the classic recipe directs, and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for the usual 30–35 minutes, judging by the usual signs: golden top, edges a shade darker, centre set but still slightly soft to a press.

The same arithmetic serves any round tin in the table above: an 18cm round wants about two-thirds of the batch (115g each of butter, syrup and sugar, 230g of oats, near enough), and a 23cm round takes the full batch as it stands. Scales matter more than spoons here — syrup and sugar measured by eye drift far more than 20 percent.

Option two: the full batch, deeper, a few minutes longer

The full batch in a 20cm round tin is not a mistake; it is a decision. You get a deeper, softer-centred slab — closer to a pudding-bar than the classic — and some people prefer it. What it asks for is patience: a few extra minutes in the oven, because heat has further to travel to the centre, and a stricter cooling, because a deep slab is at its most fragile while warm. Check the centre, not the edges: in a deep round bake the edges can look thoroughly done while the middle is still loose, so press the very centre gently and look for set rather than shine. Our guide to telling when flapjacks are done goes through those signs in full. Do not raise the temperature to hurry it — a deeper slab at a higher heat just gives you dark edges around a wet middle.

Lining a round tin: a circle of paper does the job

A round tin needs its base lined, and a circle is easier than it sounds: sit the tin on the baking paper, draw round it, cut just inside the line, and drop the circle in. A smear of butter under it keeps it from skating while you press the mixture in. The sides need no paper if the tin is sound — the slab shrinks very slightly as it cools and releases — but a springform tin makes the whole thing easier still, because the wall lifts away and there is no turning out to argue with. A loose-based tin is the next best thing: push the base up and the slab comes with it. With a fixed tin, run a palette knife around the edge once the slab is cold, then invert it onto a board and peel the circle away. Our guide to how to line a flapjack tin covers the paper choices themselves; everything in it applies to a circle as much as to a square.

Cutting wedges instead of bars

A round slab cuts into wedges, and wedges are cut differently from squares. Cool the slab completely first — a round, deep-ish slab cut warm tears at the centre, where all the cuts meet. Cut on a board, not in the tin: mark the slab into eight by eye, then cut straight across through the centre in halves, halves again into quarters, and quarters into eighths, pressing a large knife straight down rather than sawing. Eight wedges from a 0.8 batch works out at about a classic bar each — the area maths again — while eight wedges of a full-batch deep slab are generous portions, closer to a pudding serving. The point of the wedge, the centre end, is the softest part of the bar; the wide outer end carries the firmer edge. Anyone who has opinions about edges and middles — most flapjack eaters — will have a preference, and a wedge gives everybody both in one piece. Our post on how to cut flapjacks covers knives and clean edges in more detail.

The same trick works for a rectangular roasting tin

Area, not shape, is the whole lesson, and it rescues the opposite problem too. A 20 × 30cm roasting tin — the one many kitchens actually own — has a base of about 600cm², half as much again as the classic's tin. The full batch spread in it bakes thin, quick and dry-edged; a batch and a half (265g each of butter, syrup and sugar, 525g of oats, near enough) restores the classic depth and the classic timing. Once you start thinking in base area, every tin in the cupboard becomes usable: measure it, work out its area against 400cm², and scale the mixture to match. The flapjack does not mind where it bakes. It minds how deep it sits while it does.

Storing a round batch

Wedges store exactly as bars do: an airtight tin at room temperature, about 5 days, with paper between layers if you stack them. Wedges stack less neatly than squares and their points are delicate, so a single layer is kinder if the tin allows. Freezing works the same way too — wrap each wedge, seal, and keep up to 3 months. Nothing about the shape changes the keeping; it only changes the geometry of the tin you keep them in.
