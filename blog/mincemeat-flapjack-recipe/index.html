Mincemeat Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

A mincemeat flapjack is the classic oat bar with a jar of mincemeat folded through it — the one left at the back of the cupboard after the mince pies are made. The mincemeat brings dried fruit, spice and suet richness; the orange zest lifts the whole thing. Nothing else about the method changes, which is why it works so well.

The short answer: Melt 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado sugar in a pan, stir in 300g porridge oats, a pinch of salt, 200g mincemeat and the finely grated zest of 1 orange, press into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes. Cool completely in the tin and cut into 12 bars.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Mincemeat flapjacks: the jar left over after the mince pies

Almost every kitchen that bakes mince pies ends up with the same problem: half a jar of mincemeat, or a whole unopened jar bought in a fit of optimism, sitting in the cupboard with nowhere to go. Mincemeat keeps for months unopened, but once the pies are done it tends to sit there until someone throws it out. These bars are the answer we use. The mincemeat goes through the oat mixture of our classic flapjack recipe, and what comes out is a bar that tastes like the best part of a mince pie — the spiced, fruity filling — held in a chewy, buttery oat base.

It is worth saying what mincemeat actually is, because the name confuses people. Modern mincemeat contains no meat at all. It is a mixture of dried fruit — raisins, currants, sultanas and candied peel — with sugar, suet, spices and usually a little brandy or rum. That makes it almost perfectly designed for a flapjack: the dried fruit behaves exactly like the fruit in any fruit flapjack, the sugar is already in there, and the suet melts into the bake and enriches it. If you enjoy this kind of festive baking, our Christmas flapjacks work the same cupboard-clearing trick with mixed spice and dried fruit.

Why the mincemeat replaces oats, not sugar

The one adjustment in this recipe that needs explaining is the swap: the mincemeat replaces 50g of the oats, and the butter, syrup and sugar all stay at their full classic amounts. That looks wrong at first glance — mincemeat is sweet, so surely the sugar should come down? In practice, no, and for two reasons. First, mincemeat is only partly sugar. Roughly half of a typical jar is dried fruit, and fruit brings bulk and chew as well as sweetness; what it replaces in the mixture is bulk, which is the oats' job. Second, the syrup and sugar in a flapjack are not just sweeteners — they are the glue. Melted with the butter, they coat every oat and set as the slab cools, and that set is what holds the bar together when you cut it. Reduce the sugar and you reduce the binding, and bars with chunks of fruit through them need more binding than plain ones, not less, because every piece of fruit is a place where the bar can break.

The result is sweet, certainly — this is a festive bar, not an everyday one — but it is not sweeter than a mince pie, and the orange zest and the spices keep it from tasting flat. If your mincemeat is a particularly sweet, syrupy one, you can take the muscovado down to 150g and the bars will still hold; do not take the syrup down, because the syrup does more of the binding work.

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides so the cold slab lifts out cleanly, and a large pan — the mixture is bulky once the mincemeat goes in. A note on the mincemeat itself: most standard jars use beef suet. If you are baking for vegetarians, check the jar before you start and choose one made with vegetable suet; several supermarkets sell one, and the bars work identically. Alcohol-free jars are also available if that matters to you.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven, line the tin and zest the orange. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving overhang at two sides. Finely grate the zest of the orange, taking only the orange skin and none of the bitter white pith, and set it aside.
  2. Stir the mincemeat. Give the jar a good stir before you weigh out 200g. The suet and the syrupy part settle at different ends of the jar, and a well-stirred jar means the fruit and fat spread evenly through the bars instead of landing in one corner.
  3. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado in a large pan over a low heat. Stir until everything has melted into one smooth liquid. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil — boiled syrup sets like toffee and makes hard bars.
  4. Add the oats, salt and zest. Take the pan off the heat and tip in 300g porridge oats, a pinch of salt and the orange zest. Stir until every oat is coated and shiny.
  5. Fold in the mincemeat. Add the 200g mincemeat and fold it through with the spoon until the fruit is spread through the mixture rather than sitting in lumps. The mixture will look darker and wetter than a plain flapjack mixture — that is right.
  6. Press into the tin. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working well into the corners. Firm pressing matters even more here than in a plain bar, because the fruit pieces are weak points where a loosely pressed bar breaks.
  7. Bake for 30–35 minutes. The top should be golden with the edges a shade darker, and any mincemeat showing at the surface should look dark and glossy rather than wet. The centre will feel slightly soft when pressed — it firms up as it cools.
  8. Cool completely and cut into 12. Leave the slab to cool fully in the tin; warm mincemeat is soft and the bars smear if you cut early. Lift the cold slab out using the paper overhang and cut into 12 bars with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down.

Mincemeat varies: how the jar changes the bars

No two jars of mincemeat are quite alike, and the differences show up in the bake. A luxury jar with more suet and a splash of brandy gives a richer, softer bar; a basic jar with more peel gives a sharper, chewier one. The table below covers the variations you are most likely to meet and what, if anything, to do about them.

The jar you haveWhat changesWhat to do
Standard mincemeat, beef suetThe recipe as writtenNothing — bake it exactly as above
Vegetarian mincemeat, vegetable suetSets a fraction softerBake to the full 35 minutes and cool completely before cutting
Luxury mincemeat with brandy or rumRicher, darker, slightly softerNothing extra — the alcohol cooks off in the bake
A very syrupy, loose mincemeatMixture looks wet and may bake softAdd the oats back up to 325g to take up the extra moisture
Homemade mincemeatOften chunkier and less sweetChop any very large pieces of peel or apple, and bake as written
Less than 200g left in the jarFewer fruit pieces through the barsUse what you have and make the oats back up to 350g minus a spoonful per 25g of mincemeat missing

Serving mincemeat flapjacks: warm, with the mince-pie treatment

These bars are good cold from the tin, in the way of all flapjacks, but they are at their best given the mince-pie treatment: ten seconds in the microwave or a few minutes in a low oven until just warm, so the suet softens and the spices smell of themselves again. A warm bar with a spoonful of brandy butter melting over it is a proper pudding, and a much easier one than making pastry. They also sit well on a plate of Christmas baking generally — dark, spiced and sticky, they hold their own next to the richer things. If you are putting together a festive batch, our Christmas flapjacks are the natural partner: same season, different flavour, one tin each.

Storing mincemeat flapjacks: about 5 days in an airtight tin

Keep the cut bars in an airtight tin at room temperature, with baking paper between the layers so the sticky fruit tops do not weld together. They keep about 5 days, and arguably improve over the first two: the spices settle into the oats and the bars turn chewier. The mincemeat's sugar and fat keep them moist longer than a plainer bar, but do not push them past the week — the fruit can start to ferment in a warm kitchen. They freeze well for up to 3 months, wrapped individually and sealed; defrost in the wrapping at room temperature. The general rules are in our guide to how to store flapjacks and how long they last.

Questions people ask

A standard jar is usually around 410g, and doubling the mincemeat unbalances the recipe: too much fruit and syrup for the oats to hold, and the slab comes out soft and falls apart at the cut. If you want to use the jar up, bake two batches, or stir the second 200g through a batch of our Christmas flapjacks mixture in place of some of its dried fruit. Within this recipe, 200g to 300g of oats is the ratio that sets properly.

Most of it does, but "most" is not "all" — a little can remain after 30 minutes in the oven, as with any baking that uses alcohol. If the bars are for children or anyone avoiding alcohol entirely, choose one of the alcohol-free jars, which are widely sold and work identically in this recipe. The flavour difference is small; the spice and fruit do most of the work either way.

Only if the jar is. Despite the name, mincemeat has no meat in it, but most standard jars are made with beef suet, which makes them unsuitable for vegetarians. Jars made with vegetable suet are easy to find and are usually labelled vegetarian on the front. Everything else in the recipe — butter, syrup, sugar, oats — is vegetarian as standard.

The usual cause is a loose, syrupy mincemeat adding more moisture than the reduced oats can absorb — the fix is in the table above: take the oats up to 325g with a wet jar. The other two causes are pressing too loosely, which matters more in a fruit-filled bar than a plain one, and cutting before the slab is completely cold. Warm mincemeat is soft; cold mincemeat sets firm.

Yes — mincemeat is sold all year in most supermarkets, and the bars taste just as good in March as in December. The recipe is at its most useful in January, when it deals with the jar left over from the mince pies, but nothing about it is seasonal except the flavour. Jars keep for months unopened, so a bargain jar bought in the January sales is worth having.

You can, and the bars will still work, but the zest earns its place: mincemeat is dark and rich, and the fresh citrus edge stops the whole bar tasting heavy. If you have no orange, the zest of a lemon is a decent stand-in. What does not work is orange juice — it adds water rather than flavour, loosens the mixture and softens the set.
