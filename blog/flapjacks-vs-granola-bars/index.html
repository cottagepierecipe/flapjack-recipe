Flapjacks vs Granola Bars: What Is the Difference?

By Michael

Supermarket shelves park flapjacks and granola bars side by side, and they look like close relations — both are bars, both are built on oats, both are sold as the wholesome option in the snack aisle. Under the wrapper they are different bakes made in different ways: a flapjack is one slab of oats bound in melted butter, syrup and sugar, baked and then cut; a granola bar is granola — oats, nuts and seeds baked loose into clusters — pressed into a bar shape with a sticky binder. This post separates the three things that get muddled together — flapjack, granola bar and granola itself — by ingredients, method and texture, and gives the honest nutrition picture without pretending either bar is a health food.

The short answer: A flapjack is baked as a single slab — oats in melted butter, golden syrup and sugar, pressed into a tin and cut after baking. A granola bar is made from granola: oats with nuts, seeds and oil, baked loose into clusters first, then pressed into bars with a syrupy binder. Granola on its own is never a bar at all — it is eaten loose, by the spoonful. The flapjack is chewier and denser; the granola bar is crunchier and more broken-up. Both are sweet bakes, and neither earns a health halo just from containing oats.

What a flapjack is

Start with the bar this site exists for. A flapjack begins as a liquid: butter, golden syrup and sugar melted together in a pan. Porridge oats are stirred in until every flake is coated, the mixture is pressed into a lined tin, and the whole thing bakes as one slab — our classic flapjack recipe uses 175g each of butter, syrup and light muscovado sugar to 350g of oats, in a 20cm tin at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30 to 35 minutes. Only when the slab has cooled and set is it cut into bars. That order of operations is the definition: the bar shape comes last, from a knife, not from a mould or a press. The texture follows from it — a continuous, chewy crumb where the oats have softened into the syrup and set as one piece.

What granola is

Granola starts from a similar shelf of ingredients and then goes a completely different way. Oats — usually jumbo oats, because the flakes need to survive — are mixed with chopped nuts, seeds, a little oil and honey or syrup, then spread thin on a tray and baked loose, stirred partway through so the coating dries onto the flakes in clusters. There is no tin, no slab and no pressing. The result is a jar of crunchy, separate clusters, closer to a breakfast cereal than to a cake, and it is eaten that way: by the spoonful, over yoghurt, with milk. Our post on which oats are best for flapjacks covers why the big flakes suit this loose bake — and why the same jumbo oats need help to bind in a flapjack tin.

What a granola bar is

A granola bar is what happens when granola is asked to hold a shape. The cluster mixture — or a close relative of it, with extra syrup, honey or nut butter as the glue — is pressed hard into a tin or mould while warm, sometimes baked again briefly, sometimes simply chilled until the binder sets, then cut or unmoulded. Two things follow. First, the binder is doing structural work against ingredients that do not want to stick: nuts and seeds have no give, and clusters are already dry and separate, so granola bars tend to be nut- and seed-heavy mixtures with just enough oat to fill the gaps. Second, the texture stays granular — the bar in the photograph above shows it. Break a granola bar and it fractures along cluster lines into crunchy pieces; break a flapjack and it bends, then tears, because the crumb is one set piece.

Where muesli fits

Muesli is the unbaked member of the family, and it is worth placing because it shows what baking does to the other two. A muesli is raw rolled oats with nuts, seeds and dried fruit, eaten soaked in milk or juice, or with yoghurt — never baked, never bound, never a bar. Flapjacks and granola both start near muesli's ingredient list and then add the two things muesli refuses: fat or oil in quantity, and heat. That is the honest way to see the whole shelf: the same grains and nuts, separated by how much sweet binding they carry and what the oven did to them.

How granola is usually eaten

The serving habits underline the difference. Granola belongs to breakfast: tipped over yoghurt with fruit, as in the bowl below, or eaten with milk like any cereal, in portions measured by the handful or the small bowl. A flapjack belongs to the cake tin: it is baked in a batch, cut into portions and kept for the week — teas, lunchboxes, walks, the tin on the worktop. A granola bar is the portable compromise between those two lives, granola made pocketable. None of that makes one better than another; it just means they answer different moments, and swapping one for another in a recipe rarely works, because the structures are built differently from the first step.

Texture, portions and the honest nutrition picture

Put the two bars side by side and the contrast is texture first. A flapjack is dense and chewy, cut from a slab into even portions — our classic cuts into 12 bars of about 73g. A granola bar is crunchy and open-textured, its portion fixed by its mould or its wrapper rather than by your knife, and two bars from the same box can eat very differently depending on how the clusters packed. The bowls below show the loose end of that texture range: granola crumble, all edges and crunch, nothing holding it but the bowl.

Now the part both bars' marketing prefers to skip. A flapjack's figures are known, because we have calculated and verified them: our classic bar carries 320 kcal per 73g bar, 439 kcal per 100g — it is a sweet bake and this site says so on its flapjack nutrition page in full. For granola bars there is no single figure to set against it, and this post will not invent one: a granola bar's energy depends entirely on its recipe, and a bar heavy with nuts, seeds and oil is carrying some of the most energy-dense ingredients in the kitchen, bound with honey or syrup. The fair comparison is by ingredients and method, not by halo. Oats do not cancel sugar in either bar. If the numbers matter to you, compare per 100g on the label or the recipe, and treat "made with oats" as a description, not a promise.

And the two are not enemies. If you like the flavour of granola and the manners of a flapjack, they meet in the middle: our granola flapjack recipe bakes a nut-and-seed oat mixture by the flapjack method, slab and all. What you cannot do is take a granola recipe, press it into a tin with no binder change, and expect a flapjack — the mixture has not been given anything to set with. Structure first, flavour second: that is the whole lesson of the comparison.

No — they are made in opposite orders. A flapjack is bound before baking: the oats go into melted butter and syrup, and the bar is cut from a set slab afterwards. A granola bar is bound after (or instead of) a loose bake: the clusters exist first and are then pressed into shape with a binder. The textures give it away — chewy and continuous against crunchy and granular. In America the word "flapjack" means a pancake, which is a third thing entirely.

Because their oats never soften in a liquid. In a flapjack the flakes sit in melted butter and syrup before and during the bake, so they soften and set into one chewy crumb. Granola is baked spread thin and stirred, so the coating dries onto the flakes and they stay crisp; pressing those crisp clusters into a bar keeps the crunch. Jumbo oats help too — granola uses the big flakes precisely because they hold their texture through a loose bake.

Yes, if you give it something to set with. Warm the finished granola with extra syrup, honey or melted nut butter, press it very firmly into a lined tin — far harder than feels reasonable — and chill it until the binder sets before cutting. Without that added binder the bars crumble back into granola at the first bite, which is a respectable outcome too. Or bake the mixture as a slab from the start, as our granola flapjack does.

A homemade flapjack keeps about 5 days in an airtight tin at room temperature and freezes well for up to 3 months wrapped — the full rules are in how to store flapjacks. Homemade granola bars keep similarly in a tin, though the clusters soften as they sit. Loose granola outlasts both in a sealed jar, because there is nothing in it trying to stay chewy. Bought bars of either kind carry their own dates from their preservatives and packaging, which is a different game.

For sturdiness, the flapjack: it is one set piece, travels without shedding, and a wrapped bar survives a bag until lunchtime. A granola bar is lighter to carry but fractures and crumbs more readily, especially a homemade one. For sweetness and energy they are closer than the packaging suggests — both are oat-and-sugar bars — so choose by texture and by which one will actually get eaten rather than traded.
