Date Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

Dates do two jobs in a flapjack: cooked down into a rough paste, they replace a good part of the golden syrup and sugar, and they give the bars a deep toffee flavour and a soft, fudgy chew that plain flapjacks never quite manage. This recipe keeps the classic method — melt, stir, press, bake — and lets the fruit carry the sweetness.

The short answer: Simmer 200g chopped dried dates in 100ml water for about 5 minutes and mash to a rough paste. Melt 125g butter, 100g golden syrup and 75g light brown sugar, stir in the paste, then 350g porridge oats. Press into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for about 30 minutes. Cool completely in the tin, cut into 12 bars, and keep about 5 days in an airtight tin.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Why dates work in flapjacks: less syrup, more toffee

The classic flapjack is sweetened by 175g golden syrup and 175g light brown sugar — a lot of sweetness, and it is also the glue. Dates let you shift that balance. A date paste is sweet in its own right, but it is not just sweetness: it is fruit pulp and fibre, and when it is stirred through the melted butter mixture it behaves like a soft, sticky binder that coats the oats alongside the syrup. That is why this recipe can drop the syrup to 100g and the sugar to 75g without the bars falling apart — the paste takes over part of the binding as well as part of the sweetening.

The flavour change is just as important. Dates taste of caramel and toffee rather than of plain sugar, and a little of that depth goes a long way in an oat bar, which is otherwise a fairly plain canvas. The finished bars are darker than the classic, softer in the middle, and chewy in a way that lasts — date flapjacks are at their best on the second and third day, when the paste has settled into the oats. If you already like the banana-and-date direction of baking, our healthy flapjack recipe works along similar lines with mashed banana alongside dates.

Choosing dates: Medjool, Deglet Noor or the baking block

Any dried date will work, but they do not all work the same way, and the recipe behaves slightly differently depending on which you buy. Soft, plump dates break down into paste with almost no effort; firm, dry dates need a longer simmer and a splash more water. What matters most is that the dates are dried, not fresh — fresh dates have too much water and too little concentrated sweetness for this job.

Whichever you choose, check for stones. Even bags labelled "pitted" occasionally keep one, and a date stone in a flapjack is a nasty surprise for whoever bites it. Squeeze each date lightly as you chop — a stone makes itself known immediately.

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper, a small pan for the dates, and a large pan for the main mixture. A fork or a potato masher is all the paste-making equipment required — this is a rough paste, not a purée, and a few small pieces of date left in it are a pleasure in the finished bar.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, with a little overhang at two sides for lifting the slab out later.
  2. Chop the dates and check for stones. Roughly chop 200g of dates — kitchen scissors make short work of sticky fruit — squeezing each piece lightly as you go to catch any stone the bag missed.
  3. Simmer the dates into a paste. Put the chopped dates in a small pan with 100ml water. Bring to a gentle simmer and cook for about 5 minutes, stirring, until the dates soften and collapse. Mash with a fork or potato masher into a rough, thick paste. It should be soft and spreadable, not runny; if it looks wet, simmer another minute, and if it is stiff and dry, stir in a spoonful of hot water.
  4. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. In a large pan over a low heat, melt 125g butter with 100g golden syrup and 75g light brown sugar, stirring until you have one smooth liquid. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil.
  5. Stir in the date paste. Take the pan off the heat and stir the warm date paste through the melted mixture until it is evenly combined — it will look darker and thicker than the classic mixture, which is exactly right.
  6. Add the oats and salt. Tip in 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt. Stir until every flake is coated and no dry oats remain. The mixture will be stiffer than a classic flapjack mix; that is the paste doing its work.
  7. Press into the tin. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly, working well into the corners. A date mixture rewards firm pressing — it has more moisture than the classic, and a well-compacted slab cuts far more cleanly.
  8. Bake for about 30 minutes. The top should be golden brown — deeper in colour than the classic, because of the fruit sugars — with the edges a shade darker. The centre should look set and feel firm with a slight give.
  9. Cool completely in the tin. Date flapjacks are softer than the classic while warm and must cool fully before cutting; warm, they will slump and smear. Give them at least two hours, or overnight.
  10. Lift out and cut into 12. Use the paper overhang to lift the cold slab onto a board and cut with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down and wiping the blade between cuts — the dates make the cut edges sticky.

Getting the texture right: chewy, not sticky

The one judgement call in this recipe is the paste. Everything else is weighing and timing, but the paste's consistency decides whether the bars are chewy or claggy. Too much water left in the dates and the extra moisture steams the oats instead of baking them, giving a soft, damp bar that will not hold a clean edge. Too little, and the dry paste will not spread through the oats, leaving pockets of intense sweetness and bars that crumble around them.

The target is a paste about as thick as porridge that has stood for a minute: it should hold a soft peak on the spoon and fall off in one dollop, not pour. If you are using very dry Deglet Noor dates or a pressed block, expect to simmer a few minutes longer and add water a spoonful at a time. If your dates are exceptionally soft Medjools, five minutes may leave them wet — give the paste another minute on the heat, stirring, until the surface looks matte rather than glossy. Because the bake time in this recipe is marked, judge doneness by the cues in step 8 rather than the clock alone on your first batch.

Variations: orange, walnut and spice

The date base takes additions graciously. The grated zest of an orange stirred into the melted mixture is the classic partner for dates and changes the whole character of the bar towards something that tastes like a sticky pudding. A teaspoon of ground cinnamon, or half a teaspoon each of cinnamon and ginger, works the same warming way. Chopped walnuts — about 50g, folded in with the oats — add crunch against the soft chew, and a small handful of dark chocolate chips turns the batch into something closer to a sticky toffee bar. If sugar is your main concern rather than flavour, our piece on which sugar is best for flapjacks sets out how the different sweeteners behave in the classic recipe, and it is worth knowing that dates reduce the added syrup and sugar here, but the finished bar is still a sweet bake — fruit sugar is sugar, and the honest gain is flavour and fibre-rich fruit in place of some of the refined sweetening, not a sugar-free bar.

Storing date flapjacks: about 5 days, best on day two

Keep the cut bars in an airtight tin at room temperature with baking paper between the layers. They keep about 5 days — the date paste actually helps here, because the fruit holds moisture and the bars stay chewy rather than drying at the edges the way a plain flapjack can. Many people find them better on the second day than the first, once the paste has fully settled into the oats. They also freeze well: wrap bars individually, seal them in a bag or box, and use within 3 months, defrosting at room temperature in the wrapping. There is more on keeping times and freezing in our guide to how to store flapjacks and how long they last.
