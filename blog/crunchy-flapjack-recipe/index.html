Crunchy Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

Most flapjacks are soft and chewy, and our classic is no exception. This version pushes the same tested base the other way: part of the muscovado is swapped for coarse demerara sugar, the slab is pressed harder, baked longer until it is deep golden, and then left to cool completely and rest before anyone cuts it. The result is a bar with crisp edges and a firm snap — crunchy by flapjack standards, though honestly not biscuit-hard, and all the better for it.

The short answer: Melt 175g butter, 175g golden syrup, 100g light muscovado sugar and 75g demerara sugar. Stir in 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt. Press very firmly into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 35–40 minutes (estimate) until deep golden. Cool completely in the tin, rest the slab before cutting, and cut into 12 bars.

Not yet kitchen-tested: this recipe adapts our tested classic flapjack and has not been cooked by us yet. Michael tests every new recipe himself, and this page will be updated once he has cooked it. Times marked (estimate) are estimates.

What crunchy means here: crisp edges and a firm snap, not biscuit-hard

It is worth being straight about the word crunchy before anything goes into the oven. A flapjack is oats bound in butter and syrup, and no version of it will ever crunch like a biscuit, because there is no flour dough to dry out into a snap. What this recipe gives you is the crunchy end of what a flapjack can be: edges that are genuinely crisp, a surface that resists the teeth, and a bar that breaks with a short, firm snap rather than bending. The middle of each bar stays dense and satisfying rather than soft and bending. If you want a bar that shatters, you want a biscuit recipe, not this one — but if the classic flapjack has always felt a shade too soft to you, this is the version that fixes that.

The base is our classic flapjack recipe, the one we have kitchen-tested: equal weights of butter, golden syrup and sugar to twice that weight of porridge oats, in a 20cm tin. The structure does not change — the same syrup binds the oats and sets as it cools. What changes is everything around that structure: the kind of sugar, how hard the mixture is pressed, how long it bakes, and how long it rests before cutting. Crunch in a flapjack is not one trick; it is four small ones added together.

Why 75g of the sugar becomes demerara

Sugar does more in a flapjack than sweeten it. Light muscovado, the classic's sugar, is fine-grained and slightly moist, and it dissolves completely into the melted butter and syrup, which is part of why the classic sets soft and chewy. Demerara is a different animal: big, coarse, golden crystals that dissolve slowly and incompletely. Swap part of the muscovado for demerara and two things happen. First, some crystals survive the bake as tiny crisp pockets scattered through the bar, so the crunch is in the eating, not just at the edges. Second, the mixture carries a little less dissolved sugar syrup, so the set is firmer overall.

The swap is partial on purpose: 75g demerara to 100g muscovado. Go all-demerara and the mixture loses too much of the muscovado's moisture and caramel flavour, and the bars turn fragile rather than firm. The muscovado that stays behind keeps the bake bound and gives the deep toffee colour; the demerara that joins it brings the crystals. There is a fuller rundown of what each sugar does in our guide to the best sugar for flapjacks, but the short version is that grain size is a texture ingredient here, not just a flavour one.

The four levers of crunch, and what each one does

Every difference between this bar and the classic comes down to four levers. None of them is dramatic on its own; together they move a flapjack from bending to snapping.

LeverWhat it doesIn this recipe
Sugar grainCoarse crystals dissolve less completely, leaving crisp pockets and a firmer set75g demerara replaces part of the muscovado; 100g muscovado stays for binding and colour
Bake lengthA longer bake drives off more moisture and deepens the caramelisation at the edges35–40 minutes (estimate) instead of the classic's 30–35, until deep golden
PressingA tightly packed slab has fewer air gaps, so it sets dense and breaks with a snap instead of crumblingPressed very firmly, harder than you would for a chewy bar
Cooling and restingThe syrup finishes setting as the slab cools, and firms further as it rests; cutting early undoes all of itCooled completely in the tin, then rested before cutting

Our comparison of chewy vs crunchy flapjacks goes into the science of these levers in more depth — why syrup temperature, oat size and moisture all push a batch one way or the other. This recipe simply pulls all four levers in the crunchy direction at once.

Ingredients

IngredientAmountNotes
Butter175gThe classic base, unchanged. Salted or unsalted both work
Golden syrup175gThe binder as well as a sweetener — weigh it into the pan with the butter
Light muscovado sugar100gPart of the classic's sugar, kept for moisture, binding and colour
Demerara sugar75gThe crunch swap. Coarse crystals; golden granulated is the nearest substitute
Porridge oats350gStandard porridge oats, not jumbo — they pack tighter and set firmer
Fine sea saltA pinchKeeps the sweetness in check

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper and a large pan, plus something flat and heavy for pressing — the base of a glass or a sturdy jam jar works well. The total sugar is the same 175g as the classic; only the blend has changed, so the sweetness of the finished bars is much the same and the difference is all in the texture.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides for lifting the slab out later.
  2. Melt the butter, syrup and both sugars. Put 175g butter, 175g golden syrup, 100g light muscovado and 75g demerara in a large pan over a low heat. Stir until the butter and muscovado have melted into one smooth liquid. The demerara will not fully disappear — visible crystals in the liquid are expected and wanted. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil: boiled syrup sets like toffee, and that is the road to hard bars rather than crunchy ones.
  3. Add the oats and salt. Tip in 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt. Stir until every oat is coated and shiny and there are no dry patches hiding at the bottom of the pan. Work fairly quickly — the mixture stiffens as it cools, and a stiff mixture is harder to press evenly.
  4. Press into the tin, firmly. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down hard and evenly, working well into the corners. Start with the back of a spoon, then go over the whole surface with the flat base of a glass or jar, leaning your weight into it. This is the step where crunchy is made: a loosely pressed slab sets crumbly, a tightly pressed one sets dense.
  5. Bake for 35–40 minutes (estimate). This is longer than the classic's tested 30–35 minutes, and the extra time is an estimate based on the deeper colour you are after. The top should be deep golden all over with the edges a shade darker again, and the surface should feel firm and dry when pressed lightly. The very centre firms up further as it cools, so do not wait for it to feel rock hard in the oven — that way lies a burnt bottom.
  6. Score while warm. About 5 minutes after the tin comes out, mark the portion lines with a table knife — 12 bars — while the top is soft enough not to crack. Scoring a firm slab like this one matters more than it does for a soft batch; cutting from scratch when cold is where edges shatter.
  7. Cool completely in the tin. Leave the slab until it is fully cold and set before cutting. Warm flapjack of any kind cuts raggedly, and a batch aiming for crunch that is cut warm will crumble at the edges and lose the snap you baked it for. An hour at least, and longer does no harm.
  8. Rest the slab before cutting. Once cold, leave the uncut slab to rest — at least a couple of hours, and overnight in the tin under a clean tea towel is fine. The set continues to firm as the slab rests, and bars cut after a rest hold a cleaner edge than bars cut the moment they reach room temperature.
  9. Lift out and cut. Use the paper overhang to lift the rested slab onto a board and cut through along the scored lines with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down in one firm motion rather than sawing. The bars should feel firm in the hand and break with a short snap.

Crunchy is not the same as hard

There is a line between a crunchy flapjack and a hard one, and it is mostly drawn in the oven. A crunchy bar is firm, crisp-edged and dense, but your teeth go through it without a fight. A hard bar — the kind that threatens the teeth and bends not at all — has usually been baked past golden into dark brown, or made with syrup that boiled in the pan, or both. If a batch comes out that way, our guide to why flapjacks turn out hard works through the causes one by one. The honest summary is that hard is an accident and crunchy is a decision: the same levers, pulled a little too far. Deep golden is the colour to stop at; the moment the edges look mahogany rather than gold, the next batch wants five minutes less.

Storing crunchy flapjacks

Cut bars keep about 5 days in an airtight tin at room temperature, with baking paper between the layers. One honest warning: crunch is the first thing storage takes away. The bars slowly draw in moisture from the air each time the tin is opened, and by day four or five they will be firmer than the classic but noticeably less crisp than on day one. Sealing the tin well and opening it less often slows that down. They also freeze well — wrap the bars individually, seal them in a bag or box and use within 3 months, defrosting at room temperature in the wrapping; expect a slight softening of the crunch after freezing, though the firm set survives. There is more in our guides to how to store flapjacks and whether you can freeze flapjacks.

Questions people ask

Demerara on its own brings less moisture and less caramel flavour than muscovado, and because more of it stays as crystals, less of it dissolves to help bind the oats — the result tends to be fragile and crumbly rather than firm and snappy, and paler in flavour. The 100g of muscovado in this recipe is doing structural work, not just adding sweetness. If you do try an all-demerara batch, press it even harder and rest it overnight before cutting to give it the best chance of holding together.

Almost always moisture. If the bars went into the tin while still slightly warm, the trapped steam softens the surfaces from inside the tin. If the tin is not truly airtight, the oats steadily draw in moisture from the kitchen air — a flapjack is hygroscopic, meaning it pulls water towards itself, and crisp edges are the first casualty. Cut bars stored somewhere warm, like beside the oven, soften faster still. The fix for the next batch is full cooling before the tin, and a tin that seals properly.

Often, yes. A short return to the oven — around 5 minutes (estimate) at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3), on a lined tray — drives surface moisture back off and brings a good part of the crispness back. Let them cool completely before eating, because they crisp as they cool, not while hot. This works on bars that have softened in storage; it cannot rescue bars that were soft because they were underbaked in the middle, and it will push a batch that was already near the hard line over it, so use it on day-three bars, not day-one disappointments.

No, though they can look similar from across the room. An overbaked chewy flapjack is dried out right through — hard, dark and often a little burnt at the base — because the bake was the only thing changed and it went too far. A crunchy flapjack made this way is built differently from the start: coarse sugar crystals, a tighter press and a full rest, with the longer bake as only one of four changes. The centre of a crunchy bar is dense and rich rather than dry, and that difference is what you taste.

They make them chunkier, which is not quite the same thing. Jumbo oats are whole rolled flakes, so they leave larger gaps in the slab that the syrup cannot fully fill, and the set is weaker and more crumbly — bars break into rubble rather than snapping cleanly. If you like the chunky texture, swapping about a quarter of the porridge oats for jumbo is a reasonable middle path, pressed extra firmly; but for a clean snap, standard porridge oats packed tight are the better tool.

Three things together: score the portion lines while the slab is still warm, so the cuts are half-made before the set hardens; wait until the slab is fully cold and rested before cutting, because a warm centre drags and tears; and use a large, heavy, sharp knife pressed straight down in one motion rather than a sawing action, which worries the edges apart. If edges still shatter, the slab was probably baked a few minutes too long — a slightly gentler bake next time cuts noticeably cleaner.
