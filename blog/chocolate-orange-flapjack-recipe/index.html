Chocolate Orange Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

A chocolate orange flapjack is the classic oat bar with two additions: the finely grated zest of two oranges stirred through the mixture, and a layer of dark chocolate spread over the top once the bake has cooled. The zest does the flavouring. The chocolate does the finishing. Neither one changes the base method at all, which is exactly why the combination works.

The short answer: Melt 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado sugar in a pan, stir in 350g porridge oats, a pinch of salt and the finely grated zest of 2 large oranges, press into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes. Cool completely in the tin, spread 150g melted dark chocolate over the top, leave it to set, and cut into 12 bars.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Chocolate orange flapjacks: zest in the mixture, chocolate on top

Chocolate and orange is one of those pairings that turns up everywhere in British baking — in cakes, in biscuits, in the chocolate oranges that appear in Christmas stockings — because the two flavours genuinely suit each other. Dark chocolate is bitter and rich; orange is bright and sharp. Each one stops the other becoming too much. In a flapjack the pairing has a particular advantage: the base itself is sweet, being mostly butter, syrup and sugar, so a flavour that cuts against sweetness earns its place rather than just decorating the edges.

The base here is our classic flapjack recipe — the version we have kitchen-tested — in its full, unadjusted form: 175g each of butter, golden syrup and light muscovado sugar to 350g of porridge oats. On top of that base, the orange goes in as zest, stirred through the oats so every bite carries it, and the chocolate goes on as a proper layer, melted and spread over the cooled bake and left to set into a snap. It is the same arrangement as the topping route on our chocolate flapjack recipe page, with the orange doing the extra work in the base underneath. Nothing about the tin, the temperature or the pressing changes.

The zest is the flavouring, so zest the oranges properly

Everything orange about these bars comes from about two tablespoons of finely grated zest, which is why the zesting deserves more care than it usually gets. The flavour lives in the thin orange skin, in the oils that spray out when you grate it. The white pith underneath holds none of that flavour and a great deal of bitterness, so the aim is to take the skin and only the skin. Use a fine grater or a zester, turn the orange as you go, and stop on each patch as soon as you see white showing through. Pale streaks in your pile of zest mean pith, and pith means a bitter edge in the finished bars that no amount of chocolate covers up.

Zest the oranges before you start melting anything, while your hands are dry and the fruit is at room temperature — warm oranges give up their zest more easily than fridge-cold ones, and the oils are more fragrant. Stir the zest through the oats in the pan, off the heat, and give it a few extra turns of the spoon: zest clumps, and a clump means one bar with all the flavour and eleven without. One more thing not to do: do not be tempted to add orange juice for extra flavour. Juice is mostly water with a little sharpness. It loosens the mixture, softens the set and tastes of very little once baked. The zest is the flavour; the juice can go in a glass.

Extra orange zest (optional)From ½ an orangeScattered over the chocolate before it sets

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper, a large pan for the oat mixture, and a fine grater or zester — the finer the grate, the more evenly the zest spreads through the mixture. For melting the chocolate, a heatproof bowl that sits over a pan of barely simmering water.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven, line the tin and zest the oranges. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides. Finely grate the zest of the 2 oranges, taking only the orange skin and none of the white pith, and set it aside.
  2. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado in a large pan over a low heat. Stir until everything has melted into one smooth liquid. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil — boiled syrup sets like toffee and makes hard bars.
  3. Add the oats, salt and zest. Take the pan off the heat and tip in 350g porridge oats, a pinch of salt and all the orange zest. Stir until every oat is coated and shiny and the zest is spread through the mixture rather than sitting in clumps.
  4. Press into the tin. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working well into the corners. Firm pressing is what holds the bars together once cut.
  5. Bake for 30–35 minutes. The top should be golden brown with the edges a shade darker, and the centre should look set but still feel slightly soft when pressed — it firms up as it cools. Leave the slab to cool completely in the tin; the chocolate goes onto a cold bake.
  6. Melt the chocolate. Break 150g dark chocolate into a heatproof bowl set over a pan of barely simmering water, making sure the bowl does not touch the water. Stir now and then until it is smooth and fully melted, then take the bowl off the pan. A microwave works too, in short bursts, stirred between each one.
  7. Spread the chocolate over the cold bake. Pour the melted chocolate over the cooled flapjack, still in its tin, and spread it to the corners with the back of a spoon or a palette knife in an even layer. If you are using the extra zest, scatter it over now, while the chocolate is wet.
  8. Leave the chocolate to set. Leave the tin at room temperature until the chocolate is firm and matte rather than glossy — about an hour. Setting it in the fridge is faster, but chocolate set too cold is more likely to crack when cut.
  9. Lift out and cut into 12. Use the paper overhang to lift the cold slab onto a board. Cut with a large sharp knife at room temperature, pressing straight down. If the chocolate threatens to crack, warm the blade under the hot tap and dry it between cuts.

Chocolate orange problems: cracked tops, bitter notes, soft bars

Most of what goes wrong with this recipe goes wrong at the two ends — the zesting at the start or the chocolate at the finish — rather than in the bake itself. The table below covers the usual failures. One theme runs through it: temperature. Chocolate spread onto a warm bake, or cut while fridge-cold, causes more trouble than everything else put together.

Bars too soft and crumblyCut while still warm, or pressed too looselyPress firmly into the corners and cut only when the slab and the topping are fully cold and set
Orange flavour barely thereToo little zest, or juice used insteadUse the zest of both oranges, finely grated; keep juice out of the mixture entirely

The chocolate top: why it goes on cold, and stays fairly thin

Spreading the chocolate over a completely cold bake is the one fussy step in this recipe, and it is worth being fussy about. Warm oat mixture gives off steam and heat as it cools, and chocolate spread onto it absorbs both: it can seize at the edges, sweat as it sets, or bond so loosely that the whole layer lifts away from the bar in the tin. On a cold base it does none of that. It sits where it is spread, sets with a clean snap and slices as one piece with the bar beneath. Our guide to why chocolate cracks when you cut flapjacks covers the cutting side of the same problem in more detail.

Keep the layer honest, too. A 150g layer over a 20cm tin is a coating you can bite through cleanly. Double it and the chocolate takes over: the bar becomes a chocolate slab with oats underneath, and the orange — the whole point of the recipe — gets shouted down. If you prefer chocolate all the way through rather than chocolate on top, the chips route on our chocolate flapjack recipe page is the better arrangement; this one is built around the contrast between a bright, zesty base and a plain dark top.

Storing chocolate orange flapjacks: about 5 days in an airtight tin

Keep the cut bars in an airtight tin at room temperature, with baking paper between the layers so the chocolate tops do not mark each other. They keep about 5 days; the base stays chewy and the zest stays bright for most of that time, fading a little towards the end. Keep the tin somewhere cool and dark — chocolate kept in a warm kitchen or in sunlight can develop a pale bloom on the surface, which looks worse than it is and changes nothing about the taste, but a cool cupboard avoids it. The bars also freeze well for up to 3 months, wrapped individually and sealed; defrost in the wrapping at room temperature, which keeps the chocolate from sweating. The general rules are in our guide to how to store flapjacks and how long they last.

Questions people ask

It is not a swap that works. The flavour of an orange lives in the oils in its skin; the juice is mostly water with a mild sharpness, and baked in a flapjack it adds moisture without adding much flavour — the mixture comes out looser and the set softer. If your oranges are small or not very fragrant, the better fix is the zest of a third orange, not a splash of juice. Save the juice for drinking.

Dark chocolate is the better balance here. The base is sweet — syrup, sugar and oats — and dark chocolate's bitterness, plus the sharpness of the zest, keeps each bar tasting of chocolate and orange rather than simply of sugar. Milk chocolate works if it is what you have, but the bars come out noticeably sweeter and the topping sets a little softer, so cut them somewhere cool. White chocolate is sweeter again and most people find it too much against this base.

Almost always because the chocolate was too cold or too thick. Chocolate set in the fridge contracts and hardens, and a knife pressed through it splits the layer instead of slicing it. Set the topping at room temperature, cut the slab at room temperature, and if cracks still threaten, warm the blade under the hot tap and dry it between cuts — the warm knife melts its way through instead of forcing a split. Yes — you get orange flapjacks, which are good in their own right: bright, buttery and less rich. Without the topping, there is no need to wait for anything to set, so the bars can be cut as soon as the slab is cold. If you want a little more orange presence to make up for the missing layer, scatter the extra half-orange of zest over the top of the mixture before it goes in the oven instead.

You can — about a teaspoon of a good orange extract, stirred in with the oats, gives a recognisable orange flavour. What it does not give is the freshness: extract tastes flatter and more one-note than zest, and it is easy to overshoot into something that tastes like sweets. Fresh zest from two oranges is the better flavour for very little effort, and the oranges themselves are hardly wasted — they keep for days afterwards for eating.
