Granola Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

Granola is already halfway to being a flapjack: oats toasted with oil and sweetener until they cluster, often with nuts, seeds and dried fruit already mixed in. Baking it into bars takes very little — the same melted butter and syrup treatment as a classic flapjack, but with the sugar and syrup cut back, because the granola arrives sweetened and oiled before it ever meets the tin. The result is a flapjack with the toasted flavour and chunky texture of your favourite granola, in a bar you can wrap and carry.

The short answer: Break up any big clusters in 350g plain granola. Melt 175g butter with 125g golden syrup and 100g light muscovado sugar — less syrup and sugar than the classic, because granola is sweetened already. Stir the granola and a pinch of salt through the melted mixture, press firmly into a lined 20cm square tin, and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 25–30 minutes. Cool completely in the tin, then cut into 12 bars.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Why granola works in a flapjack: it is halfway there already

Look at what granola actually is. A maker takes porridge oats, coats them in oil and something sweet — honey, syrup or sugar — stirs through nuts, seeds or dried fruit, and bakes the lot until the oats turn golden and clump into clusters. A flapjack, meanwhile, is oats bound with melted butter and syrup and baked in a tin. The two recipes share their bones; granola is essentially a flapjack that was stirred as it baked so it stayed loose, instead of being pressed so it set into a slab.

That is why this recipe is such a short step from our tested classic flapjack. The method is identical — melt, stir, press, bake, cool — and only the quantities of sugar and syrup change, for reasons the next section explains. What the granola brings is everything it picked up in its first bake: a deeper, toasted oat flavour, clusters that make the finished bar chunkier and more interesting to bite through, and whatever extras your granola carries. A granola with hazelnuts and raisins gives you a fruit and nut flapjack without a single extra ingredient to weigh out.

It also makes this a genuinely useful recipe for the end of a packet. The last of a bag of granola — too broken and dusty for bowls, too good to throw away — presses into bars beautifully, because the fine crumbs that look sad in a bowl are exactly what fills the gaps between clusters in the tin and helps the bars hold together.

Why the sugar and syrup are cut: granola is sweetened already

This is the one change that matters, so it is worth understanding. The classic flapjack uses equal weights of butter, syrup and sugar because raw porridge oats bring no sweetness and no fat of their own. Granola brings both. It was baked with oil, which coats every oat, and with sugar or honey, which is part of why it clusters. If you added the full classic quantities on top, you would get bars that are cloying, loose at the edges and prone to spreading rather than setting.

So the butter stays at 175g — it is the main binder and the flavour — while the golden syrup drops to 125g and the sugar to 100g. The granola's own sweetness makes up the difference, and its oil means the mixture needs less help to coat and bind. The pinch of salt stays too: granolas vary in how salted they are, and a pinch keeps the sweetness in check.

That is the whole list — five ingredients, one of which is a pinch of salt. You also need a 20cm square tin, baking parchment to line it, and a large saucepan.

Choosing a granola: plainer and lower in sugar is better

The bars can only be as good as the granola, and granolas vary enormously. For your first batch, choose a plain or honey granola with a short ingredient list — oats first — and check the back of the packet: granolas sweetened heavily or loaded with chocolate are made for bowls, and in a flapjack they push the bars towards sickly and make them set less cleanly. A plainer, lower-sugar granola lets the butter do the talking and gives you a bar that tastes of toasted oats rather than of syrup on syrup.

Think about cluster size too. Big, chunky clusters look wonderful in a bowl but leave gaps in a pressed slab; small clusters and a bit of oat dust press down into a bar that holds together. You do not need a special brand — just break the big clusters up with your fingers or the back of a spoon before the granola goes into the pan, aiming for nothing much bigger than a thumbnail. And whatever is in your granola is in your bars: seeds, coconut, dried fruit and nuts all carry over. If you are choosing between two packets, the one whose extras you would happily eat in a flapjack is the right one — our guide to the best oats for flapjacks explains the oat side of the equation in more depth.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking parchment, leaving a little overhang at two sides so the slab lifts out easily later.
  2. Break up the clusters. Tip the granola into a bowl and break up any cluster much bigger than your thumbnail with your fingers or the back of a spoon. Big clusters leave gaps in the slab and gaps become weak spots where bars break. Keep the dusty crumbs — they are useful filler, not waste.
  3. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put the butter, golden syrup and light muscovado in a large saucepan on a low heat on the hob and let them melt together, stirring now and then, until smooth. Do not let the mixture boil — boiled syrup sets hard and makes hard flapjacks.
  4. Stir the granola through. Take the pan off the heat, add the pinch of salt and the granola, and stir until every cluster is coated and glossy. Then let the mixture sit in the pan for a minute or two: granola clusters are dry in the middle, and a short rest lets the syrup soak in rather than pool at the bottom of the tin.
  5. Press the mixture into the tin. Spoon it into the lined tin and press down hard and evenly with the back of the spoon. Granola is springier than raw oats, so this takes more effort than the classic — work it well into the corners and keep the surface level. Firm pressing is the difference between bars and crumbs.
  6. Bake for 25–30 minutes. Bake until the edges look set and a shade darker. Remember the granola was already toasted once, so it colours faster than raw oats do — start checking from 20 minutes, and take the tin out while the centre still looks slightly soft; it firms as it cools.
  7. Cool completely, then cut. Leave the slab to cool in the tin until completely cold. Granola flapjacks are at their most fragile while warm. Once cold, lift the slab out on the parchment and cut into 12 bars with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down.

Texture: chunkier than the classic, and a little more crumbly

Set your expectations before the first bite. A granola flapjack is not the smooth, dense chew of the classic: the surface is knobbly, the bite breaks through clusters, and the edges shed a few crumbs. That is the trade you make for the toasted flavour and the nuts, seeds and fruit that come built in. Most people who make both keep both — the classic for purists, this one for using up a good granola and for a bar with more going on in it.

If your batch comes out more crumbly than you would like, the fixes are all in the making: clusters broken smaller, a longer rest in the pan so the syrup soaks in, and harder pressing — really lean on the spoon, especially at the corners. What will not fix it is extra syrup: with an already-sweetened granola, more syrup loosens the set as fast as it binds it.

Storing granola flapjacks: an airtight tin, and eat the fruity ones first

Keep the cut bars in an airtight tin at room temperature, with baking parchment between layers if you stack them. Plain granola bars should keep well for up to 5 days, in line with the classic flapjack. Bars made with a fruit-heavy granola soften a little faster, because dried fruit draws in moisture from the air and from the bar itself — eat those within about 3 days, and keep the tin somewhere cool and dry rather than in the fridge, which dries flapjacks out.

To freeze, wrap the bars individually or layer them in a box with parchment between, and freeze for up to 3 months. Defrost at room temperature for a couple of hours. Clusters soften slightly after freezing but the bars eat well; a few seconds in a warm oven brings back some of the toasted edge if you want it.
