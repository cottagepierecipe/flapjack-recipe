Cherry Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

Cherries and oats are a natural pairing: the fruit brings a deep, jammy sweetness that stands up to golden syrup, and the bars look properly festive when you cut them. The one thing that makes or breaks this version is the state of the cherries when they go in — rinsed, dried and quartered glacé cherries fold through cleanly, while wet, whole ones loosen the set and make a mess of the cutting.

The short answer: Stir 125g glacé cherries — rinsed, patted dry and quartered — through the classic mixture of 175g butter, 175g golden syrup, 175g light brown sugar and 350g porridge oats. Press into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes. Cool completely in the tin, then cut into 12 bars.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Why cherries work in flapjacks: firm fruit that survives the bake

Most soft fruit falls apart or leaks in a flapjack. Cherries are different. A glacé cherry is already preserved in syrup, so it holds its shape through 35 minutes in the oven, stays chewy rather than collapsing, and gives you a distinct bite of fruit in every mouthful instead of a general fruity blur. Dried cherries work the same way for the same reason: the moisture has already been dealt with before the fruit meets the oats.

That reliability is why this recipe builds on our classic flapjack without changing its bones. The butter, syrup, sugar and oat quantities stay exactly as tested; the cherries are an addition, not a substitution, so the mixture that coats and binds the oats is untouched. If you have baked the fruit flapjack on this site, think of this as its single-fruit cousin — one fruit, properly prepared, in a bigger role.

You will also need a 20cm square tin, baking paper for lining, and a large saucepan. The tin size matters: a bigger tin spreads the same mixture thinner, and thinner bars bake faster and cut differently. If your tin is a different size, the adjustments are covered in our guide to flapjack tin sizes.

Glacé or dried cherries: the two routes compared

Both work, and they give slightly different bars. Glacé cherries are the traditional choice — bright, sweet and soft, the ones you see in a cherry cake. Dried cherries are less sweet, a little tart, and chewier. The table below sets out the practical differences so you can choose on purpose rather than on habit.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Heat the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving paper overhanging two opposite sides so the cooled slab lifts out cleanly.

  2. Prepare the cherries. If using glacé cherries, tip them into a sieve and rinse under the cold tap to wash off the sticky syrup they are packed in. Shake well, then spread them on kitchen paper and pat dry. Quarter each cherry with a sharp knife. Dried cherries need none of this — just weigh them out.

  3. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put the butter, golden syrup and light brown sugar in a large saucepan and warm over a low heat, stirring now and then, until the butter has melted and the sugar has dissolved into a smooth, glossy mixture. Do not let it boil: a boiled syrup mixture sets hard and makes tough bars.

  4. Add the almond extract, if using. Take the pan off the heat and stir in the almond extract. Half a teaspoon is plenty — almond extract is strong, and you want it as a background warmth behind the cherries, not the main flavour.

  5. Stir in the oats and cherries. Tip in the porridge oats and the pinch of salt and stir until every oat is coated and glossy, with no dry patches at the bottom of the pan. Fold the cherries through last, stirring gently so the quarters spread evenly through the mixture rather than gathering in one corner.

  6. Press into the tin. Tip the mixture into the lined tin and spread it into the corners. Press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon or the base of a glass. Firm pressing is what gives flapjacks their dense, sliceable texture — a loosely packed slab crumbles.

  7. Bake until golden at the edges. Bake for 30–35 minutes until the edges are golden and the centre looks set, with the surface an even pale gold. The cherries on top should look darkened and glossy, not shrivelled. Our guide to how to tell when flapjacks are done covers the cues in more detail.

  8. Cool completely in the tin. Leave the slab to cool in the tin on a wire rack. Flapjack firms up as it cools — the syrup mixture that felt soft in the oven sets into the chew you want. Cutting a warm cherry flapjack drags the cherries and tears the bars.

  9. Lift out and cut into 12 bars. Lift the cold slab out by the paper and cut into 12 bars with a large, sharp knife, pressing straight down. Quartered cherries cut cleanly; you should see red quarters dotted through every bar.

Why the cherries are rinsed and dried: syrup is the enemy of the set

Glacé cherries arrive coated in the thick syrup they were preserved in. That syrup feels harmless, but in the tin it does two unhelpful things. First, it adds unmeasured liquid sugar in the wrong places, pooling around the fruit instead of coating the oats, so the bars around each cherry set softer and can fall away when cut. Second, given half a chance it bleeds: pink streaks run from the cherries through the surrounding crumb, which looks untidy and tastes of little more than sugar.

Rinsing takes the coating off in seconds, and patting the cherries dry on kitchen paper means they go into the mixture as fruit, not as fruit plus a spoonful of syrup. The cherries themselves stay sweet — the sugar is inside them, not just on them — so you lose none of the flavour, only the mess.

Why quartering matters: whole cherries fight the knife

A whole glacé cherry is roughly the size of the flapjack's depth. Leave them whole and three things go wrong. The cherries bulge out of the surface and catch the full force of the oven heat, so they darken and toughen while the oats are still pale. They create weak points in the slab, because a sphere barely bonds to the oats around it. And when you come to cut the bars, the knife meets a round, slippery cherry that either squashes, pops out or drags through the bar, leaving a hole and a torn edge.

Quarters sit flat, spread their flavour across more of the bar, and slice through without argument. It takes two minutes at the start and saves the finish. If your dried cherries are large, halving them is enough — the aim is pieces that are small relative to the depth of the slab.

Cherry flapjack texture: what you are aiming for

A good cherry flapjack eats like the classic with interruptions — in the best sense. The oats should be chewy and dense, golden at the edges, and every second or third bite should land on a cherry: soft, sweet and slightly jammy against the toffee flavour of the base. If the bars are crisp all through, they were probably baked a few minutes too long; if they bend and sag, they needed a firmer press or a touch more time. The cherry version follows the same rules as the chewy flapjack: take it out while the centre still looks a fraction soft, and let cooling do the rest.

Storing cherry flapjacks: an airtight tin and five days

Once the bars are completely cold, store them in an airtight tin or container at room temperature and eat within about five days. There is no need for the fridge, which only firms the butter and dulls the chew. Keep the layers separated with a sheet of baking paper if you stack them, as the cherry pieces can stick to the bar above. The storage rules are the same as for any flapjack on this site — our guide to how to store flapjacks and how long they last goes into freezing and refreshing day-old bars as well.

One last note on occasions: cherry flapjacks keep and travel well, cut neatly, and look festive without any decoration, which makes them a sound choice for bake sales, lunchboxes and the Christmas baking round.
