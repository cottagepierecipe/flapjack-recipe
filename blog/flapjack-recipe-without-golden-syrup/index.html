Flapjack Recipe Without Golden Syrup

By Michael

No golden syrup in the cupboard does not mean no flapjacks. The main recipe below uses runny honey instead — more honey than the syrup it replaces, a little less sugar, and a slightly shorter bake because honey browns faster. After the recipe there is a comparison of the other routes: maple syrup, brown sugar on its own, and date paste.

The short answer: Melt 175g butter with 200g runny honey and 100g light brown sugar, stir in 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt, press into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C / 140°C fan / Gas Mark 3 for about 25–30 minutes until the edges are golden. Cool in the tin and cut into 12. Never give honey to a baby under 12 months.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Flapjack Without Golden Syrup Ingredients: The Honey Route

Golden syrup does two jobs in the classic flapjack: it sweetens, and its thick, sticky body binds the oats as it cools. Honey can do both jobs, but it is not a straight swap. Honey is thinner than golden syrup and tastes sweeter, and it carries more water, so the mixture needs more honey by weight and less sugar alongside it — 200g honey with 100g light brown sugar in place of the classic's 175g syrup and 175g sugar. The bake is a touch shorter, because honey browns faster than syrup and the edges will catch if you give it the full classic timing.

IngredientAmountNotes
Butter175gThe same weight as the classic; salted or unsalted
Runny honey200gRunny, not set — set honey is hard to melt evenly
Light brown sugar100gLess than the classic's 175g because honey is sweeter
Porridge oats350gPorridge oats give the chewiest set
Fine sea saltA pinchKeeps the honey sweetness in balance

One firm rule before anything melts: children under 12 months must not have honey, in baking or any other form. If any of these bars are intended for a baby under one, use one of the other routes in the comparison table instead — the brown-sugar-only version is the simplest safe swap.

The Method, Step by Step: Honey Browns Faster, So Watch the Edges

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Heat the oven to 160°C / 140°C fan / Gas Mark 3 and line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving an overhang on two sides.
  2. Melt the butter, honey and sugar. Put the butter, runny honey and light brown sugar in a large pan over a low heat and stir until the butter has melted and the sugar has dissolved. Do not let it boil — honey that boils loses its flavour and can turn bitter.
  3. Stir in the oats and salt. Take the pan off the heat, tip in the porridge oats and the pinch of salt, and stir until every oat is glossy and coated with no dry patches left.
  4. Press into the tin. Scrape the mixture into the lined tin, spread it level and press down firmly with the back of a spoon, especially into the corners.
  5. Bake for about 25–30 minutes. Bake until the edges are a clear golden brown and the centre still looks slightly soft — honey browns faster than golden syrup, so start checking at 25 minutes and take the tin out as soon as the edges colour. Leave it in too long and the honey edges turn dark and bitter.
  6. Cool in the tin, then cut. Leave the slab to cool in the tin for at least 20 minutes; it firms as it cools. Mark into 12 bars while still slightly warm, then cut through once cold and lift out on the paper.

How a Honey Flapjack Differs from the Classic: Flavour, Colour and Set

Honey changes the flavour more than any other swap on this page. The bars taste of honey in a way the classic does not taste of syrup — floral if your honey is floral, darker and more caramel-like if it is a dark honey. The colour is deeper too: honey's sugars brown readily, which is why the bake is shorter and why the finished bars come out a richer amber than the classic's pale gold.

The set is slightly softer. Honey holds more water than golden syrup, so the centre stays chewy rather than firming up as much. If you like a firmer bar, bake at the longer end of the time and cool the tin completely before cutting — but do not push the bake further to chase firmness, because the edges pay for it. Our honey flapjack recipe page uses honey alongside sugar in the classic proportions and explains the same browning behaviour; this post's recipe is the fuller swap, made for when there is no syrup in the house at all. The two approaches are compared side by side in honey vs golden syrup for flapjacks.

The Other Routes Compared: Maple Syrup, Sugar Only and Date Paste

Honey is the main recipe because it is the swap most kitchens already have, but it is not the only one. Each route below keeps the classic's 175g butter and 350g oats unless stated, and each behaves differently enough that the adjustment matters.

RouteWhat changesHow it turns out
Runny honey (main recipe)200g honey + 100g light brown sugar; bake about 25–30 minutesSweet, distinctly honey-flavoured, deep amber, soft-chewy centre
Maple syrup175ml maple syrup + 175g light brown sugar; increase oats to 375gMaple syrup is much thinner, so extra oats are needed; softer set, gentle maple flavour
Brown sugar only225g light brown sugar + 2 tbsp water melted with the butterNo syrup at all; more crumbly and less chewy, with a plain caramel flavour
Date pasteDates blended with water replace the syrup and most of the sugarDark, fruity and less sweet; the full method is in our date flapjack recipe

The maple route deserves one extra warning: maple syrup is mostly water compared with golden syrup, so a straight swap without the extra oats gives a slack mixture that spreads thin and sets soft. The brown-sugar route is the one to choose for babies under 12 months and for anyone avoiding honey altogether — the 2 tablespoons of water stand in for the liquid the syrup would have provided, and the crumblier texture is the honest price of it.

Getting the Syrup Measure Right When You Do Have Golden Syrup

If you have golden syrup but are unsure how much a flapjack needs — too little and the bars crumble, too much and they set greasy and bendy — that question has its own full answer in how much golden syrup in flapjacks, including how the amount shifts the texture. The short version is that the classic's equal weights of butter, syrup and sugar to double the oats is the balance the swaps on this page are all trying to match.

Weighing Honey and Syrup: Put the Pan on the Scales

Sticky ingredients are where flapjack quantities quietly go wrong. A cook who measures 200g of honey with a spoon leaves a good spoonful behind in the jar and another coating the spoon, and the bars come out drier than intended. Put the pan on the scales, zero it, and pour the honey straight in until the display reads 200g — the same trick works for golden syrup in the classic recipe. If your honey has set in the jar, stand the jar in warm water for 10 minutes until it runs again rather than trying to melt a solid lump in the pan, where it scorches before it melts through.

Volume measures cause the same problem across the Atlantic. A recipe written in cups of syrup and sticks of butter converts badly to grams, because syrups of different kinds weigh differently per cup — honey is heavier than golden syrup, and maple syrup is lighter than both. That is one more reason the weights on this page are given for each swap separately rather than as one universal number: the binding has to be right by weight, not by spoon.

What Not to Reach For: Treacle, Agave and Granulated Sugar

Three cupboard stand-ins disappoint. Black treacle is too bitter and too strongly flavoured at flapjack quantities — a tablespoon stirred into the honey version adds depth, but a full swap overwhelms the oats. Agave syrup is thinner and sweeter than honey with little flavour of its own; bars made with it set soft and taste flat. Granulated sugar melted with water is sometimes suggested as homemade syrup, but it recrystallises as the bars cool and gives a grainy, fudge-like set rather than a chewy one. The brown-sugar route in the table works precisely because the sugar melts into the butter rather than into water alone.

Storing Honey Flapjacks: An Airtight Tin for Up to 5 Days

Keep the cut bars in an airtight tin at room temperature for up to 5 days. Honey holds moisture, so these bars stay soft rather than crisping at the edges as the days pass; if anything they become chewier. They freeze well for up to 3 months, wrapped individually. Thaw at room temperature for about an hour. Because honey bars brown faster, leftovers are best eaten cold — reheating in a hot oven darkens them quickly.
