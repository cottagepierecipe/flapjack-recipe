Raisin and Sultana Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

Take the classic flapjack — butter, golden syrup, sugar and oats — and stir through a generous handful of raisins and sultanas, and you get the flapjack most people grew up with: chewy bars studded with fruit that swells gently in the baking and turns almost jammy at the edges. This version keeps the tested classic exactly as it is and simply folds in 150g of mixed dried fruit, with an optional short soak first if you like your fruit plump.

The short answer: Melt 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado sugar, then stir in 350g porridge oats, a pinch of salt and 150g mixed raisins and sultanas — soak the fruit in hot water for 10 minutes first if you like it plump, and drain and dry it well. Press into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes. Cool completely in the tin, cut into 12 bars, and keep about 5 days in an airtight tin.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Raisins and sultanas in flapjacks: what the fruit does

A plain flapjack is all chew and caramel. Dried fruit breaks that up: every few bites you hit a raisin that has taken in a little of the syrup and gone soft and jammy, or a sultana that has stayed bright and sharp against the sweetness. The fruit brings its own moisture too, which is why fruited flapjacks often stay chewy a day longer than plain ones, and its sugars deepen the colour — a raisin flapjack comes out a shade darker than the classic flapjack baked for the same time.

The quantity matters more than the choice of fruit. This recipe uses 150g of fruit against 350g of oats, and that ratio is the balance point: enough that nearly every bite contains fruit, but not so much that the oats stop being the structure. Go much beyond 200g and the extra moisture and sugar work against the set — the bars turn soft in the middle, cut raggedly and crumble at the corners. If you want a heavier fruit load, use a recipe built for it, like our date flapjack, where the fruit is cooked down into the binder itself.

Raisins vs sultanas for flapjacks: the difference, and the soaking question

Raisins and sultanas are both dried grapes, but they are not the same fruit wearing two names — the table below sets out the difference. In a flapjack it is small but real: raisins hold their shape and chew, sultanas plump up softer and sweeter. Half and half — 75g of each — gives you both textures in the same bar, which is why this recipe mixes them rather than choosing a side.

Then there is the soaking question. A 10-minute soak in hot water plumps dried fruit back up, and plump fruit behaves better in the oven: it is less likely to dry out, shrivel or catch at the edges, and it gives a softer, juicier bite. The catch is the water itself — soaked fruit must be drained and patted properly dry with a clean tea towel or kitchen paper before it meets the oats, because extra water is the enemy of a firm set. Soak and dry well, and you get the best of both. Skip the soak and the bars still work; the fruit will simply be chewier. What does not work is the halfway version — fruit tipped in dripping — so if you are short of time, no soak beats a careless one.

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper, a large pan for the mixture, and — if you are soaking — a bowl and a clean tea towel for drying the fruit. Nothing else changes from the classic: same tin, same oven temperature, same method.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides for lifting the slab out later.
  2. Soak the fruit, if you are soaking. Put the 75g raisins and 75g sultanas in a bowl, cover with hot water from the kettle and leave for 10 minutes. Drain in a sieve, then tip onto a clean tea towel or a double layer of kitchen paper and pat thoroughly dry. The fruit should feel plump but surface-dry.
  3. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. In a large pan over a low heat, melt 175g butter with 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado sugar, stirring until you have one smooth, glossy liquid. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil — boiled syrup sets like toffee and makes hard bars.
  4. Stir in the oats and salt. Take the pan off the heat. Tip in 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt and stir until every flake is coated and shiny and there are no dry patches at the bottom of the pan.
  5. Fold in the fruit (and zest). Add the raisins and sultanas, and the orange zest if you are using it. Stir well, turning the mixture over on itself, until the fruit is spread evenly from edge to edge — clumps of fruit in one corner mean bare oats in another.
  6. Press into the tin. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly, working into the corners. As you finish, press any fruit sitting proud on the surface gently down into the mixture — exposed fruit is the fruit that scorches.
  7. Bake for 30–35 minutes. The top should be golden brown, a shade deeper than a plain flapjack because of the fruit sugars, with the edges slightly darker. The centre should look set and feel firm with a slight give when pressed.
  8. Score while warm. After about 10 minutes out of the oven, mark the portion lines with a table knife — four by three for 12 bars. Scoring now, while the top is still soft, stops it cracking later.
  9. Cool completely in the tin. Leave the slab in the tin until it is fully cold and set. Fruited flapjacks are soft while warm and will slump and smear if you cut them early.
  10. Lift out and cut into 12. Use the paper overhang to lift the cold slab onto a board and cut along the scored lines with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down and wiping the blade between cuts.

Troubleshooting: stopping the fruit from scorching

The one real risk in a fruited flapjack is not the oats — it is the fruit on the surface. Raisins and sultanas are full of sugar, and a raisin sitting exposed on top of the slab takes the oven's heat directly and can go from deep brown to burnt while the rest of the bar is still perfect. Three habits prevent it. First, the pressing step: fruit pushed just below the surface is insulated by the mixture around it. Second, the soak: plumped fruit has moisture to spare and dries out more slowly. Third, your eyes in the last five minutes: if the top fruit is darkening much faster than the oats, lay a loose sheet of foil over the tin for the remaining time. Do not turn the oven down and bake longer instead — a slow, cool bake dries the oats out and gives you a crumbly bar.

Underbaking is the opposite mistake, and the fruit disguises it: a fruited slab looks darker and more done than a plain one at the same stage. Judge by the centre, not the colour — it should look set across the whole surface, with no wet sheen, and feel firm when pressed gently. Flapjacks finish setting as they cool, so a slightly soft-looking centre is right; a loose, glossy one needs a few more minutes.

Variations: orange, spice and other dried fruit

The orange zest in the ingredients is the variation to try first: it lifts the whole bar and makes the fruit taste more like itself. Ground spice works the same way — a teaspoon of cinnamon, or mixed spice for a more wintry bar, stirred in with the oats. The fruit can be swapped freely as long as the total stays at 150g: dried cranberries bring a welcome sharpness, chopped dried apricots a softer, tangier chew, and glacé cherries a bar that looks as good as it tastes. Keep at least part of the mix as raisins or sultanas if you can — very soft fruits alone can make the bars a touch too moist. And if you want fruit all the way through rather than in pieces, our date flapjack recipe cooks the fruit down into the mixture itself.

Storing raisin and sultana flapjacks: about 5 days

Keep the cut bars in an airtight tin at room temperature, with baking paper between the layers so they do not stick to each other. They keep about 5 days, and the fruit works in your favour here — raisins and sultanas hold moisture, so these bars stay chewy to the end of the week rather than drying at the edges the way a plain flapjack can. They also freeze as well as the classic does: wrap bars individually, seal them in a bag or box, and defrost at room temperature in the wrapping.
