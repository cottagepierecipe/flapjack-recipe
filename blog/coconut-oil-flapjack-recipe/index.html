Coconut Oil Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

Coconut oil is the one-for-one swap for butter that actually works in a flapjack, because a flapjack asks so little of its fat: melt, coat the oats, set again as it cools. This version takes the tested classic and replaces the butter with exactly the same weight of coconut oil — nothing else changes — and the interesting part is not the swap itself but what the different fat does to the bars afterwards, in the tin, in the fridge and in a warm kitchen.

The short answer: Melt 175g coconut oil, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado sugar. Stir in 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt. Press into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes. Cool completely in the tin and cut into 12 bars. Use refined coconut oil for a neutral taste, or unrefined if you want the coconut flavour. With golden syrup — which is vegan — and no other change, the recipe is vegan.

Coconut oil flapjacks: the classic recipe with 175g of oil in place of the butter

Most butter swaps in baking are compromises, because butter does several jobs at once — it flavours, it creams, it laminates, it browns. A flapjack asks for none of that. In our classic flapjack recipe, the butter's whole job is to melt with the golden syrup and sugar, coat 350g of porridge oats, and help the syrup set the bars as they cool. Any fat that is solid when cool and liquid when warm can do that job, and coconut oil is exactly that kind of fat. So the swap is clean: 175g of coconut oil where 175g of butter used to be, and the syrup, sugar, oats, tin, temperature and timing all stay as tested.

That does not mean the bars are identical. Butter is about four-fifths fat, with water and milk solids making up the rest; coconut oil is essentially all fat, and it behaves differently at each end of the thermometer. It melts faster and at a lower temperature than butter, and it sets harder when it gets cold. In the pan that barely matters. In the finished bars it matters a great deal, and it is the main thing to understand before you bake: a coconut oil flapjack is a bar whose firmness depends on where you keep it, more than a butter flapjack's ever does.

There is also a flavour decision to make before you start, because coconut oil comes in two forms that taste completely different. That choice — refined or unrefined — decides whether these bars taste like the classic with a different texture, or like a coconut flapjack in their own right.

Refined or unrefined: the choice that decides the flavour

Refined coconut oil has been processed to remove the coconut taste and smell. In a flapjack it does its work quietly: the bars taste of golden syrup, muscovado and toasted oats, and most people would not guess the butter was gone. Unrefined coconut oil — often sold as virgin coconut oil — keeps the full coconut flavour, and in a warm, sweet, oaty bar that flavour comes through clearly. Neither is better; they are two different flapjacks from the same method. If the point of the swap is that you have run out of butter, or that the bars need to be dairy-free without tasting any different, refined is the one. If the point is coconut, use unrefined and lean into it.

Type of coconut oilFlavour in the barsBest for
Refined coconut oilNeutral — the bars taste of syrup, sugar and oats, like the classicA straight butter replacement; dairy-free baking where nobody wants to taste the swap
Unrefined (virgin) coconut oilClear coconut flavour through the whole barBaking coconut flapjacks on purpose, especially with desiccated coconut added (see below)
Liquid coconut oil (fractionated)Neutral, but it never setsNot this recipe — it stays liquid at room temperature, so the bars would not firm up properly

That last row matters when you shop. The coconut oil for this recipe is the kind sold in a jar as a firm white solid. Liquid coconut oil, sold in bottles for cooking and dressings, has been fractionated so that it never solidifies — and a flapjack needs its fat to set. If the jar in your cupboard is solid in a cool kitchen and liquid on a hot day, you have the right one.

A vegan flapjack with no other changes

Because the classic recipe contains no eggs and no milk chocolate, the butter is the only non-vegan ingredient in it. Swap the butter for coconut oil and the recipe is vegan — provided the golden syrup stays, which it should, because golden syrup is vegan. Honey is the trap here: it is the swap people reach for when they change a flapjack's sweetener, and honey is not vegan. Keep the syrup and this recipe needs no other adjustment at all. Our dedicated vegan flapjack recipe builds on the same idea with its own quantities and variations, and it is worth reading alongside this one if vegan baking is your main interest — the two pages approach the same bars from different directions, this one as a straight swap in the classic, that one as a recipe written vegan from the start.

The same swap also makes the bars dairy-free, which is not quite the same thing as vegan but often the actual reason for making it. Everything else in the recipe — oats, syrup, muscovado sugar — is naturally free of dairy, so a coconut oil flapjack serves both purposes with one change. Do check the label on your oats if you are baking for someone who avoids gluten as well as dairy; oats themselves are gluten-free, but standard porridge oats are often milled alongside wheat, and only certified gluten-free oats remove that doubt.

Ingredients

IngredientAmountNotes

Golden syrup175gThe binder as well as a sweetener — weigh it into the pan with the oil. Golden syrup is vegan
Light muscovado sugar175gThe classic's sugar; light brown sugar is the nearest swap
Porridge oats350gStandard porridge oats, not jumbo — jumbo oats bind less well
Fine sea saltA pinchKeeps the sweetness in check; it matters more here because there is no salted butter
Desiccated coconut (optional)2 tbspAn optional extra, sprinkled over the top before baking or stirred through. Best with unrefined oil

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper and a large pan. One small difference from the classic: butter can be salted, which quietly seasons the batch, while coconut oil never is — so the pinch of salt in the ingredients is doing slightly more work here, and it should not be skipped.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides for lifting the slab out later.
  2. Melt the coconut oil, syrup and sugar. Put 175g coconut oil, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado in a large pan over a low heat. The oil will turn liquid quickly — faster than butter does — and that is normal. Stir until everything has melted into one smooth mixture, and never let it boil: boiled syrup sets like toffee and makes hard bars.
  3. Add the oats and salt. Take the pan off the heat and tip in 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt. Stir until every oat is coated and shiny and there are no dry patches hiding at the bottom of the pan. The mixture may look a touch glossier than the butter version; coconut oil coats thinly and evenly.
  4. Add the desiccated coconut, if using. Stir in 2 tablespoons of desiccated coconut now, or save it to sprinkle over the top in the next step. Through the mixture it flavours every bite; on top it toasts and shows.
  5. Press into the tin. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working well into the corners. Firm pressing is what stops flapjacks crumbling later, and it matters just as much with oil as with butter. If you kept the desiccated coconut back, scatter it over the surface now and press it in lightly.
  6. Bake for 30–35 minutes. The classic's timing, unchanged. The top should be golden brown with the edges a shade darker, and the centre should look set but still feel slightly soft when pressed — it firms up as it cools, and a coconut oil flapjack firms up more than most.
  7. Score while warm. About 5 minutes after the tin comes out, mark the portion lines with a table knife — 12 bars — while the top is soft enough not to crack. Do not skip this step on this recipe: once a coconut oil flapjack is cold and fully set, it is noticeably harder to cut.
  8. Cool completely in the tin. Leave the slab until it is cold and set before cutting. Warm flapjack cuts raggedly and can fall apart; cold coconut oil flapjack cuts cleanly but firmly, so this step is about letting it reach that middle state of fully set.
  9. Lift out and cut. Use the paper overhang to lift the cold slab onto a board and cut through along the scored lines with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down. If the slab has been in the fridge, let it stand at room temperature for 15 minutes first — cutting a fridge-cold coconut oil flapjack is how knives slip and bars crack.

Setting, the fridge and the warm room: how the bars behave

This is the section that decides whether you love this swap or find it baffling. Coconut oil melts at a lower temperature than butter and sets harder when cold, so the same bar can be three different textures in three different places. Straight from the fridge, a coconut oil flapjack is firm — firmer than a butter flapjack ever gets — and it can feel almost hard on the teeth. In a cool kitchen tin it is a normal, well-set flapjack. In a warm room, or in a lunchbox that has sat in the sun, it softens more than a butter bar does, because the fat holding it together is close to melting again. None of these is a failed flapjack; they are the same bake at different temperatures.

Where the bars are keptHow they eatWhat to do
In the fridgeVery firm, almost hard; clean snap when cutNothing wrong — but let a bar stand at room temperature for 15 minutes before eating or cutting
In a tin at cool room temperatureProperly set and chewy, closest to the classicThe right home for them; this is how the storage times below are measured
In a warm room or a hot carSoft, and can feel slightly greasy as the oil nears meltingMove them somewhere cooler; they firm up again as the oil re-sets, with no harm done

The practical lessons follow from that. Score the bars while warm, because cold-set coconut oil resists the knife. Store them in the tin, not the fridge, unless your kitchen is genuinely hot. And if a batch seems disappointingly hard, check where it has been sitting before you blame the recipe — a quarter of an hour on the worktop usually solves it. If bars do crumble rather than set, that is a different problem, and our guide to why flapjacks fall apart covers the pressing and syrup causes that apply to every version.

Storing coconut oil flapjacks

Keep the cut bars in an airtight tin at room temperature, with baking paper between the layers. They keep about 5 days; because the oil sets firmly, they hold their edges and travel well, which makes them good lunchbox bars as long as the lunchbox does not sit somewhere hot. They freeze well — wrap the bars individually, seal them in a bag or box and use within 3 months. Defrost at room temperature in the wrapping, and give them a few minutes after defrosting before judging the texture, because a just-defrosted coconut oil flapjack is at its hardest. There is more detail in our guide to how to store flapjacks and how long they last.
