Air Fryer Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

Flapjack in an air fryer works — as long as you respect the two things that make the machine different from an oven: the batch has to be small, and the heat comes from directly above, fast. This version halves our classic recipe, bakes it in a small tin that fits the basket, and treats the first batch as a test, because no two air fryers behave quite alike.

The short answer: Melt 90g butter, 90g golden syrup and 90g light brown sugar, stir in 175g porridge oats and a pinch of salt, and press into a lined 15cm round tin or heatproof dish that fits your air fryer basket. Air fry at 150°C (estimate) for 15–18 minutes (estimate) until the edges are golden and the centre just set. Rest in the dish for 15 minutes before lifting out. Makes 6 bars.

Not yet kitchen-tested: this recipe adapts our tested classic flapjack and has not been cooked by us yet. Michael tests every new recipe himself, and this page will be updated once he has cooked it. Times marked (estimate) are estimates.

Can you make flapjacks in an air fryer? Yes, in a smaller, thinner batch

An air fryer is a small convection oven: a heating element and a powerful fan in a tight space. Anything that bakes in a shallow tin in a conventional oven can, in principle, bake in an air fryer — and flapjack, which is pressed flat and baked until set rather than risen, is one of the better candidates. The oat mixture does not need headroom, does not soufflé, and tells you plainly when it is done.

What you cannot do is take the full classic flapjack — 350g of oats in a 20cm tin — and simply move it into the basket. Most baskets will not take a 20cm tin at all, and a slab that thick in that fierce, close heat would scorch on top before the centre set. So this recipe halves the quantities and uses a 15cm tin, giving a thinner slab that suits the machine. The flavour and method are the classic's; only the scale and the cooking vessel change.

You also need a 15cm round cake tin or a heatproof dish of a similar size that fits inside your air fryer basket with space around it, plus baking paper to line it. Check the fit with the empty tin before you make the mixture — this is the step that decides whether the recipe happens at all. The temperature drops by about 10 degrees and the time roughly halves. That is not a formula to apply blindly to every air fryer recipe; it reflects this slab being thinner and the heat source being inches away rather than spread around a big oven cavity.

The method, step by step

  1. Check the tin fits, then line it. Put your empty 15cm tin or dish in the air fryer basket and close it. There should be a gap around the tin for air to circulate, and the tin must not touch the element above. Take it out and line it with baking paper, leaving a little paper overhanging to help you lift the slab out later.

  2. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put the butter, golden syrup and light brown sugar in a saucepan on the hob and warm over a low heat, stirring, until smooth and glossy. Do not let it boil — boiled syrup sets hard, in an air fryer batch just as much as an oven one.

  3. Stir in the oats. Take the pan off the heat, add the porridge oats and the pinch of salt, and stir until every oat is coated with no dry patches. The mixture will be stiffer and shallower-looking than the full batch; that is correct.

  4. Press into the tin. Tip the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, right into the edges. An even thickness matters more here than in the oven, because a thin patch under that close heat will overcook while a thick patch is still setting.

  5. Air fry at 150°C (estimate) for 15–18 minutes (estimate). Put the tin in the basket and cook. Start checking at 12 minutes: you are looking for golden edges and a centre that looks set rather than wet and shiny. If the top is colouring much faster than the centre is setting, cover the tin loosely with a small piece of foil for the last few minutes — weighed down by the tin's contents, it will stay put. Our guide to how to tell when flapjacks are done lists the cues to judge by, since the clock alone will not tell you.

  6. Rest in the dish for 15 minutes. Take the tin out and leave the flapjack to rest in it for 15 minutes. The slab firms as it cools, and a small, thin slab is fragile while hot. Lifting it out too early is how air fryer flapjacks break.

  7. Lift out and cut into 6. Lift the rested slab out by the paper, put it on a board and cut into 6 bars or wedges with a sharp knife, pressing straight down. Eat warm if you like, or cool completely and store as below.

Why the flapjack must bake in a tin: never on loose paper alone

This is the one firm safety point in the recipe. It is tempting to line the basket itself with baking paper and press the mixture straight onto it, skipping the tin. Do not. Air fryers move a lot of air, and a sheet of baking paper with nothing heavy holding it down can lift, flap and touch the heating element above — a genuine fire risk, and the reason manufacturers warn against loose paper in the basket. Inside a tin, the paper is pinned down by the weight of the mixture and the tin's walls, and it behaves. The tin also gives the slab its shape and protects the bottom from the fierce heat through the basket. If you do not have a tin or dish that fits, this is a recipe for the oven, not a reason to improvise.

Why air fryers vary: treat the first batch as a test

Air fryers are not standardised the way ovens roughly are. Basket size, wattage, how close the element sits to the food, and how honest the temperature dial is all differ between models — a small 2-litre machine runs fiercer than a large drawer model at the same marked temperature. Some models run hot; some have cold spots at the corners. That is why every time and temperature in this post is marked (estimate), and why the honest approach is to bake the first batch when you can watch it, check from 12 minutes, and write down what your machine actually needed. Once you know your air fryer takes, say, 16 minutes at 150°C, the recipe becomes repeatable — your notes turn the estimate into your tested version.

Edges, thickness and setting: judging an air fryer batch

The pattern to expect is fast edges. The fan drives hot air over the top and down the sides, so the rim of the slab colours and crisps first while the centre is still pale. A thinner slab narrows that gap — one more reason this recipe is scaled down rather than squeezed in whole. Judge doneness by the centre, not the edges: the edges will look done several minutes before the middle has set. If you like the soft middle of a chewy flapjack, take the tin out the moment the centre loses its wet shine; the slab sets further as it rests. And keep the bars in an airtight tin at room temperature once cold, eating them within about five days, as you would any flapjack from this site.

Adding flavours to a small batch: keep them dry and modest

The half batch is a good place to experiment, because a failed experiment costs you six bars, not twelve. Keep additions dry and small in quantity: a teaspoon of ground ginger or cinnamon stirred into the oats, 40g of raisins or chopped dried apricots, or a tablespoon of desiccated coconut all sit comfortably in this mixture. Wet additions — fresh fruit, extra syrup, jam swirled through — change the set of a slab that is already thinner than usual, and in an air fryer they also give the top more sugar to scorch. If a variation matters to you, bake it in the oven first using the full-size recipes on this site, then scale it down once you know it works.
