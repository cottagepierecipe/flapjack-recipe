Why Do My Flapjacks Burn at the Edges?

By Michael

It is one of the most frustrating finishes in home baking: the middle of the slab is pale gold and perfect, but the edges and corners have gone dark, bitter and hard. Flapjacks are unusually prone to it, because the very things that make them good — sugar, syrup and a shallow slab — are quick to colour and quicker to burn. The edges always cook faster than the centre; the trick is knowing why, and closing the gap before it turns into burnt corners.

The short answer: Flapjack edges burn because they are thinner, hotter and more exposed than the centre: they carry the same sugar and syrup in less mixture, sit against the hot tin wall, and catch the full force of hotspots and over-hot ovens. Press the mixture to an even depth right into the corners, bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) on the middle shelf, use a light-coloured tin, and take the slab out when the edges are deep gold and the centre is just set — the edges carry on darkening as it cools.

Thin edges, thick middle: the depth problem in the tin

When you tip flapjack mixture into a tin and press it down with the back of a spoon, the mixture naturally ends up deeper in the middle and thinner at the sides — and worst of all in the corners, where the spoon never quite reaches. That thin rim is your burnt edge in waiting. It holds the same butter, sugar and syrup as the rest of the slab, but spread through far less oat, so there is less bulk to absorb the heat and more exposed sugar per bite. Sugar colours fast: it turns golden, then amber, then dark and bitter, and a 5mm edge will race through those stages while a 2.5cm centre is still setting.

The fix is deliberate pressing. Work the mixture right into the corners first, then level the whole slab so a ruler laid across the top would touch it evenly. The classic recipe is built for a 20cm square tin, which gives a slab of the right depth for 350g of oats; tips on matching mixture to tin are in what size tin you need for flapjacks. Use the correct tin and press evenly, and the edges and centre finish within a minute or two of each other instead of ten.

The tin itself: dark metal, glass and the hot wall effect

The edges of a flapjack bake against the wall of the tin, and that wall is hotter than the air around the centre of the slab — heat travels into the mixture by conduction through the metal as well as by the oven air. The tin you choose changes how aggressive that is. Dark-coloured and black non-stick tins absorb more heat and hand it on to the mixture, so edges in a dark tin colour noticeably faster. Thin, cheap metal can warp and develop hot patches. Glass and ceramic heat more slowly but hold their heat, so the edges keep cooking after the tin leaves the oven.

A light-coloured, reasonably heavy metal tin is the kindest choice for flapjacks, and lining it fully with baking paper puts a thin buffer between mixture and metal along the base and sides. Let the paper come up the sides rather than trimming it flush — a paper sling also makes lifting the slab out afterwards clean and easy. If dark tins are what you have, reduce the oven temperature by about 10°C and start checking five minutes early.

Oven heat: too hot, wrong shelf, and the hotspot you cannot see

Flapjacks do not need a fierce oven. The classic bake is 160°C, 140°C fan, Gas Mark 3 — a moderate heat that sets the slab through before the sugar over-colours. Bake at 180°C or higher and the edges, with their head start, will be dark before the middle has set; you then face a choice between burnt edges or a raw centre, when the real answer was a cooler oven. Fan ovens need the reduction: the fan moves hot air constantly over the surface, and edges facing the fan dry and colour faster still. If your oven has no fan setting marked, the standard rule is fan 20°C lower than the conventional temperature in the recipe.

Shelf position matters too. The top shelf sits close to the upper element or the hottest stratum of air in many ovens, and the back corners are classic hotspots. Bake on the middle shelf, and if your oven is known to run hot on one side — most are, to some degree — rotate the tin halfway through the bake so each edge gets an equal turn. If edges burn even at the right temperature in a correctly sized tin, an inexpensive oven thermometer will usually explain it: plenty of domestic ovens read 10–20°C lower on the dial than the heat inside.

Sugar at the edges: why syrup makes it worse

Of everything in the tin, sugar is the ingredient that burns. Golden syrup, once its water has driven off in the oven, caramelises — and caramelisation runs from pleasant to burnt in a narrow window. The edges reach that window first, and they also tend to hold proportionally more syrup: the liquid mixture slumps and flows as it warms, and syrup can pool slightly towards the sides and corners before the slab sets. A flapjack made with extra syrup "for chewiness" therefore burns at the edges sooner, and honey-based bakes colour faster still, because honey browns at a lower temperature than golden syrup.

This is also why the centre can look deceptively pale when the edges are done. The middle is still releasing steam, which holds its temperature down and keeps it light; the dry edges have no such protection. Judge the bake the way it is set out in how to tell when flapjacks are done: deep gold at the edges, golden across the top, and a centre that looks set with just a little give. Then take it out. The slab keeps cooking in its own heat for several minutes — chewy in the middle is a stage the edges pass through on their way to burnt, so err on the side of early. If you love a soft centre, the chewy flapjack recipe is timed for exactly that balance.

Shielding and rescuing: foil, trimming and second uses

If your oven runs hot and edges are a repeat problem, shield them: once the slab is golden across the top but the centre needs a few more minutes, lay a sheet of foil loosely over the tin, or fold narrow foil strips over the edges. Foil reflects heat away from the parts that are done while the middle finishes. Keep the foil loose — pressed down onto hot syrup, it will stick and tear the surface when you lift it off.

For a batch that has already caught, trim honestly. A dark-gold edge is fine eating — caramelised and crisp. A blackened, bitter edge is not improved by optimism: cut a centimetre off each side with a sharp knife once the slab is cold, and eat the rest. Badly burnt trimmings are best thrown away, but sound offcuts and the trimmed bars themselves can start a second life crumbled over ice cream or yoghurt, where a little deep caramel flavour reads as intentional. And note what your oven did — every oven has a personality, and the baker who writes "my oven: 150°C, middle shelf, turn at 15" on the recipe card stops burning edges for good.
