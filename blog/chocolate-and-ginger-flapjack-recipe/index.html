Chocolate and Ginger Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

These flapjacks run two kinds of ginger through the classic base — ground ginger for a warm heat in every bite, crystallised ginger for chewy, fiery pockets — and fold dark chocolate chips through to finish. Ginger and golden syrup are old partners in British baking, and dark chocolate turns out to be the third point of the triangle: bitter against the heat, rich against the spice.

The short answer: Melt 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado sugar, stir in 350g porridge oats, a pinch of salt, 2 teaspoons of ground ginger, 75g finely chopped crystallised ginger and 100g dark chocolate chips, press into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes. Cool completely in the tin and cut into 12 bars.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Chocolate and ginger flapjacks: warmth in every bite, fire in pockets

Ginger is the flavour of half the British baking tradition — gingerbread, parkin, gingersnaps — and it keeps the same company in most of those recipes: dark sugar and golden syrup. A flapjack base is built from exactly that company, which is why ginger settles into it so naturally. The muscovado's deep, almost liquorice sweetness and the syrup's warmth are the same backdrop a ginger cake uses. What a flapjack adds is texture: a dense, chewy oat bar that carries spice the way it carries everything else, evenly and without fuss.

The recipe keeps our classic flapjack recipe completely intact — 175g each of butter, golden syrup and light muscovado to 350g of oats — and simply loads it. Ground ginger goes through the whole mixture so no bite is plain. Crystallised ginger, chopped small, lands in pockets, so one bite in three has a chewy, fiery piece in it. And the dark chocolate chips — the same chips route as our chocolate flapjack recipe — fold through at the end, bitter against the spice. Three additions, no subtractions, and the classic's setting ratio is untouched, which is why the bars hold like the classic does.

Two gingers, two jobs: ground for warmth, crystallised for pockets

Ground ginger is the reliable one. Two teaspoons stirred through the mixture gives a warmth that is present in every mouthful but never shouts — it builds as you eat, the way ginger should. It is also forgiving: half a teaspoon either way changes the emphasis, not the outcome. If you know you like a strong ginger hit, go to three teaspoons; if the bars are for people who are not sure, one and a half. What ground ginger cannot give is texture. It dissolves into the bake and disappears as an ingredient you can see.

Crystallised ginger is the opposite: all presence. It is fresh ginger cooked in syrup and rolled in sugar, sold in bags of soft, chewy chunks, and chopped into the mixture it stays as distinct pieces — sweet on the outside, properly fiery in the middle, chewy in a bar that is itself chewy. Chop it small, about pea-sized: a big chunk in a thin bar is a mouthful of ginger and nothing else. Shake off the loosest of its sugar coating as you chop, so the pieces spread through the mixture rather than clumping, and toss them in with the oats so each piece gets coated and stays where it lands instead of sinking. Seventy-five grams is enough for pockets in most bites without the bars becoming a ginger delivery system.

Ingredients

IngredientAmountNotes
Butter175gSalted or unsalted both work
Golden syrup175gThe full classic amount — ginger and syrup are old partners
Light muscovado sugar175gIts deep flavour is half the gingerbread effect. Light brown sugar swaps in
Porridge oats350gStandard porridge oats, not jumbo — jumbo oats bind less well
Fine sea saltA pinchKeeps the spice and chocolate in balance
Ground ginger2 tspThe warmth through the whole bar. Adjust between 1½ and 3 tsp to taste
Crystallised ginger75gFinely chopped, about pea-sized pieces. Shake off the loosest sugar
Dark chocolate chips100gFolded through. A chopped bar works in the same quantity

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper and a large pan. A sharp knife matters for the crystallised ginger — it is soft and sticky, and a blunt knife drags it into ropes instead of cutting it into pieces. Chopping it with a dusting of the recipe's own oats on the board stops it sticking.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven, line the tin and chop the ginger. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides. Chop 75g crystallised ginger into small, roughly pea-sized pieces and set aside.
  2. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado in a large pan over a low heat. Stir until everything has melted into one smooth liquid. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil — boiled syrup sets like toffee and makes hard bars.
  3. Add the oats, salt and ground ginger. Take the pan off the heat and tip in 350g porridge oats, a pinch of salt and 2 teaspoons of ground ginger. Stir until every oat is coated and shiny and the ginger is spread through evenly — no pale patches.
  4. Fold in the crystallised ginger and the chocolate chips. Add the chopped crystallised ginger and 100g dark chocolate chips and fold them through in a few turns of the spoon. Off the heat, the chips soften at the edges but stay whole, and the ginger pieces coat in the mixture so they stay distributed.
  5. Press into the tin. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working well into the corners. Firm pressing is what holds the bars together once cut.
  6. Bake for 30–35 minutes. The top should be golden brown with the edges a shade darker, and the centre should look set but still feel slightly soft when pressed — it firms up as it cools. The kitchen will smell of ginger well before it is done; go by the look and the feel, not the smell.
  7. Cool completely in the tin. Leave the slab in the tin until fully cold — at least an hour. The chocolate chips need to re-set, and a warm slab cuts raggedly around the ginger pieces.
  8. Lift out and cut into 12. Use the paper overhang to lift the cold slab onto a board. Cut with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down, and wipe the blade between cuts — crystallised ginger drags on a dirty blade and smears the edges.

Chocolate and ginger problems: too fierce, too faint, sunken ginger

Ginger is the variable ingredient here — jars of ground ginger lose strength as they age, and crystallised ginger varies from mild to properly hot between brands — so this recipe's problems are mostly about calibration. The table below covers the usual ones. The general rule: judge the heat by the batch in front of you, and adjust the ground ginger next time rather than trying to fix the baked bars.

What happenedLikely causeThe fix next time
Ginger heat barely thereOld ground ginger, or too little of itUse a fresh jar and go to 3 teaspoons; ground ginger fades in the cupboard within months of opening
One bite mild, the next all gingerCrystallised ginger chopped too big, or clumpedChop to pea-sized pieces, shake off loose sugar and toss through the oats so the pieces separate
Chocolate chips melted into streaksChips stirred too long in the hot mixtureFold in off the heat, in a few turns, and get the mixture into the tin promptly
Bars set hardThe syrup mixture boiled, or the bake ran longKeep the melt at a gentle heat, never boiling, and judge the bake by the edges and a soft centre
Ginger pieces all at the bottomPieces too big and heavy, added to a loose mixtureSmaller pieces, coated in the oat mixture before pressing, and press firmly so nothing can travel

The topping variation: 150g of dark chocolate instead of chips

The recipe as written folds the chocolate through as chips, which puts chocolate in every bite. If you prefer the smarter finish — or the bars are for giving away — swap the arrangement: leave the chips out, bake the ginger base exactly as above, cool it completely in the tin, and spread 150g of melted dark chocolate over the top. A plain chocolate lid against a spiced base is a very clean contrast, and a few spare pieces of crystallised ginger, chopped finer and scattered over the wet chocolate, tell people what is inside before they bite. Set at room temperature for about an hour and cut at room temperature, warming the knife if the layer threatens to crack.

One more variation worth knowing: stem ginger — the kind sold in jars in its own syrup — swaps for crystallised ginger in the same 75g quantity. Drain it well and chop it the same way. It is softer and milder than the crystallised sort, with a rounder, less fiery heat, and because it arrives wet it should be patted dry on kitchen paper first so it does not add moisture to the mixture. Between the three forms — ground, crystallised, stem — the ground is the one never to leave out; it is the warmth the other two build on.

Storing chocolate and ginger flapjacks: about 5 days in an airtight tin

Keep the cut bars in an airtight tin at room temperature and they keep about 5 days. Ginger is one of the flavours that settles well: the bars arguably taste better on the second day, once the ground ginger has spread itself through the crumb and the first fierceness has mellowed into warmth. They freeze well for up to 3 months, wrapped individually and sealed, and the crystallised ginger keeps its chew through freezing better than most inclusions. Defrost at room temperature in the wrapping. The general rules are in our guide to how to store flapjacks and how long they last.

Questions people ask

You can — about a tablespoon of finely grated fresh root, stirred in with the oats, in place of the ground ginger. The flavour is brighter and sharper, with a raw edge ground ginger does not have, and it distributes a little less evenly, so some bites are hotter than others. Most people find ground ginger the better backbone for a baked bar, with the crystallised pieces providing the fresh-style heat in pockets. If fresh is what you have, grate it finely and squeeze out the worst of the juice first.

Not quite, though they start as the same root. Crystallised ginger is cooked in syrup then dried and rolled in sugar, so it is chewy, dry to handle and quite fiery. Stem ginger is kept in its syrup in a jar, so it is softer, wetter and milder. They swap in the same 75g quantity: drain stem ginger well, pat it dry, chop it small. Expect a rounder, gentler heat from the stem version and a firmer bite from the crystallised.

For the batch already baked, serve the bars with something plain alongside — a cup of tea is the traditional answer — and know that the heat mellows noticeably by the second day. For the next batch, the ground ginger is the dial: drop it to 1 teaspoon for warmth without fire, and keep the crystallised ginger at 75g, since its heat arrives in pockets you can see coming. Reducing only the crystallised ginger while keeping 2 teaspoons ground leaves the background heat untouched.

Different, rather than better. Chips put chocolate in every bite and keep the bars informal and packable; a 150g melted topping, spread over the cold base, gives a smarter finish and a cleaner contrast between the plain chocolate lid and the spiced base. The topping version needs the extra setting time and a room-temperature cut so the layer does not crack. If the bars are being posted or lunchboxed, choose chips; if they are being served on a plate, the topping earns its hour.
