Cranberry Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

The classic flapjack is sweet all the way through, and that is exactly what a handful of cranberries is for. This version changes nothing about the tested base — same butter, syrup, sugar and oats, same tin, same bake — and stirs 150g of dried cranberries through the mixture, so every bar is flecked with deep red, sweet-tart fruit that cuts the richness instead of adding to it.

The short answer: Make the classic exactly: melt 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado sugar, then stir in 350g porridge oats, a pinch of salt and 150g dried cranberries (plus the zest of 1 orange, if you like). Press into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes. Cool completely in the tin and cut into 12 bars. Use dried cranberries, never fresh.

Not yet kitchen-tested: this recipe adapts our tested classic flapjack and has not been cooked by us yet. Michael tests every new recipe himself, and this page will be updated once he has cooked it. Times marked (estimate) are estimates.

Cranberry flapjacks: the tested classic with 150g of dried cranberries stirred through

Fruit flapjacks usually sweeten an already sweet bake. Dried cranberries do the opposite, because the cranberries sold for baking are only lightly sweetened and keep most of the berry's natural sharpness. Stirred through a butter-and-syrup mixture, they land in the finished bar as chewy, ruby-red pockets of tartness, and the effect is similar to a good pinch of salt: the whole thing tastes more balanced, and you notice the oats and the butter more, not less. It is the version of a fruit flapjack most likely to be asked for a second piece.

The base is our classic flapjack recipe, the version we have kitchen-tested, and it is used here completely unchanged: equal weights of butter, golden syrup and light muscovado sugar, twice that weight of porridge oats, a pinch of salt, a lined 20cm tin and the classic bake. The cranberries are the only addition, and they go in with the oats, which makes this about as simple as a variation gets. If the classic works in your oven, this should behave in exactly the same way — the berries are passengers, not a structural change.

Dried cranberries, not fresh: a fresh cranberry is mostly water and bursts in the oven

It is worth being firm about this, because fresh cranberries look like the more wholesome choice and they will quietly ruin the bake. A fresh cranberry is mostly water held inside a tough skin. In the oven the water turns to steam, the skin bursts, and the juice runs out into the surrounding oats — wet patches in a mixture whose setting depends on syrup, not on added liquid. The berries are also fiercely tart eaten raw, far sharper than the dried sort, because drying is always paired with sweetening. The result of using fresh berries is a bar that is soggy in streaks, slow to set, and sharper than anyone wanted.

This is the same trap that catches all wet fruit in flapjacks, and our guide to why fruit flapjacks turn soggy goes through it in detail. Dried cranberries avoid the problem completely: their water has already been removed, they are soft enough to eat straight from the packet, and in the oven they simply warm through, plump very slightly in the buttery mixture and keep their colour and shape. Everything the fresh berry does wrong, the dried one does right.

Ingredients

IngredientAmountNotes
Butter175gThe classic base, unchanged. Salted or unsalted both work
Golden syrup175gThe binder as well as a sweetener — weigh it into the pan with the butter
Light muscovado sugar175gThe classic's sugar; light brown sugar is the nearest swap
Porridge oats350gStandard porridge oats, not jumbo — jumbo oats bind less well
Dried cranberries150gThe sweetened baking kind. No soaking and no chopping needed
Orange zest (optional)Zest of 1 orangeFinely grated, stirred in with the cranberries. Zest only — not the juice
Fine sea saltA pinchKeeps the sweetness in check

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper and a large pan — oats and fruit together make a bulky mixture, and a big pan makes the stirring far easier. Check the cranberry packet when you buy: you want the soft, sweetened dried cranberries sold for baking and snacking, which is what nearly every supermarket sells. Unsweetened dried cranberries exist and are dramatically more tart; if those are what you have, the bars will be sharper and you may want to know that before the first bite rather than after.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides for lifting the slab out later.
  2. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado in a large pan over a low heat. Stir until everything has melted into one smooth liquid. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil — boiled syrup sets like toffee and makes hard bars.
  3. Add the oats, salt, cranberries and zest. Take the pan off the heat and tip in 350g porridge oats, a pinch of salt, 150g dried cranberries and the finely grated zest of 1 orange if you are using it. Stir until every oat is coated and shiny and the cranberries are scattered evenly rather than clumped in one corner.
  4. Press into the tin. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working well into the corners. As you level the top, press any cranberries sitting proud of the surface down into the mixture — berries left exposed to the oven's full heat can darken and toughen.
  5. Bake for 30–35 minutes. The top should be golden brown with the edges a shade darker, and the centre should look set but still feel slightly soft when pressed — it firms up as it cools. The cranberries do not change the timing or the colour of the bake; judge it exactly as you would the classic.
  6. Score while warm. About 5 minutes after the tin comes out, mark the portion lines with a table knife — 12 bars — while the top is soft enough not to crack.
  7. Cool completely in the tin. Leave the slab until it is cold and set before cutting; warm flapjack cuts raggedly and can fall apart. An hour at least, and longer does no harm.
  8. Lift out and cut. Use the paper overhang to lift the cold slab onto a board and cut through along the scored lines with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down.

Orange zest: the one extra that earns its place — zest yes, juice no

Cranberry and orange are a long-standing pairing, and in a flapjack the zest is worth adding. The zest of one orange, finely grated and stirred in with the cranberries, brings the sharp, aromatic top notes of the fruit without any liquid at all, and it lifts the whole bar in the same direction the cranberries do — brighter, less flatly sweet. Use a fine grater and take only the orange outer layer; the white pith underneath is bitter and adds nothing you want.

The juice is a different matter, and it should stay out. Orange juice is flavoured water, and adding it to the mixture works against the set in exactly the way fresh cranberries do — the same wet-fruit problem covered in the soggy guide linked above. If you want more orange flavour, the honest route is a second orange's worth of zest, not a squeeze of juice. Kept to zest, the recipe's liquid balance is untouched and the bake behaves like the classic it is based on.

What the berries do in the oven: they plump, soften and hold their colour

Dried fruit changes less in a flapjack than people expect. The cranberries go in chewy and come out chewy, slightly plumper for having sat in hot butter and syrup, with their deep red colour intact — which is why the cut bars look so good, all that plain golden oat flecked with red. They do not melt, burst or disappear, and they do not need protecting, beyond the pressing tip in the method for any berry left sitting on the very top surface. Sweetness-wise, the berries give back as good as they get: they take up some of the syrup's sweetness in the bake and return their own tartness, which is the whole point of choosing them.

Swapping the cranberries: what else works at the same 150g

Cranberries are the flavour here, but the slot they fill — 150g of dried fruit stirred through the classic base — takes other fruits without any other change to the recipe. The table below covers the realistic options. If you want a broader mixture rather than a single fruit, our fruit flapjack recipe uses a soaked mixed-fruit approach instead, which is a different bake with a softer result.

OptionAmountWhat you get
Dried cranberries150gThe recipe as written: sweet-tart, chewy, deep red flecks through every bar
Dried sour cherries150gThe closest swap — sharper and darker than cranberries, and usually pricier
Dried blueberries150gSofter and sweeter, with less bite; the bars taste fruitier but less bright
Raisins or sultanas150gThe familiar, gentler option — sweeter than cranberries and without the tart edge
Fresh cranberriesNot recommendedThey burst and leak water into the mixture; the bars turn soggy in streaks and very tart

Storing cranberry flapjacks: about 5 days in an airtight tin

Keep the cut bars in an airtight tin at room temperature with baking paper between the layers. They keep about 5 days; the cranberries stay chewy and the bars hold together well, because nothing in this recipe adds moisture to hurry the staling along. They freeze well too — wrap the bars individually, seal them in a bag or box and use within 3 months, defrosting at room temperature in the wrapping. There is more detail, including how freezing affects different flapjack types, in our guide to freezing flapjacks.

Questions people ask

You can, in the sense that nothing stops you — but go in expecting a different and worse result. Fresh cranberries are mostly water inside a tough skin; they burst in the oven, leak juice into the oats, and leave wet streaks that never quite set, while tasting far more tart than the dried sort. If fresh berries are all you have, the least-bad route is to cook them down first into a thick, dry compote and swirl a little through — but that is a different recipe. For this bake, dried is not a compromise; it is the right ingredient.

A short soak is reasonable if the berries are genuinely hard rather than just chewy: 10 minutes in warm water or orange juice softens them. The part that matters is what happens next — drain them well and pat them dry with kitchen paper before they go in, so the soak does not smuggle extra water into a mixture balanced without it. Most supermarket cranberries are soft enough to use straight from the packet and need none of this.

Not with the standard baking cranberries. The dried cranberries sold in supermarkets are sweetened as they are dried, so in the finished bar they read as sweet-tart rather than sharp — a bright edge against the butter and syrup, not a sour bite. The exception is unsweetened dried cranberries, which are sold in some health-food ranges and are dramatically more tart; if you use those, expect a much sharper bar, and that choice is better made knowingly than discovered at the first bite.

The bars would be slow to set, soft in the middle and prone to turning soggy as they sit. If the jar is what you have, save it for serving alongside cheese or cold meats, and buy the dried berries for baking.

Chopping is a job for bigger dried fruits, like apricots or large dates, where whole pieces would land in a few slices and leave the rest bare. With cranberries, the only preparation worth doing is picking over the handful for any stalky bits before they go into the pan.
