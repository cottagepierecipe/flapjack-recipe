Flapjacks for a Crowd: Scaling Ingredients and Tray Sizes

By Michael

A school fair, a team tea, a family gathering with forty mouths: flapjacks are the right bake for a crowd — sturdy, portable, happy without plates, and forgiving of being made ahead. The mistake is to treat the job as one giant flapjack. Crowd flapjacks are the standard recipe made several times over, in the right trays, and most of the planning is tray maths done before the oven goes on.

The short answer: Work out how many bars you need and divide by 12 — that is how many classic batches to make, since each batch fills one 20cm square tin and cuts into 12 bars. Scale the ingredients in straight multiples: a double batch is 350g each of butter, golden syrup and light muscovado sugar with 700g porridge oats; the table below goes up to ×4. But keep the baking shallow — several standard trays bake evenly, while one very deep tray darkens at the edges long before its centre sets. The temperature never changes: 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3).

Start From Bars, Not Batches

The first number is people, not ingredients. Count the mouths, decide on a bar each — flapjacks are rich, and at a mixed table one bar a head is usually the right allowance, with a few spare for the inevitable seconds — and you have your target. Thirty people means thirty-plus bars. Each classic batch, from our classic flapjack recipe, gives 12 bars of the usual size, so thirty bars is three batches with six to spare. Work in whole batches like this and the rest of the planning — trays, oven space, storage — falls out of one number.

Cutting is the other lever, and for crowds it is often the better one. A 20cm slab cut into 12 gives generous bars; the same slab cut into 24 half-bars gives twice the pieces, each a two-bite portion that suits a table where there are other things to eat, or a children's event where a full bar is a meal. Halving the cut doubles your count without a single extra gram of ingredients — but decide it before you bake, because the number of batches hangs on it, and 48 half-bars and 48 full bars are very different afternoons.

The scaling itself is the easy part — everything multiplies in straight lines, and the pinch of salt stays a generous pinch or two judged by eye. Shopping follows from the same table: a ×4 bake wants 700g of butter, which is three standard 250g blocks with a little left over, and well over a kilo of oats, so the big bag rather than the small one. What the table cannot show is the part that actually decides success, which is not how much mixture you make but how you spread it — and that is about depth, next.

Why One Very Deep Tray Lets You Down

Faced with a ×4 batch, the temptation is a single huge vessel: the roasting tin, the biggest tray in the house, everything in at once. Resist it. Flapjack mixture bakes from the outside in, and a deep layer insulates its own centre — the edges, exposed to the tin's heat, set and then keep darkening while the middle is still a soft, unset mass. By the time the centre firms up, the margins are hard and bitter, and the slab cuts into bars that are burnt at one end and soggy at the other. It is the commonest way crowd baking fails, and it fails after you have spent the most ingredients on it.

Shallow is the whole secret. The classic depth — the depth a single batch makes in its 20cm tin — is the depth that bakes evenly at 160°C in 30 to 35 minutes, edges and centre finishing together. Hold every tray to that depth and each one has the same, known, reliable bake. Our post on why flapjacks burn at the edges is really a post about depth and heat reaching a slab unevenly; crowd trays are where that goes wrong at scale.

Trays: More of Them, Standard, and Shallow

The straightforward arrangement is one 20cm square tin per batch, as many tins as batches — borrowed tins are fine, and a crowd bake is the one occasion where owning four matching tins stops looking eccentric. If your tins are larger, scale the mixture to the tray rather than the tin: a 20×30cm tray has half as much base area again as the 20cm square, so it takes a ×1.5 batch — 260g each of butter, syrup and sugar, 525g of oats — and gives 18 bars at the same depth. Base area is the only measure that matters; our guide to flapjack tin sizes works through the standard sizes.

Line every tray before any mixture is made, with parchment overhangs to lift by. Assembling trays one at a time while mixture waits is where crowd bakes get ragged; a row of lined tins on the worktop turns the middle of the job into a simple filling exercise. Weigh each tray's portion of mixture if you want the depths — and therefore the bakes — truly even: equal weights in equal tins cannot help but match.

Mixing in Bulk Without Tears

A ×4 batch means melting 700g of butter with 700g of syrup and 700g of sugar, and the first practical question is whether your largest pan holds it with room to spare — the mixture must not boil, and a pan filled to the brim boils over at the first nudge. If the pan is not up to it, melt in two lots. Nobody's crowd will ever know, and the baking is identical.

The second question is the mixing itself. One and a half kilos of oats in one bowl is a lot of stirring, and an under-stirred corner of the bowl is a tray of dry, crumbly bars later. Work in the biggest bowl you have, add the melted mixture to the oats in two or three goes, stirring between each, and keep going until no pale, dry oats remain visible anywhere — bulk mixing fails at the bottom of the bowl, so dig down and turn it over properly. Weighing the golden syrup rather than counting spoonfuls matters more at this scale, not less; our post on how much golden syrup goes in flapjacks explains the weighing habit that keeps big batches balanced.

Oven Time: Baking in Rounds

Four trays and one oven means a plan for the shelves. Most ovens bake two trays decently at once, one on each of two middle shelves; beyond that, crowding the oven chokes the airflow and the bakes come out uneven. The good news is that flapjacks queue gracefully. A tray of pressed mixture can wait on the worktop while the first trays bake — the mixture is patient at room temperature over the span of a baking session — and each finished tray frees its shelf for the next.

Work to a rhythm: tray one and two in, score them when they come out, tray three and four in while the first two cool, and cut the cooled slabs while the last ones finish. Each tray still gets its own 30 to 35 minutes at the same temperature, because each is at the standard depth — the crowd changes the schedule, never the bake. Keep a note of which tray went in when; at four trays, memory is the first casualty, and an unmarked tray is an overbaked one.

Cutting, Storing and Getting Ahead

Score each slab while it is warm, cut it when cold, and stack the cut bars with a sheet of baking parchment between the layers so the sugary surfaces do not weld together in storage. Cut size is a planning decision you made at the start — 12 to a tray, or 24 halves — and a crowd table usually rewards the smaller cut: more pieces taken, less waste left on plates.

The real crowd-baking luxury is how far ahead flapjacks let you work. Baked bars keep about five days in an airtight tin at room temperature, so a Saturday event can be baked for on Wednesday without any loss anyone can detect — our post on how to store flapjacks has the detail. Further ahead still, baked and cut bars freeze wrapped for up to three months; our guide to freezing flapjacks covers the wrapping and defrosting. Between the tin and the freezer, there is no crowd deadline a flapjack cannot be ready for early — which is more than can be said for the person organising everything else.

Questions people ask

If the flapjacks are one item among several on the table, cut each tray into 24 half-bars instead and two batches give you 48 pieces — usually plenty, and the cheaper afternoon.

It is a slower production line, not a worse one. Start earlier than feels necessary, keep a note of timings for each tray, and the last tray will be in the oven while you are stacking the first.
