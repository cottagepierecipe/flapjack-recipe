Which Oats Are Best for Flapjacks?

By Michael

Oats are not a background ingredient in a flapjack — they are the whole structure. The butter, syrup and sugar only hold the bar together if the oats drink them up and set around them, so the type of oat you tip out of the packet decides whether your bars come out soft and chewy, chunky and crumbly, or dense as cake. Supermarket shelves offer porridge oats, jumbo oats, instant oats and steel-cut oats, and they behave so differently that using the wrong one can undo a good recipe.

The short answer: Ordinary porridge oats are the best all-round choice for flapjacks: their medium, thin flakes absorb the melted butter and syrup quickly, bind firmly and bake into a soft, chewy bar. Jumbo oats give a chunkier, chewier texture but bind less readily. Instant oats turn pasty and cake-like, and steel-cut oats stay hard because they cannot absorb the syrup in baking time. A half-and-half mix of porridge and jumbo oats is the baker's compromise — body from the jumbo, binding from the porridge oats.

Porridge oats: the classic choice, and why they work

What British shops sell as porridge oats are rolled oats milled relatively fine: the whole oat grain is steamed, rolled flat and then cut or rolled again so the flakes are small and thin. That thinness is exactly what a flapjack needs. A thin flake has a large surface compared with its weight, so when it meets warm melted butter and syrup it starts absorbing at once. The flakes soften, swell slightly and pack tightly together in the tin, and as the bake cools the syrup sets around a dense mat of flakes. The result is the flapjack most people picture: a clean-cutting bar, soft and chewy, with no loose crumbs.

The classic flapjack recipe on this site is built on porridge oats — 350g of them to 175g each of butter, golden syrup and light brown sugar — and every recipe here assumes them unless it says otherwise. They are forgiving oats: a minute over or under in the oven does not undo them, and the mixture presses down easily into the corners of the tin. If you bake flapjacks only occasionally, buy porridge oats and you never have to think about this question again.

Jumbo oats: more chew, less binding

Jumbo oats are the same grain rolled whole, without the extra cutting — big, thick, handsome flakes. In a flapjack they keep their identity: the bars come out chunkier, with a distinct chew and a rustic, open texture that many people prefer. The trade-off is binding. A thick flake absorbs syrup more slowly and packs less tightly, leaving more gaps in the slab; jumbo-oat flapjacks are the ones that shed crumbs at the edges and sometimes break along a flake line when cut.

You can make jumbo oats behave with two small adjustments. First, let the mixed mixture stand in the pan for five minutes before it goes into the tin — the flakes start drinking up the syrup while they wait, and the mixture presses down more obediently. Second, press harder than you would for porridge oats, and cool the slab completely before cutting, because the set syrup is doing all the holding. Keep the quantities exactly as the recipe states: adding extra syrup to "glue" jumbo oats usually overshoots into softness. The easy flapjack recipe works well with jumbo oats treated this way, and the texture contrast is worth trying at least once.

Instant oats: the ones to keep for breakfast

Instant oats — the fine, dusty flakes in plain instant porridge and most flavoured porridge sachets — have been rolled thinner still and usually pre-cooked, so they collapse into porridge the moment hot liquid touches them. In a flapjack they absorb the butter and syrup almost too well: instead of distinct flakes in a syrupy set, you get a uniform paste that bakes into a dense, cakey slab with no chew at all. The bars cut cleanly, which is their only virtue, and taste oddly smooth and heavy.

Flavoured sachets are worse again, because they bring their own sugar, flavourings and sometimes milk powder, none of which you can account for in the recipe. If instant oats are all you have, the batch will still bake and still be edible — stir the mixture well, press it firmly, and expect a soft, close-textured bar rather than a flapjack with character. But do not judge a recipe by an instant-oat batch, and if a flapjack mixture ever looks like porridge in the pan before it is baked, the oats were too fine.

Steel-cut oats and groats: too hard for the tin

Steel-cut oats — also sold as pinhead oatmeal — are not rolled at all: the grain is chopped into small, hard pieces. Groats are one step further back, the whole unprocessed grain. Both are excellent for porridge given a long simmer, but a flapjack's 30 minutes in syrup is nowhere near enough to soften them. Bars made with steel-cut oats come out studded with hard, gritty pieces that stay unpleasantly crunchy no matter how long you bake, because the pieces absorb liquid far too slowly to cook through in the time the sugar allows before it over-colours.

The only way to use them is to pre-cook: simmer steel-cut oats in water until tender, drain them very thoroughly, and then treat them as part of the mixture, reducing nothing else at your peril — the added water has to come out again or the bars will be soft and steamy. Honestly, it is a project for using up a packet, not a route to a better flapjack. Keep steel-cut oats for the breakfast pan they are good at, and keep them out of the baking tin.

Mixing oats, and the half-and-half trick

Once you know how the types behave, mixing them stops being guesswork. Half porridge oats and half jumbo oats — 175g of each in the classic quantities — gives a bar with the best of both: the porridge flakes fill the gaps, absorb the syrup and bind the slab, while the jumbo flakes run through it giving chew and texture. It is the mix to try if plain porridge-oat flapjacks feel a touch smooth for your taste, or jumbo ones keep breaking on you — a common cause in why flapjacks fall apart.

One more option is blending down: if you have jumbo oats and a recipe that wants porridge oats, a few short pulses in a food processor will break some flakes down towards porridge size. Pulse, do not run — thirty seconds of continuous blending gives you oat flour and dust, and the bar drifts towards the instant-oat problem. Any blend still weighs the same: 350g of oats is 350g whatever the flake size, though jumbo-heavy mixtures look bulkier in the bowl and tin because the flakes pack loosely before they are pressed.

Reading the packet: names that mean the same thing

Packet wording causes more confusion than the oats themselves. "Rolled oats" is the general term for all flattened flakes — porridge oats and jumbo oats are both rolled oats, separated only by flake size, so a bag labelled simply "rolled oats" with medium flakes will behave like porridge oats in a recipe. "Oatmeal" in British shops usually means ground oats in grades (fine, medium, pinhead); medium oatmeal can stand in for part of the oats in a pinch, but pinhead is steel-cut by another name and has the same problem. And if you are baking for someone who needs gluten-free flapjacks, the oat type matters less than the certification: only oats sold as certified gluten-free are suitable, whatever the flake size, because ordinary oats are milled and transported alongside wheat. Flake size changes texture; the recipe quantities — and the syrup balance in how much golden syrup flapjacks need — stay the same across all of them.

Instead, let the mixed jumbo-oat mixture stand for five minutes so the thick flakes start absorbing, press it into the tin more firmly than porridge oats, and let the slab cool completely before cutting. The syrup already in the recipe is enough; it just needs longer to get into the flakes.

For the bake itself, there is no practical difference: flake size and freshness matter, branding does not. What does make a difference is old oats. Oats contain natural oils, and a bag that has sat open for months can taste stale or slightly rancid, which no amount of syrup will cover. Buy the size of bag you will use within a few months, store it sealed and dry, and choose by flake type rather than by label.

Five minutes in a dry frying pan over a medium heat, stirred until the flakes smell nutty, deepens the taste of any flapjack — jumbo oats benefit most, as their centres stay raw-tasting otherwise. Let them cool for a few minutes before they meet the melted butter so they do not seize the mixture. The baked result is the same texture with a warmer, more savoury edge.

Part-swaps work; full swaps are a different bake. Replacing a quarter of the oats with desiccated coconut, chopped nuts or seeds keeps the structure because the oats still do the binding. Cornflakes, rice crispies or granola in place of all the oats absorb syrup very differently and generally need their own recipes — granola in particular arrives already sweetened and oiled. Treat the oats as the load-bearing ingredient and decorate around them.

That floury, raw taste is the oats themselves, undercooked. It happens most with jumbo oats in a bake taken out too early, or with very fine instant oats that coated the tongue before they had properly baked through. The fixes are the ones in this post: porridge oats for reliability, the full 30–35 minutes at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3), and letting the slab cool completely — flavours settle and the raw edge disappears as the bar sets.
