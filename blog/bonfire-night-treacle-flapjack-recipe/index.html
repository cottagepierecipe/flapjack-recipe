Bonfire Night Treacle Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

Bonfire Night has its own flavours — black treacle and ginger, dark and warming, the taste of parkin and treacle toffee eaten outdoors with cold hands. This flapjack is built on them: part of the golden syrup swapped for black treacle, dark muscovado in place of light, and ground ginger through the mixture, so the classic bar comes out deep mahogany and properly dark. It is not parkin, and this post says so plainly — it is parkin's flapjack cousin.

The short answer: Melt 150g butter, 100g black treacle, 100g golden syrup and 150g dark muscovado sugar in a pan, stir in 350g porridge oats, 1 tsp ground ginger and a pinch of salt, press into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes (estimate). Makes 12 dark, gingery bars.

Not yet kitchen-tested: this recipe adapts our tested classic flapjack and has not been cooked by us yet. Michael tests every new recipe himself, and this page will be updated once he has cooked it. Times marked (estimate) are estimates.

Bonfire flavours: treacle, ginger and the dark end of the shelf

Every flapjack on this site starts from the same place — our classic flapjack recipe, with its equal weights of butter, golden syrup and light muscovado. This one walks away from it in a single direction: darker. Half the syrup becomes black treacle, the light muscovado becomes dark, the butter comes down a little to keep the balance, and a teaspoon of ground ginger goes through the oats. The result is a bar the colour of mahogany with a flavour to match — treacle's slight bitterness under the sweetness, ginger warming behind it. It is a flapjack designed for a particular night and a particular kind of eating: standing up, outdoors, in the cold, where a pale, delicate bake would be lost and a dark, strong one is exactly right.

The honesty note belongs here, at the top. The traditional Bonfire Night bake is parkin — a gingerbread made with oatmeal, treacle and ginger, baked days ahead and kept in a tin to go sticky. This flapjack is not parkin and does not claim to be: parkin is a cake, risen and sliced, and this is a flapjack, pressed and cut into bars. What they share is the flavour — treacle and ginger, the two tastes the night runs on — and the keeping quality, because this bar, like parkin, is at least as good on the second day as the first. Call it parkin's flapjack cousin and you have it about right. If ginger is your main interest, our ginger flapjack recipe takes the classic base in that direction without the treacle.

A practical word about the treacle before the method. Black treacle is one of the stickiest substances in the kitchen and it does not pour so much as commit. Weigh it directly into the pan with the butter — pan on the scales, tare, spoon it in — and warm the spoon under the hot tap if it refuses to let go. Anyone who has tried to weigh treacle into a bowl and then scrape the bowl into a pan has done it exactly once.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving overhang at two sides.
  2. Melt the butter, treacle, syrup and sugar. Put 150g butter, 100g black treacle, 100g golden syrup and 150g dark muscovado in a large pan over a low heat and stir until everything has melted into one dark, smooth liquid. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil — treacle scorches more readily than syrup, and scorched treacle is bitter in a way no amount of sugar fixes.
  3. Add the oats, ginger and salt. Take the pan off the heat, tip in 350g porridge oats, 1 tsp ground ginger and a pinch of salt, and stir until every oat is coated. The mixture will be much darker than the classic's — near-black in the pan, mahogany once baked.
  4. Press into the tin. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working well into the corners. A firm press matters as much here as in the classic; the binding is the same syrup set.
  5. Bake for 30–35 minutes. Bake for 30–35 minutes (estimate). Judge it by touch and by the edges rather than colour — the slab is dark from the start, so look for edges that have firmed and pulled very slightly from the paper, and a surface that looks set and matte rather than wet.
  6. Cool completely and cut into 12. Leave the slab to cool fully in the tin; the treacle set is firm once cold and soft while warm, and cutting early drags. Lift out on the paper and cut into 12 bars with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down.

Treacle: the dark heart of it

Black treacle is what gives this bake its character, and it is worth understanding what it brings. It is a dark, thick syrup from sugar refining, and unlike golden syrup — which is all light, buttery sweetness — treacle carries a bitter edge and a deep, almost smoky flavour alongside its sugar. That bitterness is the point. A flapjack is a sweet thing built on sweet things, and the treacle is the first ingredient in the recipe that argues back: it deepens the flavour, darkens the colour to mahogany, and keeps the bar from being simply sweet. It is also why the recipe keeps 100g of golden syrup beside it. All treacle and no syrup tips the balance over — the bars come out harsh and almost savoury-bitter. The half-and-half split gives you treacle's depth with syrup's kindness, and the dark muscovado, itself molasses-rich, backs the treacle up instead of fighting it.

Read the table as the recipe's reasoning. Every sweetener in the tin is pulling towards dark, and the one light element — the golden syrup — is there to stop the darkness becoming harshness. Change any single one and the bar still works; change two and you are on your way back to the classic with a treacle accent, which is a fine thing but a different post.

Ginger, and the family of dark bakes

The teaspoon of ground ginger is measured for warmth rather than fire. Treacle and ginger are old partners — gingerbread, parkin and this flapjack all run on the pairing — because ginger's heat sits exactly where treacle's bitterness sits, and the two make each other taste more like themselves. A teaspoon across 350g of oats gives a warmth you notice at the end of a mouthful; double it and the bar becomes a ginger flapjack with treacle in it, which is a legitimate bake but not this one. Fresh ginger is not the swap here — its bright, raw heat belongs to a different kind of baking, and the dried spice's rounded warmth is what the dark bakes in the photograph's family all use. This recipe sits in that family — gingerbread, parkin, treacle bakes — as the one member that is pressed in a tin and cut into bars rather than risen and sliced.

Serving on the night: a bar that travels

Flapjack is close to the ideal bonfire food, and this dark version most of all. It is baked ahead, it keeps, it travels in its tin, it is eaten with one hand — gloved, if it comes to that — while the other holds a sparkler or a mug of something hot, and it does not care about being cold; if anything the treacle set firms up pleasantly outdoors and the chew gets longer. Bake it the day before and the flavours will have settled into each other by the night itself. Cut the bars before you go, wrap them if they are leaving the tin, and expect them to be eaten standing up, which is what they were designed for. Few bakes survive being eaten next to a bonfire in the cold and come out ahead; this one was built for it.

Storing the treacle bars

The bars keep about 5 days in an airtight tin at room temperature, with baking paper between the layers, and like parkin they are arguably better on the second day, once the treacle and ginger have settled through the oats. They freeze well for up to 3 months, wrapped individually and sealed, and defrost at room temperature in their wrapping — a useful quality if you bake for the night and the night gets rained off. Keep the tin somewhere cool; the treacle set softens in warmth like any syrup set. The general rules are in our guide to how to store flapjacks and how long they last.

Questions people ask

You can, but the bars come out harsh. Black treacle carries a real bitter edge, and 200g of it with no syrup to round it gives a bar that tastes almost savoury-bitter — interesting in a small piece, too much in a full bar. The 100g of golden syrup in the recipe is there to keep the treacle's depth while taking its harshness off. If you like the dark end of the scale, try 125g treacle to 75g syrup before going all the way.

No — and the post does not pretend otherwise. Parkin is a risen gingerbread cake made with oatmeal, treacle and ginger, traditionally baked ahead and kept in a tin to go sticky. This is a flapjack: oats pressed in a tin and cut into bars, with no raising and no cake crumb. They share the Bonfire Night flavours — treacle and ginger — which makes this parkin's flapjack cousin rather than parkin itself.

Black treacle is a molasses — the dark syrup left from refining sugar — and the two pour and behave the same way in a recipe like this, which is why the molasses photograph in this post stands in for the treacle pour. Molasses sold under that name varies more: lighter grades are sweeter and milder, and blackstrap is more bitter and mineral than treacle. If you substitute, use a dark molasses and expect the flavour to land close; avoid blackstrap unless you like the bitter end.

A slight bitter edge is treacle being itself, but a properly bitter bar usually means the melt got too hot. Treacle scorches more readily than golden syrup, and once it catches it turns harsh in a way sugar cannot fix. Melt everything over a low heat, stir, and take the pan off as soon as the mixture is one smooth liquid — never let it boil. The other cause is proportion: if you increased the treacle beyond the recipe's 100g, the balance tips fast.

Yes, the bars will set and keep exactly the same — but you lose the warmth that makes this a bonfire bake rather than simply a dark one, and the treacle tastes flatter without it. If ginger is the issue because of heat, halve it to half a teaspoon rather than dropping it: at that level it reads as depth, not spice. Or make our classic recipe with the treacle swap alone and call it a treacle flapjack, which is an honest name for it.

They keep about 5 days in an airtight tin at room temperature, and like parkin they improve over the first day or two as the treacle and ginger settle — so baking two or three days ahead is not a compromise but arguably the best timing. For longer, freeze them wrapped individually for up to 3 months and defrost at room temperature in the wrapping on the day.
