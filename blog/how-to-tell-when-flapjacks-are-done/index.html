How to Tell When Flapjacks Are Done

By Michael

Flapjacks are the hardest bake in the tin to judge, because they lie. They look raw when they are done and look perfect shortly before they turn hard — and the reason is simple: a flapjack is not finished when it leaves the oven. It finishes setting as it cools, and the cues below are how to read it before that happens.

The short answer: Flapjacks are done when the edges are golden and look firm, the centre is a paler gold and just set with a slight wobble when the tin is nudged, and the bubbling on the surface has slowed right down. For the classic recipe that is 30 to 35 minutes at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Take them out looking slightly soft — they firm up in the tin as they cool, and that firming is part of the recipe, not a mistake.

Why the clock alone misleads: every oven and tin bakes differently

Recipe times are a window, not a deadline, and flapjacks sit in a wider window than most bakes. Your oven may run ten degrees hot or cool compared with its dial — a very common gap — and a fan oven dries and browns the surface faster than a conventional one at the same marked temperature. The tin changes things too: a dark metal tin absorbs more heat and browns the base and edges quickly, glass and ceramic are slower to heat through, and a mixture spread thinner than intended can be done five minutes before a deeper one has even set.

So treat the time on a recipe — 30 to 35 minutes for our classic flapjack recipe — as the point at which to start looking, not the moment to act. From about five minutes before the earliest time, judge the tin in front of you against the cues in this guide, not against the clock.

Read the colour first: golden edges, a paler centre

Colour is the most reliable cue you have, because the syrup mixture tells the truth as it cooks. An underdone slab looks pale and matte in the middle, with the surface still showing the light, raw colour of uncooked oats. As the bake progresses, the butter, syrup and sugar darken together: first the edges and corners take on a proper golden brown, then the colour creeps inward.

Done looks like this: the outer few centimetres are an even, appetising gold; the centre is a lighter gold, clearly cooked but a shade paler than the rim; and there are no pale, wet-looking patches anywhere. If the edges have moved past gold into dark brown while the centre is still pale, the oven is too hot or the tin is too small for the mixture — the bake is racing at the rim and crawling in the middle, and no amount of extra time will even it out. Note it for next time and take this batch out when the centre finally sets, accepting firmer edges.

The wobble test: nudge the tin and watch how the centre moves

Colour tells you about the surface; the wobble tells you about the middle, which is where underdone flapjacks hide. Open the oven, pull the shelf out a little way with an oven glove, and give the tin a short, gentle nudge. You are watching how the centre of the slab responds.

A done flapjack moves as one piece with a slight, slow wobble — the whole surface trembles faintly and settles. An underdone one ripples: the centre sloshes or waves independently of the edges, like the top of a just-set jelly, because the syrup in the middle is still properly liquid. If you see a ripple, close the door and give it three to five more minutes, then look again. Do not shake the tin hard, and do not nudge repeatedly — every long oven opening drops the temperature and stretches the bake out further.

Watch the bubbling: it slows right down as the syrup cooks

Midway through the bake, a flapjack's surface is busy: the syrup mixture bubbles steadily across the whole slab as water cooks out of the butter and the mixture thickens. Watch it through the oven door glass with the light on, without opening the door at all.

As the flapjacks approach done, the bubbling changes character. It retreats to the edges, grows slower and lazier, and the centre of the surface looks set and glossy rather than active — a few slow bubbles at the rim are normal and expected. A centre that is still bubbling briskly at the recipe's time is not finished, whatever the clock says. Conversely, if the bubbling has stopped everywhere and the surface looks dry and matte, you have gone past the chewy stage and are heading for crunchy; take the tin out immediately.

Touch the edge, not the centre: set with a little give

If colour and wobble leave you unsure, use touch — carefully, and in the right place. With a clean fingertip or the back of a teaspoon, press very lightly on the slab about two centimetres in from the edge. A done flapjack feels set there: the surface resists, springs back slightly, and your finger comes away clean. An underdone edge feels soft and sticky, and the mixture clings.

Do not judge by pressing the centre. The middle of a perfectly baked flapjack still feels soft while it is hot, because the syrup there is at its hottest and thinnest — it firms on cooling, like everything else in the tin. Bakers who poke the middle, decide it is raw, and add ten more minutes are the ones asking later why their flapjacks set like toffee. The centre's job is to look just set and wobble slightly.

Chewy or crunchy: your target changes the finish line

"Done" is not a single point for flapjacks — it is a short stretch of the bake, and where you stop inside it decides the texture. For chewy bars, take the tin out at the early end of the window: the edges golden, the centre only just set and still looking soft and glossy. The cooling set will firm them to a yielding chew. This is the finish our easy flapjack recipe aims for, and it is the version most people picture.

For crunchy bars, hold on for about five minutes more, until the centre has firmed, the wobble has almost gone and the colour has deepened a shade across the whole surface. Those extra minutes cook more water out of the syrup, and the bars set crisp and snappy. The mistake is drifting into that territory by accident — putting the tin back "just to be safe". Choose the texture before the tin goes in, and take it out at the matching cue.

Judge them cold: the cooling tin finishes the bake

The most important sentence in this guide is the one bakers refuse to believe: you cannot finally judge a flapjack until it is cold. The syrup bond only sets as the temperature falls, so a slab that feels worryingly soft at ten minutes out of the oven can be textbook after an hour or two cooling in its tin. Everything you do in the oven is about arriving at "slightly under, on purpose".

Cool them in the tin, on a rack, undisturbed — and note that flapjacks, unlike biscuits, are not tipped out onto a rack to cool, because the warm slab would break apart. If, once fully cold, the bars are genuinely too soft — sagging, sticky, falling apart when cut — the batch was underdone, and it is not necessarily lost: return the uncut slab, still in its tin, to a low oven for five to ten minutes, then cool it completely again before judging. The full rescue routine, and the other reasons bars refuse to set, are covered in our guide to why flapjacks are too soft or not setting. If they have cooled and set well, all that remains is to cut them cleanly and store them properly — our notes on how to store flapjacks and how long they last take it from there.

One finished bar tells you the truth: check your first cut

When the slab is cold and cut, look at the first bar properly before you serve or store the batch. A well-judged flapjack has an even, moist-looking crumb from top to bottom, holds together when picked up by one end, and bends very slightly — or not at all, for a crunchy batch — without snapping or shedding oats. Keep a note of what you find against the time you actually baked: if your oven needed 38 minutes to reach the cues the recipe promises at 30 to 35, write 38 on the recipe and start there next time. Three batches in, you will know your own oven's version of "done" — which was the real skill all along.

Questions people ask

No — leave the skewer in the drawer. A cake is done when a skewer comes out clean because cake batter sets into a crumb; flapjack mixture is oats bound in syrup, and hot syrup coats a skewer and comes out sticky-looking even when the bake is perfectly done. A skewer will tell you "raw" at every stage, and acting on it is one of the commonest routes to overbaked, rock-hard bars. Judge by colour, wobble and bubbling instead.

A probe is not the usual test for flapjacks, and it is less helpful than it sounds. The reading depends on whether the tip lands in a pocket of syrup, on the oats, or near the tin, so the same slab can give noticeably different figures a centimetre apart. There is no single target temperature that separates done from underdone for a mixture like this. An oven thermometer is the genuinely useful purchase: it tells you whether your dial is honest, which explains more mysterious bakes than any probe reading.

Two smaller contributors: a fan oven run at the conventional temperature, which bakes hotter than intended, and a dark tin, which pushes the edges and base ahead. The mixture is too deep in the middle or the oven is too hot. If the mixture was tipped in and levelled carelessly it can be a centimetre deeper at the centre than the edges, and that extra depth lags far behind. Spread and press the mixture to an even depth, right into the corners, before baking. If the depth was even, the oven is running hot: the rim races ahead and burns before the centre sets. Longer than patience suggests. The slab is handleable after about an hour at room temperature and can be scored and, carefully, cut — but the set keeps improving for several hours, and bars cut the next day are often the neatest of all. Do not judge the bake at twenty minutes and do not refrigerate the tin to hurry it: fridge-cold bars feel deceptively firm, then soften again as they warm, which tells you nothing about how they were baked.
