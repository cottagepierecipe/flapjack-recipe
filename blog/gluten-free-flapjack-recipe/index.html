Gluten-Free Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

A gluten-free flapjack does not need a different recipe — it needs the same recipe made with the right oats. This version keeps the tested classic exactly as it is: equal weights of butter, golden syrup and light brown sugar, twice the oats, one lined tin. The only change is that every ingredient is checked, and the porridge oats are certified gluten-free.

The short answer: Melt 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light brown sugar gently in a pan, stir in 350g certified gluten-free porridge oats and a pinch of salt, press into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes. Cool completely in the tin and cut into 12 bars. The oats are the deciding ingredient: ordinary porridge oats are usually contaminated with wheat, so only oats labelled certified gluten-free make this recipe gluten-free.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Gluten-free oats: naturally gluten-free, usually contaminated

Oats themselves do not contain gluten. The problem is everything that happens to them before they reach the bag. Oats are commonly grown in rotation with wheat, barley and rye, harvested with the same machinery, carried in the same lorries and milled in the same mills. By the time an ordinary bag of porridge oats reaches the shelf, it almost always carries traces of wheat — enough that it cannot be called gluten-free, and enough to matter to anyone who must avoid gluten completely, including people with coeliac disease.

Certified gluten-free oats are grown, harvested, transported and milled separately, and tested to confirm they meet the gluten-free standard. They look the same, cook the same and taste the same as ordinary porridge oats — which is precisely the point. In a recipe as simple as flapjack, the oats are nearly half the mixture by weight, so they are the one ingredient where the label really decides whether the finished bars are gluten-free or not. Our guide to which oats are best for flapjacks covers the types of oat in more detail; for this recipe, the type matters less than the certification.

Nothing else in the classic formula changes. Butter, golden syrup and light brown sugar are all naturally free of gluten as plain ingredients — but "plain ingredient" is not the same as a guaranteed product, because factory handling varies. The safe habit, and the one this recipe is built on, is to read the label of every single ingredient, every time, including the ones you have bought before. Manufacturers change suppliers and factories without changing the front of the packet.

You will also need a 20cm square tin and baking paper for lining. Nothing in the equipment list needs to be special, but the paper matters: use a fresh sheet, not one that lined a tray of wheat-flour biscuits earlier in the day. If you are baking for someone who must avoid every trace of gluten, wipe the tin and use clean utensils — see the cross-contact notes below the method.

The label check: what to look for on each packet

Reading labels for gluten is a skill that takes about a minute once you know what you are looking for. In the UK, cereals containing gluten — wheat, barley, rye and oats that are not certified — must be emphasised in the ingredients list of a product, and a "may contain" warning tells you the product was made somewhere those cereals are handled. For this recipe you are checking five packets, and the table below shows what each one needs to say.

ProductWhat you needWhy
Porridge oats"Gluten-free" or "certified gluten-free" on the packetOrdinary oats are routinely contaminated with wheat in farming and milling
Golden syrupNo gluten cereals in the ingredients; no "may contain wheat/gluten" warningPlain syrup is naturally gluten-free, but factory handling varies by brand
Butter and sugarPlain products, labels checkedFlavoured butters and sugar blends can carry extra ingredients

Dried fruit and seeds, if addingLabelled gluten-free, no "may contain" warningFruit and seeds are often packed in facilities that also handle wheat flour

If a packet carries a "may contain wheat" or "may contain gluten" warning, do not use it for this recipe — put it back and choose another brand. The warning is voluntary information from the manufacturer about their factory, and it is there to be acted on. This is plain label-checking, the same habit anyone shopping for a gluten-free household develops; it is not a medical judgement, and if you are cooking for someone with coeliac disease it is worth asking them which brands they trust, because they will know their own shopping list better than any recipe page can.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with a fresh sheet of baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides so you can lift the slab out later.
  2. Check every label before you open anything. Oats certified gluten-free; syrup, butter and sugar with no gluten cereals and no "may contain" warnings. It takes a minute now and cannot be fixed once the ingredients are in the pan.
  3. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light brown sugar in a large pan over a low heat. Stir as they melt. The mixture should become one smooth, glossy liquid — take it off the heat as soon as it does, and never let it boil, which splits the butter and makes greasy bars.
  4. Add the oats and salt. Tip in all 350g of the certified gluten-free oats and a pinch of salt. Stir steadily, turning the mixture over until every flake is coated and there is no dry oat left anywhere in the pan. This takes longer than you expect — a good full minute of stirring.
  5. Fold in any extras. If you are adding raisins, seeds or checked chocolate chips, stir them through now, while the mixture is warm and loose.
  6. Press the mixture into the tin. Spoon it into the lined tin and press it down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon or a spatula, working into the corners. A firmly pressed slab holds together and cuts cleanly; a loosely packed one crumbles.
  7. Bake for 30–35 minutes. The top should be evenly golden, the edges a shade darker and just beginning to pull away from the paper, and the centre should look set and feel firm with only the slightest give when pressed lightly.
  8. Cool completely in the tin. This is part of the recipe, not waiting time. The bars set as they cool — cut them warm and they will fall apart and feel greasy. Allow at least two hours; overnight is fine.
  9. Lift out and cut into 12. Use the paper overhang to lift the cold slab onto a board. Cut into 12 bars with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down rather than sawing, and wipe the blade between cuts for neat edges.

Cross-contact in a home kitchen: the plain, practical version

If the flapjacks are for someone who avoids gluten for preference, the label check above is the whole job. If they are for someone who must avoid gluten strictly, it is worth a few extra minutes of kitchen hygiene, because gluten travels on shared equipment just as flour dust does. None of this is complicated, and none of it needs special equipment.

Work on a wiped-down surface, and wash the pan, spoon and tin before you start if they have been used for flour-based baking. Use a fresh sheet of baking paper — paper that has already lined a tray of ordinary biscuits or a flour-dusted bake can carry traces. Keep a separate, washed butter knife or spoon for the butter if the household butter dish is regularly used for ordinary toast, because crumbs collect in shared butter. And store the finished bars in their own clean, airtight container, not in a tin shared with wheat biscuits. These are the same common-sense steps a gluten-free household uses every day; if you are baking for a guest, the kindest approach is to ask them how careful you need to be, and follow their lead.

Gluten-free extras: raisins, seeds and chocolate chips that work

The plain bar is the classic, but the mixture takes additions well, as long as each one passes the same label check. A handful of raisins or sultanas (about 75g) adds chew; sunflower or pumpkin seeds (about 50g) add crunch and make the bars feel more substantial; a teaspoon of ground cinnamon or the grated zest of an orange lifts the toffee flavour without changing the bake. Checked gluten-free chocolate chips — around 75g — melt into pockets through the bar; stir them in off the heat and work quickly, because the warm mixture will start to melt them on contact, which is no bad thing.

One variation to be careful with: oats labelled "gluten-free" are the base here, but do not be tempted to swap part of the oats for ordinary oat flour or a baking blend unless its label also says gluten-free, and never for wheat flour "just to firm it up". The recipe needs no flour at all — the syrup and sugar do the binding — which is one reason flapjack adapts to gluten-free baking more gracefully than most traybakes. If you want a dairy-free version as well, the vegan flapjack recipe on this site uses the same method with coconut oil in place of butter; make it with certified gluten-free oats and the two requirements are covered by one batch.

Storing gluten-free flapjacks: 5 days in their own tin

Once cut, keep the bars in a clean airtight tin at room temperature, with a sheet of baking paper between layers, and they keep as well as the classic — about 5 days before the edges start to dry. Keep the tin separate from wheat biscuits and crumbs; an airtight container protects the bars from cross-contact as much as from the air. For longer keeping, wrap bars individually, freeze them in a sealed bag or box, and they will keep for up to 3 months; let them defrost at room temperature in their wrapping. More detail on keeping and freezing is in our guide to how to store flapjacks. Texture-wise, bars made with certified gluten-free oats are indistinguishable from the ordinary classic — same chew, same golden edges — because the recipe never relied on gluten in the first place. The oats were only ever held together by butter, syrup and sugar, and they still are.
