Chewy vs Crunchy Flapjacks: What Makes the Difference?

By Michael

Two trays of flapjacks can come out of the same oven on the same day and eat completely differently — one soft and bendy, the other snapping at the edges. The difference is rarely luck. It comes down to a handful of levers you control: moisture, sugar, thickness and time.

The short answer: Chewy flapjacks hold on to moisture — condensed milk or plenty of golden syrup, a shorter bake and a proper rest all keep them soft and bendy. Crunchy flapjacks give moisture up — a longer bake, coarse demerara sugar, a thinner layer and more caramelisation dry them out until they snap. Most bakers sit somewhere between the two, and the classic on this site is deliberately in the middle.

Flapjack texture explained: it is the water that decides

A flapjack is really just porridge oats held together by a hot syrup of butter, sugar and golden syrup. When that syrup is wet and plentiful, it coats every oat and stays soft as it cools, so the finished bar bends and pulls — that is chew. When more of the water is driven off in the oven, the syrup sets harder, the sugar caramelises further and the edges dry out — that is crunch.

Nothing else in the recipe matters as much as this balance. The oats themselves are neutral: the same 350g of porridge oats can give you either texture. What changes is how much liquid goes in, how long the bake drives it back out, and how thick the layer is while it happens. A deep layer in a small tin traps steam in the middle; a thin layer spread over a bigger tin lets it escape.

Our tested classic flapjack — 175g butter, 175g golden syrup, 175g light brown sugar and 350g porridge oats, baked for 30 to 35 minutes in a lined 20cm tin — lands in the middle on purpose: firm enough to cut cleanly and carry in a lunchbox, soft enough in the centre to chew rather than crunch. Everything below is about pushing that same base one way or the other.

How to make flapjacks chewier: condensed milk, a shorter bake and patience

The most reliable route to a chewy bar is the one our chewy flapjack recipe uses: sweetened condensed milk. A few spoonfuls in place of some of the syrup change the set of the whole tray. Condensed milk is mostly milk solids and sugar with much of its water already removed, yet it sets soft and fudgy rather than hard — so the bars stay bendy for days instead of firming up overnight.

The second change is the bake. A chewy flapjack should come out of the oven looking very slightly underdone: pale gold at the edges, with the centre still soft enough to wobble faintly when you nudge the tin. It firms as it cools — that cooling set is part of the recipe, not a mistake. If your bars regularly come out harder than you wanted, our guide on why flapjacks turn out hard works through the causes one by one.

Third, rest them. Cut too soon and a chewy flapjack falls apart; left in the tin until properly cool, the syrup finishes setting around the oats and the bars slice cleanly while staying soft inside. Warmth is the enemy of a neat chewy cut — patience is the fix.

How to make flapjacks crunchier: demerara, more time and a thinner layer

Crunch is built the opposite way. Start with the sugar: demerara's coarse crystals do not melt as completely as soft brown sugar, so the baked syrup sets with a firmer, almost toffee-like bite. Granulated sugar works in the same direction. Light brown sugar and muscovado pull the other way — they are the chewy sugars.

Then give the tray more time. An extra five to ten minutes in the oven drives off more moisture and deepens the caramelisation, especially at the edges and corners, which is why the corner pieces of any tray are always the crunchiest. Spread the same mixture into a larger tin so the layer is thinner and the effect is stronger still — a greater proportion of every bar is edge.

Press firmly, too. A tightly packed layer of porridge oats has fewer air gaps, sets into a denser slab and slices with a cleaner snap. Just watch the colour as the bake lengthens: caramelised sugar tips from deep gold to burnt quickly, and a crunchy flapjack should be dark gold, never dark brown at the corners.

The last five minutes of the bake: where crunchy is won and lost

Most of the texture decision happens at the end of the bake, which is why two people following the same recipe get different results. The classic's 30 to 35 minutes at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) is a range, not a single number — and the position in that range is your texture dial. Out at 30 minutes, the bars set soft and chewy. Left to 35, they are firm. Given longer still, they shade into crunchy, and beyond that into simply hard.

Colour is the best guide. You are looking at the surface and the edges, not the clock: pale gold means chewy, even mid-gold means the balanced classic, deep gold at the edges means crunch is coming. Remember the tin holds heat and the bars carry on cooking for a few minutes after they leave the oven, so judge slightly ahead of where you want to end up. If your problem is the opposite — bars that never quite set — why flapjacks stay too soft covers the fix.

Resting, cooling and cutting: the texture is finished outside the oven

What you do after the bake matters more than most recipes admit. A flapjack left to cool in its tin keeps its steam, and the centre stays moist and chewy. The same tray turned out onto a rack after ten minutes dries at the base and edges and eats firmer. Neither is wrong — it is simply the last lever.

Pressing deserves a moment of its own, because it happens before the bake and decides how the bar holds together. Work the mixture into the corners and level it with the back of a spoon, pressing just firmly enough that there are no loose oats on top. A tighter press gives a denser, firmer bar; a lighter one gives a looser, more crumbly chew. If your bars crumble rather than bend, pressing and resting — not more syrup — are usually the answer.

Finally, cut at the right moment. Score the slab lightly while it is still warm and soft, then finish the cuts once it has cooled and set. Chewy bars cut cleanest when fully cool; crunchy bars are best cut before they go completely cold and hard, or the knife shatters the edges instead of slicing them.

Tin size is a texture decision: the same mixture, two different bars

One variable hides in plain sight in most recipes: the tin. The classic specifies a lined 20cm tin, and at that size the mixture sits deep enough to steam gently in the middle while the top dries — the balanced result the recipe was tested for. Spread the identical mixture across a 23cm tin and the layer thins by roughly a quarter; more of every bar is surface and edge, more moisture escapes, and the tray comes out noticeably firmer and crunchier without a single ingredient changing.

Go the other way — a smaller tin or a double batch in the same one — and the deep centre stays soft long after the top is golden, which is how you get a tray that is crunchy at the rim and almost spoonable in the middle. Neither extreme is a failure; both are the levers working exactly as described. But it does mean that if you change tin size, you have also changed the texture, and you may need to shorten or lengthen the bake by a few minutes to land where you intended.

Day one, day three, day five: how texture drifts as flapjacks keep

Texture is not fixed at the moment of cutting. A crunchy flapjack draws moisture from the air and from its own syrup and softens slowly in the tin; a chewy flapjack firms as the syrup completes its set and the oats absorb what liquid they can. The two styles drift towards each other over the keeping week, which is why a crunchy batch is at its best in the first two or three days and a chewy batch — unusually — can be better on day two than on day one.

Storage slows the drift but cannot stop it. An airtight tin at room temperature is the right home for both styles; keep the styles in separate tins, because side by side they trade moisture and meet in the middle faster. The classic, kept airtight, holds its tested balance for about a week — soft-centred, firm-edged, and recognisably the same bar from Monday to Friday.

Questions people ask

Flapjacks firm up as they sit. The syrup continues to set and the oats absorb moisture, so a tray that bends on day one can be noticeably firmer by day three. An airtight tin slows this down; a chewy batch eaten within a couple of days stays closest to how it left the oven.

Not in a single bake — the whole tray shares one syrup, one thickness and one bake time. The closest trick is to cut the corner and edge pieces for the crunch lovers and the centre pieces for everyone else, because the edges always bake firmer than the middle.

It pushes strongly that way, yes — condensed milk sets soft and fudgy rather than hard. But a long bake can still dry a condensed-milk tray out, so keep the bake at the shorter end and take the tin out while the centre still looks slightly soft.

Jumbo oats. Their larger flakes hold their shape and give a loose, distinct chew. Porridge oats are finer, pack down more densely and set into a firmer, more uniform bar — better when you are aiming for crunch or a clean snap.

Ten seconds in the microwave softens a hard bar for eating straight away. For the whole tray, a slice of bread in the storage tin overnight gives up some of its moisture to the bars. Next time, shorten the bake and check the colour rather than the clock.
