Cherry and Dark Chocolate Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

A cherry and dark chocolate flapjack is the Black Forest idea — cherries and dark chocolate together — folded through a buttery oat bar, with no cream and no cake in sight. The cherries are dried, not fresh, and that is the decision the whole recipe hangs on: dried cherries bake into chewy, jammy pockets that hold their shape, while fresh cherries leak juice into the mixture and turn the crumb soggy around every fruit.

The short answer: Melt 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado sugar in a pan, stir in 325g porridge oats, 100g dried cherries (chopped if large) and a pinch of salt, fold through 100g dark chocolate chunks or chips, press into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes. Cool completely in the tin and cut into 12 bars.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Cherry and dark chocolate flapjacks: the Black Forest idea without the cream

Black Forest gateau built its reputation on a sound combination: dark chocolate, cherries, and something rich to carry them. Strip away the cream and the sponge and the combination still works, because the real pairing is between the chocolate's bitterness and the cherry's sharp, fruity sweetness. A flapjack turns out to be a good vehicle for it. The base — our classic flapjack recipe, with the oats trimmed to 325g to make room for the fruit and chocolate — is sweet and buttery, the cherries cut through it in chewy bursts, and the dark chocolate keeps the whole bar from tipping into jammy sweetness. There is no kirsch and no cream; if you have made our cherry flapjack recipe, this is the same fruit with the chocolate turned right up.

One thing the pairing needs is balance in the chocolate. Milk chocolate against dried cherries and a syrup base makes a bar that is sweet in three directions at once. Dark chocolate at around 70% cocoa brings the bitterness that makes the cherries taste more of cherry. Use chunks chopped from a bar or chips, whichever you have — chunks give bigger, uneven pockets; chips distribute more evenly. Both are folded in at the end, off the heat, so they soften in the oven rather than melting into the mixture in the pan.

Dried cherries, not fresh: fresh fruit leaks and turns the bake soggy

This is the rule that decides whether the recipe works. Fresh cherries are mostly water. Baked into a flapjack, they burst, leak juice into the surrounding crumb and leave wet, soft patches that never properly set — and the finished bars spoil faster, because those wet patches are where staleness and mould start. Our guide to why fruit flapjacks turn soggy explains the mechanics in full: fresh fruit releases its juice as it heats, and an oat mixture has nowhere to put that juice except into itself.

Dried cherries behave completely differently. The drying has already taken the water out, so instead of leaking they absorb a little of the syrup around them and bake into soft, chewy, concentrated pockets — more cherry flavour per bite than fresh fruit would give, with none of the moisture problem. Buy the unsweetened or lightly sweetened kind if you can; some dried cherries are sold heavily sugared, which pushes an already sweet bar further. If your cherries are large, chop them in half or into rough pieces before they go in — whole dried cherries are big enough to break a bar's structure where they sit, and chopping spreads the flavour further. There is no need to soak them; they soften perfectly well in the bake.

Ingredients

IngredientAmountNotes

Light muscovado sugar175gLight brown sugar is the nearest swap
Porridge oats325gReduced from the classic 350g to make room for the fruit and chocolate
Dried cherries100gChop them if they are large. Unsweetened if you can get them
Dark chocolate100gChunks chopped from a bar, or chips. Around 70% cocoa
Fine sea saltA pinchSharpens the cherry flavour as well as the chocolate

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides, a large pan for the mixture, and a board and knife for chopping the cherries and, if you are using a bar, the chocolate.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven, line the tin and chop. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides. Chop the dried cherries if they are large, and chop the chocolate into rough chunks if you are using a bar rather than chips.
  2. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado in a large pan over a low heat. Stir until everything has melted into one smooth liquid. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil — boiled syrup sets hard and makes hard bars.
  3. Add the oats, cherries and salt. Take the pan off the heat and tip in 325g porridge oats, 100g dried cherries and a pinch of salt. Stir until every oat is coated and shiny and the cherries are spread through the mixture rather than clumped together.
  4. Rest the mixture, then fold in the chocolate. Leave the pan off the heat for a minute or two, then fold in 100g dark chocolate chunks or chips with a few turns of the spoon. The short rest keeps the chocolate as pockets instead of letting it melt and streak.
  5. Press into the tin. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working well into the corners. Firm pressing is what holds the bars together once cut.
  6. Bake for 30–35 minutes. The top should be golden with the edges a shade darker, and the centre should look set but still feel slightly soft when pressed — it firms up as it cools. The cherries at the surface may darken a little at the edges; that is caramelisation, not burning.
  7. Cool completely in the tin. Leave the slab in the tin until it is fully cold. The chocolate pockets need to set again before the bars will cut cleanly.
  8. Lift out and cut into 12. Use the paper overhang to lift the cold slab onto a board and cut it into 12 bars with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down.

Glacé cherries and other swaps: what works in place of dried cherries

Dried cherries are the right fruit for this recipe, but they are not always the easiest to find, and glacé cherries — the bright red ones sold for cake decorating — are the swap people reach for. They work, with a caveat about sweetness. The table below compares the realistic options honestly, including the one that does not work.

Serving cherry and dark chocolate flapjacks

These bars are rich enough to serve as a pudding in their own right — warm, with a spoonful of crème fraîche or a scoop of vanilla ice cream, they are as close to Black Forest as a flapjack gets. At room temperature they pack and travel like any other flapjack, and the flavour pairing holds up well with coffee. If you are baking for a mixed crowd, they sit happily alongside the plainer bars on our chocolate flapjack recipe page — the cherry version is the one that goes first.

Storing cherry and dark chocolate flapjacks: about 5 days in an airtight tin

Keep the cut bars in an airtight tin at room temperature. Because the fruit is dried rather than fresh, they keep as well as any other flapjack — about 5 days, with the cherries staying chewy throughout. The bars also freeze well for up to 3 months, wrapped individually and sealed; defrost in the wrapping at room temperature. The general rules are in our guide to how to store flapjacks and how long they last.
