Fruit and Nut Flapjacks: Combinations and Ratios That Work

By Michael

Fruit and nuts are the oldest things anyone has ever stirred into a flapjack, and the easiest to get wrong — not because the idea is complicated, but because the amounts matter. Too little and the additions vanish into the oats; too much and the slab stops holding together, because every handful of fruit and nuts is a handful of oats that is not there to bind. This guide gives the working ratio, the preparation that makes each addition behave, and the combinations that earn their place in the tin.

The short answer: Work to a total of about 150–200g of fruit and nuts combined for every 350g of porridge oats in our classic flapjack recipe. Chop nuts to roughly pea size so they spread through the slab instead of breaking it, soak hard dried fruit such as dried apricots and figs for 10 minutes in hot water and drain it well, and fold everything through the mixture before pressing. Apricot with almond, cherry with walnut, date with pecan and cranberry with cashew are the combinations that work hardest.

Start with the ratio: how much a flapjack can carry

A flapjack holds together because melted butter, syrup and sugar coat the oats and set around them as the slab cools. Fruit and nuts do not bind anything — they are passengers. Every gram of them replaces a gram of oat that would have done binding work, so there is a ceiling on how much the mixture can carry before the bars start to crumble at the cut edges and snap at the additions instead of through the oats.

That ceiling, for the classic quantities, is about 150–200g of fruit and nuts in total against 350g of porridge oats. At 150g the additions are generous but the slab still cuts like a plain flapjack. At 200g the bars are densely studded and noticeably richer, and the pressing and cooling matter more. Beyond 200g you are no longer really making a flapjack with additions; you are making a fruit and nut slice held together with oats, and it will behave like one — fragile, and better eaten from a plate. If you want to go that far, take some oats out deliberately rather than piling additions on top of the full amount, and accept the different bake for what it is.

Within the total, the split between fruit and nuts is flexible. A half-and-half split — say 100g of dried fruit and 75g of nuts — gives chew from the fruit and crunch from the nuts in most bites. Fruit-heavy mixes eat sweeter and softer, because dried fruit brings sugar and moisture with it. Nut-heavy mixes eat drier and more savoury, and they keep a little better, because nuts do not draw moisture the way fruit does. There is no wrong split inside the ratio; there is only the bar you prefer.

Choosing the nuts: chop them to pea size

The single most useful thing you can do for a nut flapjack is chop the nuts properly. Whole nuts and big halves are too large for a bar that is only about 2cm thick: they poke out of the surface, they make the knife skid when you cut, and each one is a fault line the bar can break along. Chopped to roughly pea size — pieces about 5–8mm — the same nuts spread through the whole slab, so every bite has some, and the oats can still pack around them and bind.

As for which nuts: almonds are the mild all-rounder, happy raw or toasted, and they pair with almost any dried fruit. Walnuts bring a slight bitterness that stands up well to sweet fruit, and they are soft enough to chop without a fight. Pecans are richer and more buttery, and they toast quickly — watch them. Cashews are the sweetest and creamiest of the common nuts, and they soften a little in the bake, which suits cranberry's sharpness. Hazelnuts want their skins rubbed off after a brief toast if you can be bothered; the flavour difference is real. Peanuts are the budget option and a good one, though they push the bar towards a peanut identity of its own. Pistachios are the expensive flourish — beautiful against cranberry or apricot, and best kept for a batch you are showing off.

Toast or not? Toasting chopped nuts in a dry frying pan for a few minutes, until they smell nutty, deepens their flavour and dries their surface so they stay crisper in the finished bar. It is worth doing for walnuts, hazelnuts and pecans, whose raw flavour can be flat. It matters less for almonds and cashews. Either way, let toasted nuts cool before they meet the melted butter, and never leave a pan of nuts toasting while you answer the door — they go from golden to burnt in under a minute.

The dried fruit: soak the hard ones, leave the soft ones

Dried fruit behaves in two camps. Raisins, sultanas and currants are soft enough to go in as they are. Dried apricots, figs, prunes, dried apple and dried mango are denser and drier, and in the oven they do something unhelpful: they pull moisture out of the mixture around them while they bake, then sit in the finished bar as hard, chewy lumps in a drier crumb. The fix is simple and it is the same one used in our fruit flapjack recipe: soak the hard fruit in hot water for about 10 minutes, then drain it thoroughly and pat it dry. It goes into the mixture plump, gives up nothing, and eats soft.

Draining matters as much as soaking. Fruit that goes in dripping takes its soak water with it, and that extra liquid steams in the oven and softens the set. Shake the colander, spread the fruit on kitchen paper for a minute, and then weigh or measure it — soaked weight is the weight to count towards your 150–200g total, because that is what is actually in the tin.

One more fruit behaves differently again: glacé cherries and dried cherries are already soft and moist, and need no soaking, but they are sweet enough that a little goes a long way — 75–100g is plenty against 350g of oats. Cranberries are the opposite problem: dried cranberries are sharp and usually sweetened, and they are best used as the accent in a mix rather than the whole fruit portion. A handful of cranberries with cashews or pistachios lifts a whole tray; a full 175g of cranberry makes a bar that fights back.

Big dried fruit needs cutting down to size

Whole dried apricot halves, whole figs and whole prunes have the same problem as whole nuts: they are bigger than the bar is thick. Cut dried apricots into strips or dice of about 1cm, quarter figs, and chop dates and prunes to raisin-plus size. Small, even pieces spread the fruit's sweetness and chew through every bar instead of concentrating it in a few bites, and the slab cuts cleanly because the knife is never asked to saw through a whole apricot.

Sticky fruit fights the knife as you chop it. Dates in particular glue themselves to the blade. A pair of kitchen scissors is often quicker than a knife for apricots and figs, and a light dusting of the chopped fruit with a teaspoon of the recipe's own oats stops the pieces clumping back together in the bowl. That clumping matters: a lump of six dates stuck together bakes as one dense, oversweet pocket, while the same dates separated disappear pleasantly through the bar.

Fresh fruit, for the record, is a different subject. Apple and pear bring so much water that they change the bake entirely, which is why fresh-fruit flapjacks have their own recipes on this site rather than a line in this guide. Everything here is about dried fruit and nuts — the storecupboard version, which keeps, travels and slices like a flapjack should.

Combinations that work, and what each one does

These are the combinations we would reach for first, all inside the 150–200g total for a 350g-oat batch. The pattern to notice is contrast: something sweet against something bitter or sharp, something chewy against something crunchy. Two sweet, soft additions together — say dates and raisins with no nut at all — make a bar that is pleasant but one-note.

Folding them in, pressing, and the pieces that scorch

Add fruit and nuts at the very end of mixing, once every oat is already coated in the melted butter and syrup. Stirred in earlier, the additions get in the way of coating the oats, and coating the oats is what binds the bar. Fold them through with a firm hand until the mix looks even — then look again, because fruit sinks and nuts rise, and a second fold from the bottom of the pan is what evens them out.

Press the mixture into the lined tin firmly and evenly, as always, but pay attention to the surface. Nuts and fruit sitting proud on top are exposed to the oven's direct heat for the whole bake, and dried fruit in particular scorches at the edges of the tin — raisins on the surface can catch and turn bitter in the last five minutes. The tidy solution is to press most of the mixture in, scatter a small handful of additions over the top if you want the look, and then press once more so they sit half-sunk in the surface rather than on it. They will colour, which is attractive, without burning.

Cool the slab completely in the tin before cutting — fruit and nut bars need the full set even more than plain ones, because the knife has more to travel through. Cut with a large knife pressed straight down, not sawn, and if a particular bar's edge crumbles, it is telling you the additions were at the top of the ratio rather than that anything went wrong.

Storing fruit and nut flapjacks

The bars keep about 5 days in an airtight tin at room temperature, with baking paper between the layers. Two honest qualifications: nuts stale faster than the flapjack itself does, so a nut-heavy batch is at its best in the first three days, and fruit-heavy batches stay softer and moister, so they are the ones to keep away from a warm kitchen. For longer keeping, the bars freeze well for up to 3 months, wrapped individually — though toasted nuts lose a little of their crispness in the freezer, so if you are baking specifically to freeze, add the nuts untoasted. The full rules are in our guide to how to store flapjacks and how long they last.

Questions people ask

Two checks before it goes in: pick out anything whole and large and chop it to pea size, and taste a little, because some mixes are salted or sweetened, which changes the bar. If the mix is heavy on one ingredient you do not love, it will be heavy in the bars too.

Almost always the ratio: more than about 200g of additions against 350g of oats leaves too little coated oat to bind the slab. The other usual causes are whole or halved nuts acting as fault lines, and cutting before the slab had cooled completely. Chop to pea size, keep the total inside the ratio, press firmly and cool fully, and the same recipe will hold. Our post on why flapjacks fall apart works through the other causes.

Raisins, sultanas, currants, dried cherries and cranberries are soft enough to go in as they are. Skipping the soak is the commonest reason an apricot flapjack disappoints.

Check the dried fruit too — some is packed in facilities that handle nuts, which matters for a serious allergy even though the fruit itself is nut-free.

Either works. Raw nuts give you control — chop them, then toast them yourself in a dry pan for the deepest flavour, or leave them raw for a milder one. Ready-roasted nuts save the step but are often salted, and salted nuts in a sweet bar are a seasoning decision, not a disaster: if you use them, leave the pinch of salt out of the base recipe. Unsalted is the safer default for baking.

You can, and cherry with walnut and a handful of dark chocolate chips is a genuinely good bar. Count the chocolate inside the same budget: keep the total of fruit, nuts and chocolate to about 200g against 350g of oats, or the binding suffers.
