Gingerbread Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

Gingerbread is not just ginger turned up loud — it is a darker, deeper flavour built from black treacle, dark muscovado sugar and a blend of warm spices, and it sits beautifully on a flapjack. This is the Christmas-season version of our classic: the same oats, the same method, but a bake that comes out dark, soft-set and tasting of the inside of a gingerbread house.

The short answer: Melt 175g butter with 100g golden syrup and 75g black treacle, then 175g dark muscovado sugar. Stir in 2 teaspoons ground ginger, 1 teaspoon ground cinnamon and ½ teaspoon mixed spice, then 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt. Press into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes. The set is darker and softer than the classic — cool completely before cutting into 12 bars.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

What makes a flapjack taste of gingerbread?

Three things, and ginger is only one of them. The first is treacle: part of the golden syrup in the classic gives way to black treacle, which brings a deep, almost liquorice-like bitterness that golden syrup alone never has. The second is dark muscovado sugar in place of the classic's light muscovado — same weight, but with far more molasses character, so the sweetness tastes heavy and fudgy rather than bright. The third is the spice blend: ginger still leads, but cinnamon and mixed spice sit behind it, and it is that background chorus that your mouth reads as "gingerbread" rather than merely "ginger". Leave any one of the three out and you have a pleasant dark ginger flapjack; all three together are what make this recipe itself.

The base, as with everything on this site, is our classic flapjack recipe — 175g butter, 175g of syrupy binder, 175g of sugar, 350g porridge oats, a 20cm tin. The swaps happen inside that frame, weight for weight, so the mixture presses, bakes and binds the way the classic does. If you want the brighter, simpler spiced version instead — light sugar, all golden syrup, ginger in two forms — that is our ginger flapjack recipe, and the two make an instructive pair: same bones, completely different mood.

Treacle and dark muscovado: the deep end of the sugar shelf

Golden syrup is a kind ingredient — sweet, mild, golden. Black treacle is its opposite number: nearly black, thick enough to coat a spoon and stay there, and tasting of molasses with a genuine bitter edge. Used on its own in a flapjack it can be too much — harsh, and heavy enough to flatten the oats' flavour — which is why this recipe splits the binder: 100g golden syrup to keep the familiar flapjack sweetness and the reliable set, 75g black treacle to pull the whole thing dark. The treacle also colours the bake dramatically; a gingerbread flapjack comes out of the oven a deep mahogany, several shades darker than the classic, and that is the treacle and dark sugar, not overbaking.

Dark muscovado earns its place the same way. Light muscovado, the classic's sugar, has a gentle caramel note; dark muscovado is the same sugar with much more of the molasses left in, and it tastes correspondingly deeper, damper and more fudgy. It is often lumpy straight from the packet, so break the lumps up as it melts. Because both the treacle and the dark sugar hold more moisture than the classic's ingredients, expect the finished bars to set softer, which the method below allows for.

The spice blend: ginger first, the others behind

Gingerbread spicing is a hierarchy, not a free-for-all. Ginger leads — 2 teaspoons, as in our ginger flapjack — because without its warmth up front the bake reads as generic "spiced". Cinnamon comes next at 1 teaspoon: it is sweet and woody rather than hot, and it rounds the ginger's edge. Mixed spice finishes at ½ teaspoon; a standard British mixed spice is itself a blend, usually built on cinnamon with allspice, nutmeg and smaller amounts of clove, ginger and coriander, and that trace of clove and nutmeg is a large part of what makes the flavour specifically gingerbread rather than generally wintry. Half a teaspoon is deliberately modest — mixed spice is potent, and too much tips the bars towards tasting of a scented candle.

SpiceAmountIts job in the blend
Ground ginger2 tspThe lead flavour and the warmth — gingerbread without ginger up front is just spiced treacle
Ground cinnamon1 tspSweetness and woodiness; softens the ginger's edge and makes the blend taste baked rather than raw
Mixed spice½ tspThe background: allspice, nutmeg and a whisper of clove. This is the note that says "gingerbread" specifically

One practical point: stir the spices into the melted butter and syrup before the oats go in, exactly as you would with the ginger in the plainer recipe. Dry spice tipped onto dry oats coats unevenly and can leave bitter little pockets; spice whisked through hot fat and syrup disperses through the whole batch.

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper and a large pan. A word on weighing the treacle: it is the stickiest ingredient in the kitchen, so weigh the golden syrup into the pan first, then spoon the treacle on top — the syrup layer stops most of the treacle sticking to the spoon, and what clings to the tin's measuring spoon can be scraped into the pan with the spatula you stir with.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, with a little overhang at two sides for lifting the slab out later.
  2. Melt the butter, syrup, treacle and sugar. Put 175g butter, 100g golden syrup and 75g black treacle in a large pan over a low heat, then add 175g dark muscovado, breaking up its lumps with the spoon. Stir until everything has melted into one dark, smooth liquid. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil.
  3. Stir in the spices. Take the pan off the heat and stir in 2 teaspoons ground ginger, 1 teaspoon ground cinnamon and ½ teaspoon mixed spice until they vanish evenly into the dark mixture — it will smell unmistakably of gingerbread at this point.
  4. Add the oats and salt. Tip in 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt. Stir until every oat is coated and dark and shiny, with no dry, pale patches left at the bottom of the pan.
  5. Press into the tin. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working into the corners. This dark mixture is a little stickier than the classic; a spoon dipped in hot water stops it dragging.
  6. Bake for 30–35 minutes. Judge by feel and edges rather than colour — the whole slab is dark from the start. The edges should look set and be pulling very slightly away from the paper, the surface should look matte rather than wet, and the centre should feel firm with a definite softness underneath. It finishes setting as it cools.
  7. Score while warm. About 5 minutes after the tin comes out, mark out 12 bars with a table knife while the top is still soft enough to take the lines without cracking.
  8. Cool completely in the tin. This is the step not to rush. A gingerbread flapjack is soft-set by design and cuts raggedly while warm; leave it until fully cold — two hours at least, and overnight is better still — before cutting through.
  9. Lift out and cut. Lift the cold slab out by the paper overhang and cut along the scored lines with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down and wiping the blade between cuts, because the treacle makes the cut edges tacky.

A darker, softer set: what to expect

Everything about this bake is a shade softer and darker than the classic, and it helps to know that in advance so you do not "rescue" a bar that is actually right. The colour is deep mahogany from the moment it is mixed; there is no golden stage to watch for. The set is fudgy rather than crisp — the treacle and dark muscovado hold moisture and never firm up to the classic's snap — so the bars bend a little rather than break. If you like a firmer bite, bake at the longer end of the time and cool overnight before cutting; if a first batch comes out softer than you want, the fix next time is five more minutes in the oven, not more oats, which would unbalance the ratio the classic is built on.

Storing gingerbread flapjacks through the season

Keep the cut bars in an airtight tin at room temperature, with baking paper between the layers — the tacky cut edges will stick to each other without it. They keep about 5 days at their best; the spices meld and deepen over the first two days, and the treacle keeps the bars moist rather than drying at the edges. They freeze very well, which makes them a sensible bake to make ahead in December: wrap the bars individually, seal in a bag or box, and use within 3 months, defrosting at room temperature in the wrapping. Questions people ask

Depth of flavour. Our ginger flapjack keeps the classic's light muscovado and all-golden-syrup base and adds ginger in two forms — it tastes bright and warm. The gingerbread version changes the base itself: dark muscovado, black treacle for part of the syrup, and cinnamon and mixed spice behind the ginger. Same tin, same method, same oat ratio — but the gingerbread bars are darker, softer and more mellow.

The treacle's dark, slightly bitter molasses flavour is the backbone of the whole style. Without it, and with light sugar, you have essentially made the spiced ginger flapjack. If treacle is the problem because of its stickiness rather than its flavour, warm the spoon in hot water first and weigh it straight into the pan over the syrup.

Probably because they are meant to be — up to a point. Treacle and dark muscovado hold more moisture than the classic's syrup and light sugar, so this bake sets fudgy and soft rather than crisp. The usual real causes of a too-soft result are cutting before the slab was completely cold, or bringing the bake out early because the dark colour fooled you into thinking it was done. Judge by touch and the set edges, give it the full time, and cool it overnight before judging the set.

Light muscovado works and the bars will still be good, but paler and less deep — you lose part of the molasses character that dark muscovado and treacle build together, so the result sits somewhere between the ginger and gingerbread versions. Golden caster sugar is a step further away: it has no molasses at all, and the flavour will be noticeably thinner. If dark muscovado is what is missing from the cupboard, light muscovado plus the full treacle quantity is the closest honest substitute.

Frozen, comfortably: bake them whenever you like in the run-up, wrap the cut bars individually, seal and freeze for up to 3 months — they defrost at room temperature in a couple of hours still in their wrapping. Unfrozen, bake within about 5 days of eating; the spices actually improve over the first two of those days. A tin posted to someone should be baked, cooled completely and wrapped the same day it travels.

A thin lemon icing — icing sugar worked to a drizzling consistency with lemon juice — is the traditional partner for dark spiced bakes and cuts through the treacle's richness; drizzle it over the cold, uncut slab and let it set before cutting, so the lines stay clean. Avoid thick buttercream or chocolate coatings: the bars are soft-set and rich already, and a heavy topping overwhelms the spice rather than showing it off.
