Chocolate-Stuffed Flapjack Bars

By Michael

Chocolate-stuffed flapjack bars hide their chocolate in the middle. Half the classic oat mixture is pressed into the tin, a layer of dark chocolate goes over it — chunks, or chocolate spread — and the rest of the mixture is pressed gently over the top, so the chocolate bakes into a seam that only shows when the bar is cut or bitten into. The method is the classic flapjack method with one extra press, and one firm rule at the end: the slab must be completely cold before it is cut, or the middle squeezes out.

The short answer: Make the classic mixture — 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado sugar melted, with 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt stirred in. Press half into a lined 20cm square tin, cover with 150g dark chocolate as chunks or 150g chocolate spread, press the rest gently over the top and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes. Cool completely in the tin and cut into 12 bars.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Chocolate-stuffed flapjack bars: the chocolate is a seam, not a topping

Most chocolate flapjacks put the chocolate where you can see it — on top, or folded through. These bars bury it. From the outside, a chocolate-stuffed flapjack looks like a plain, sturdy oat bar, the kind that travels well and keeps its shape in a bag. The chocolate is a seam through the middle, and it changes how the bar eats: the oats stay chewy and distinct top and bottom, and the centre is soft, dark and rich. It is the layered-bar idea — the same construction as a Nanaimo bar or a filled traybake — built on our classic flapjack recipe mixture, unaltered. The full 350g of oats stays, because the mixture has to do two jobs: a base firm enough to hold the filling, and a lid that covers it.

Because the filling is enclosed, it stays softer than a chocolate topping would. A topping sets hard in the open air of the tin; a filling, insulated by oats on both sides, cools slowly and sets to something between a truffle and a firm ganache. That softness is the pleasure of the bar, and also the reason the cutting rule at the end of the recipe is not negotiable.

Chunks or spread: two fillings, two different middles

The filling can be 150g of dark chocolate in chunks, or 150g of chocolate spread, and the two give genuinely different bars. Chunks, scattered over the base layer, melt in the oven and join up into a broken, uneven seam. Where the chunks were thick, the middle is thick; the seam sets fairly firm once cold, and when you bite a bar the chocolate has a slight snap before it gives. It is the more chocolatey of the two, bite for bite, because nothing has been added to the chocolate itself.

Chocolate spread behaves differently. It is already soft, and it stays soft: the seam it makes is even, smooth and spoonable in texture even when the bar is cold, and because it never fully firms up, the bars cut more cleanly than you would expect — a knife passes through the spread layer without dragging chunks out of line. The flavour is sweeter and milder than plain dark chocolate, since spreads are sweetened and often made with milk. Neither filling is better; they are different bars. Chunks for a dark, firm, grown-up seam; spread for a soft, sweet, even one. Our chocolate flapjack recipe page covers the third arrangement — chocolate as a topping — if you want to compare all three before you bake.

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper, leaving a generous overhang at two sides — a layered slab is heavier and more fragile than a plain one, and the overhang is how it comes out in one piece. A large pan for the mixture, and a palette knife or the back of a spoon for spreading the filling if you are using spread.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a generous overhang at two sides. If you are using chocolate chunks, chop 150g of dark chocolate into rough pieces now.
  2. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado in a large pan over a low heat. Stir until everything has melted into one smooth liquid. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil — boiled syrup sets hard and makes hard bars.
  3. Add the oats and salt. Take the pan off the heat and tip in 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt. Stir until every oat is coated and shiny.
  4. Press in the first half, firmly. Spoon half the mixture into the lined tin and press it down hard and evenly with the back of a spoon, working well into the corners. This layer is the foundation — it carries the filling and the lid — so press it as firmly as you would a whole plain flapjack.
  5. Add the filling. Scatter the chocolate chunks evenly over the pressed base, right to the edges, or spread 150g of chocolate spread over it in an even layer. Keep the filling level; a heap in the middle makes a bar that splits at the sides.
  6. Cover with the second half, gently. Spoon the remaining mixture over the filling in small dollops spread across the surface, then press it down gently — just firmly enough to level it and make contact with the filling all over. Pressing this layer as hard as the first squeezes the filling out at the sides.
  7. Bake for 30–35 minutes. The top should be golden with the edges a shade darker, and the surface should look set. You cannot judge the middle by pressing — the filling is supposed to be soft — so trust the colour of the top and the time.
  8. Cool completely in the tin. Leave the slab in the tin until it is fully cold, all the way through. This takes longer than a plain flapjack because the filling holds its heat. Do not be tempted to hurry it in the fridge and cut it early; a warm middle squeezes out under the knife and the layers separate.
  9. Lift out and cut into 12. Use the paper overhang to lift the cold slab onto a board. Cut with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down in one motion rather than sawing, and wipe the blade between cuts if the filling starts to drag.

The gentle second press, and why these bars are cut cold or not at all

Two steps decide whether a stuffed bar holds together: the press on the second layer, and the temperature at cutting. The second press is a different action from the first. The base is pressed hard, because it is the structure. The lid is pressed gently, because its job is only to lie flat, touch the filling everywhere and knit to it as the slab bakes and cools. Press the lid hard and the filling — softening fast in the oven's heat — squirts out at the edges and the seam ends up thin in the middle and thick at the sides. Press it too lightly and the lid lifts away from the filling when the bars are cut, the layered version of the problem covered in our guide to why chocolate topping lifts off flapjacks: layers separate where they never made proper contact.

Cutting is the other half of it. The filling sets as it cools, and until it has set it behaves like a sauce under pressure — the knife's downward force pushes it sideways, out of the bar. Completely cold, the same filling is firm enough to slice as one piece with the oats around it. The table below covers the failures we see most often with layered bars.

Serving chocolate-stuffed flapjack bars

Cut bars show their seam best, so serve these where the cut face can be seen — on a plate or a board rather than wrapped. They are rich: the standard 12-bar cut is generous, and for serving with coffee you can cut the slab into 16 smaller pieces without changing anything else. A stuffed bar warmed briefly in a low oven softens its middle again and makes a proper pudding with ice cream, but warm them gently and serve at once, because a warm seam is a soft seam. If the layered idea appeals, our salted caramel flapjacks build a caramel layer on a flapjack base by a similar route.

Storing chocolate-stuffed flapjack bars: about 5 days in an airtight tin

Keep the cut bars in an airtight tin at room temperature, in a single layer if you can, or with baking paper between layers so the cut faces do not stick to each other. They keep about 5 days; the filling stays soft and the oats stay chewy. The bars also freeze well for up to 3 months, wrapped individually and sealed; defrost in the wrapping at room temperature, which lets the filling come back to its proper texture without sweating. The general rules are in our guide to how to store flapjacks and how long they last.
