Rhubarb Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

Rhubarb is the sharpest thing that ever goes into a flapjack tin, and that is exactly why it works: the classic recipe is sweet from top to bottom, and chunks of tart rhubarb cut through it like lemon through a rich pudding. This version keeps the tested classic exactly as it is and adds about 300g of rhubarb, cut small and tossed with a tablespoon of sugar. It is also honest about the catch — rhubarb is very wet, and unless you draw some of that water out first, it will make soggy bars.

The short answer: Cut about 300g rhubarb into 1cm pieces, toss with 1 tablespoon of sugar and leave for 30 minutes, then drain well. Melt 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado sugar, stir in 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt, and fold in the drained rhubarb. Press into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 35–40 minutes. Cool completely in the tin and cut into 12 bars.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Rhubarb flapjacks: sharp fruit against a sweet base

Most fruit you put in a flapjack sweetens it further. Rhubarb does the opposite, and the opposite is what the classic recipe needs. The base is our classic flapjack recipe, the version we have kitchen-tested: equal weights of butter, golden syrup and light muscovado sugar, twice that weight of porridge oats, a pinch of salt, baked in a lined 20cm square tin. On its own it is all caramel and butter. Pieces of baked rhubarb scattered through it stay soft and distinct, and each one delivers a clean, sour jolt that resets your mouth for the next sweet bite. It is the same pleasure as rhubarb crumble with custard, in bar form: the sharpness is not a flaw to be sweetened away but the reason the thing is worth eating.

That is why this recipe keeps the classic's sugar at its full weight. It would be natural, adding a sour fruit, to think about balance and trim the muscovado or the syrup — resist it. The syrup is the binder as well as a sweetener, and the sugar is doing structural work; cut either and you get a bar that is both too tart and too fragile. The balance is already there, between the full sweetness of the base and the full tartness of the fruit. A spoonful of sugar on the rhubarb itself, and an optional grating of orange zest, are the only adjustments the flavour needs.

Forced rhubarb and maincrop rhubarb: what to buy

Rhubarb comes in two distinct crops, and they are different enough to be worth knowing apart. Forced rhubarb is grown in the dark in heated sheds — famously in Yorkshire — and picked by candlelight from about January to March. The stalks are slender, tender and a vivid, almost luminous pink, and because the plants grew without light and without cold, the flavour is milder and sweeter than you expect from rhubarb. It needs little trimming and no peeling, and it is the premium product, priced accordingly. Maincrop rhubarb grows outdoors through spring and early summer. Its stalks are thicker, tougher, greener as often as red, and decidedly more tart, with stringier fibres on older stems.

For flapjacks, either crop works, and the choice is mostly about timing and price. Forced rhubarb's tenderness is slightly wasted in a bake where everything softens anyway, but its milder acidity makes a gentler bar and its colour is beautiful. Maincrop is cheaper, tarter and widely available when it is in season, and its extra sharpness arguably suits this recipe even better — you may just want to pull the stringy fibres off the thickest stalks and cut it a touch smaller. Whichever you buy, choose firm, crisp stalks that snap rather than bend, and avoid any that are limp, slimy at the cut end, or badly blemished. One firm rule applies to both: only the stalks are eaten. The leaves are never food — trim them off and put them on the compost, not in the pan.

Taming the water: macerate and drain, or pre-roast

Rhubarb is roughly 95 per cent water, held in crisp stalks that give it all up the moment heat and sugar get involved. Put raw rhubarb straight into a flapjack mixture and it releases that water into the oats as it bakes, and you get the classic failure of fruit flapjacks: edges that are fine, a middle that never sets, and bars that tear instead of cutting. Our guide to why fruit flapjacks turn soggy works through the problem in full. With rhubarb the fix is not complicated, but it does have to happen before the tin, not after it.

Raw, straight inCut and fold in with no preparationThe simplest route and the wettest. The bake runs long, the middle stays soft, and the bars need a long cool and a forgiving eater

Whichever route you take, the size of the cut matters as much as the draining. Pieces of about 1cm soften right through in the time the oats need to bake. Much larger and you get raw, crunchy, mouth-puckering chunks in a cooked bar; much smaller and the rhubarb dissolves into the mixture entirely, flavouring it pleasantly but leaving no distinct fruit to bite into. You want pieces — softened, tart, recognisable pieces.

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper, a large pan, and a bowl and sieve or colander for draining the rhubarb. If your stalks are thick maincrop ones, run a vegetable peeler lightly down the rounded back of each stalk and pull away any long stringy fibres before cutting — thin and forced stalks do not need it.

The method, step by step

  1. Prepare the rhubarb. Wash about 300g of stalks, trim off the leaf end and the dried base, and cut the stalks into pieces of about 1cm. Pull the stringy fibres off any thick stalks as you go.
  2. Macerate and drain. Toss the pieces in a bowl with 1 tablespoon of sugar and the grated zest of an orange, if you are using it. Leave for 30 minutes, until a pool of pink juice has collected in the bowl. Tip everything into a sieve or colander and let it drain for a few minutes, pressing very lightly — you want the surface juice gone, not the pieces squeezed dry. Keep the juice for a drink.
  3. Heat the oven and line the tin. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides for lifting the slab out later.
  4. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado in a large pan over a low heat. Stir until everything has melted into one smooth liquid. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil — boiled syrup sets like toffee and makes hard bars.
  5. Add the oats and salt. Take the pan off the heat and tip in 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt. Stir until every oat is coated and shiny and there are no dry patches at the bottom of the pan.
  6. Fold in the drained rhubarb. Add the rhubarb pieces and fold them through in a few slow strokes, just enough to spread them evenly. The mixture will be looser than the classic because the fruit is still juicy even drained — that is expected.
  7. Press into the tin. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working well into the corners. Pieces of rhubarb poking slightly out of the surface are fine and look good; what matters is that the oats are packed tight, because a wet mixture needs the pressing more than a dry one does.
  8. Bake for 35–40 minutes. A few minutes longer than the classic, because the fruit is still giving up moisture. The edges should be golden brown, the rhubarb pieces softened and slumped, and the centre set but still slightly soft to a fingertip — it firms as it cools.
  9. Score while warm. About 5 minutes after the tin comes out, mark the portion lines with a table knife — 12 bars — while the top is soft enough not to crack. Try to run the lines between rhubarb pieces rather than through them.
  10. Cool completely in the tin. Leave the slab until it is fully cold and set before cutting, and give it longer than you would the classic — a warm rhubarb slab tears at the fruit and leaks juice into everything it touches.
  11. Lift out and cut. Use the paper overhang to lift the cold slab onto a board and cut through along the scored lines with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down. If any bars feel soft, they are still good eating — just serve them on a plate rather than in a hand.

Getting the sharp-sweet balance right

The balance in this recipe is set before the tin goes in the oven, and there are only two levers. The first is the fruit itself: forced rhubarb gives a gentler, sweeter bar, maincrop a sharper one, and a bunch that is mostly green stalk will be tarter than a mostly red one from the same plant — colour is about light, not ripeness, but greener stems do tend to come from older, more acidic growth. Taste a small raw piece while you prepare it and you will know exactly where your batch sits. The second lever is the macerating sugar: the tablespoon it is tossed in takes the raw edge off without sweetening the fruit through, and if your rhubarb is fiercely tart you can raise it to 2 tablespoons without harming the bake — the extra sugar leaves with the drained juice more than it stays in the bars.

What you should not do is rebalance from the other side. Cutting the muscovado, thinning the syrup or swapping part of the butter all weaken the structure that has to hold a wet filling, and they move the flavour the wrong way: a rhubarb flapjack that is not sweet enough is simply sour, and sour with crumbly edges is nobody's goal. If you prefer a gentler bar overall, the better route is a drier fruit filling — our fruit flapjack recipe uses dried fruit, which sweetens as it goes and brings no water with it at all.

Storing rhubarb flapjacks

Keep the cut bars in an airtight tin at room temperature with baking paper between the layers. They keep about 5 days, and like most fruit bakes they are at their best in the first two or three, before the rhubarb's moisture gradually softens the oats around it. They freeze well — wrap the bars individually, seal them in a bag or box and use within 3 months, defrosting at room temperature in the wrapping. There is more detail in our guides to how to store flapjacks and whether you can freeze flapjacks.

Questions people ask

But doing nothing at all is a mistake, because the stalks release their juice into the oats and the middle stays wet. The middle course is the one this recipe uses: toss the pieces with a tablespoon of sugar, leave them 30 minutes and drain off the juice that collects. A brief pre-roast of 10–15 minutes does the same job more thoroughly if you prefer it.

Yes, with the same caution as any frozen fruit. Freezing breaks the stalks' cell walls, so frozen rhubarb gives up even more juice than fresh. Let it thaw in a sieve over a bowl so the liquid drains away as it comes out, then toss the drained pieces with the sugar and carry on. Do not tip the thawing juices into the mixture. Expect the set to be a little softer and give the slab its full cooling time.

Almost always the rhubarb went in with its water still in it. Undrained rhubarb releases its juice during baking, and the oats in the centre — the last part to dry out — absorb more than they can hold. The other causes are pieces cut too large, which hold their moisture longer, and a slab cut before it was fully cold, which lets the juice migrate. If a batch is already baked and soft, serve it warm in squares as a pudding; next time, macerate and drain, or pre-roast.

Green stalks are fine to eat and are not unripe in the way green fruit is. A stalk's colour depends on the variety and how much light reached it, not on ripeness, and some varieties stay largely green however long they grow. Green stalks do tend to taste a little more tart, so you may want the full tablespoon — or two — of macerating sugar. The part of the plant that is never safe is the leaf: trim leaves off and compost them; only stalks are cooked.

With rhubarb, no — it is the one fruit filling where the classic's full sweetness is doing necessary work. Rhubarb brings acid and water but almost no sugar of its own, so the 175g of muscovado and 175g of syrup balance the tartness as well as binding the oats. Reduce them and you get bars that are both sour and fragile. If you want a less sweet flapjack overall, choose a naturally sweet filling instead of cutting the base — dried-fruit versions need no rebalancing at all.

Orange is the classic partner, and this recipe uses the zest tossed through the fruit — it perfumes the bar without adding liquid, which juice would. Ginger is the other traditional match: half a teaspoon of ground ginger stirred into the melted mixture gives warmth behind the tartness, and the combination is a long-standing one in British baking. Vanilla, a teaspoon of extract in the melted butter, softens the edges of the flavour. Keep additions dry or tiny in volume — anything wet works against a filling that is already giving up juice.
