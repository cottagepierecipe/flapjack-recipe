Millionaire's Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

Millionaire's shortbread is three layers: biscuit at the bottom, caramel in the middle, chocolate on top. This recipe keeps the three layers exactly as they are and swaps the bottom one — in place of the shortbread goes the classic flapjack mixture, baked just until set, so the whole thing becomes an oat-bar take on the original rather than the original itself. It is richer, slower and more of an occasion than an ordinary flapjack, and it does not pretend to be anything else.

The short answer: Make the classic flapjack mixture — 175g butter, 175g golden syrup, 175g light muscovado sugar, 350g porridge oats, a pinch of salt — press it into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 25–30 minutes, a few minutes less than the classic, until just set. Cool. Simmer one 397g tin of sweetened condensed milk with 100g butter and 2 tablespoons of golden syrup until deep amber, pour over the base and chill until set. Spread 200g melted dark chocolate over the top, chill again, and cut into 12 with a hot, dry knife.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Millionaire's shortbread, rebuilt on an oat base

The original is a Scottish traybake with a plain shortbread base, and the honest starting point for this recipe is that a flapjack base is not a like-for-like swap. Shortbread is crumbly, pale and plain — a quiet platform for the caramel and chocolate above it. A flapjack base is none of those things: it is sweet in its own right, chewy rather than crumbly, and tastes strongly of butter and golden syrup. Put the layers on it and you get a bar that is richer and chewier than the original, where the base argues with the topping instead of sitting quietly underneath. Plenty of people will prefer the original. This version exists for people who prefer oats.

The base is our classic flapjack recipe, unchanged in its ingredients and proportions — the version we have kitchen-tested. The one adjustment is the bake: the base is taken out a few minutes earlier than the classic, at 25–30 minutes, so it stays tender under its layers instead of baking to the firm chew you want from a bar that is eaten on its own. A base that is perfect by itself is too hard once it is carrying cold caramel and a chocolate lid, and the layers should cut together, not fight each other.

Three layers, three different jobs

Each layer in this bake behaves differently, sets differently and goes wrong in its own way, and it helps to think of them as three small recipes stacked rather than one big one. The base is baked and must be completely cold before anything touches it. The caramel is cooked on the hob and sets as it chills — its texture is decided at the pan, by colour, not by the clock. The chocolate is melted, spread and chilled, and its only real enemy is the knife at the end. Taken one at a time, none of the three is difficult; the patience sits in the gaps between them, while things cool and set.

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper with a generous overhang — you will be lifting out a three-layer slab, and the overhang is how you do it without breaking the chocolate. A heavy-based pan matters for the caramel: condensed milk catches and burns on thin pan bottoms. And keep a jug of hot water by you at the end, for the knife.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a good overhang at two sides for lifting the finished slab out later.
  2. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar for the base. Put 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado in a large pan over a low heat. Stir until everything has melted into one smooth liquid. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil — boiled syrup sets like toffee and makes hard bars.
  3. Add the oats and salt. Take the pan off the heat and tip in 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt. Stir until every oat is coated and shiny and there are no dry patches hiding at the bottom of the pan.
  4. Press into the tin. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working well into the corners. The surface wants to be level — the caramel will find any slope and pool at one end.
  5. Bake until just set, 25–30 minutes. This is a few minutes less than the classic bake, on purpose: the edges should be pale gold and the centre set but still soft-looking. The base firms as it cools, and a tender base is what you want under the layers. Leave it to cool completely in the tin.
  6. Make the caramel. Put the whole 397g tin of sweetened condensed milk, 100g butter and 2 tablespoons of golden syrup into a heavy-based pan over a low to medium heat. Stir constantly as the butter melts and keep stirring as the mixture comes to a gentle simmer — condensed milk scorches on the bottom of the pan if it is left alone for even half a minute.
  7. Simmer until deep amber, about 8–12 minutes. The caramel will thicken and darken as it cooks. Stop when it is a deep amber colour, thick enough that the spoon leaves a trail that holds for a second or two, and pulling away from the sides of the pan as you stir. Pale caramel will not set; caramel taken much darker will taste burnt, so watch the colour, not the clock.
  8. Pour the caramel over the base and chill. Pour it over the cold flapjack base, tilting the tin gently so it reaches the corners in an even layer. Leave to cool for 10 minutes, then put the tin in the fridge until the caramel is firmly set — about 1 hour. It should feel solid and not tacky under a fingertip.
  9. Melt the chocolate. Break 200g dark chocolate into a heatproof bowl set over a pan of barely simmering water, making sure the bowl does not touch the water, and stir until smooth. Short bursts in a microwave, stirring between each, also work. Let it cool for a couple of minutes so it is pourable but not hot enough to melt the caramel.
  10. Spread the chocolate and chill again. Pour the chocolate over the set caramel and spread it gently to the edges with the back of a spoon or a palette knife. Put the tin back in the fridge until the chocolate is just set — about 30–45 minutes. Set is enough; rock-hard is what makes the top crack when cut.
  11. Lift out and cut with a hot, dry knife. Use the paper overhang to lift the slab onto a board. Run a large sharp knife under the hot tap, dry it thoroughly, and cut straight down through the layers in one firm press — 12 bars. Reheat and dry the knife between cuts; a hot blade melts its way through the chocolate instead of cracking it, and a dry blade keeps the cut clean.

The caramel: condensed milk, watched like a hawk

The caramel is the one stage of this recipe that cannot be left alone, and it deserves its own warning. Sweetened condensed milk is milk and sugar cooked down together, and it is already most of the way to caramel before it meets the pan — which is why this method works, and also why it burns so willingly. The mixture needs a low to medium heat and a spoon that does not stop moving, scraping the bottom and corners of the pan in a slow figure of eight. The change, when it comes, is easy to see: the mixture thickens, darkens from cream to amber, and starts to feel heavy on the spoon. Trust those signs over any timer.

What you are making is a close relative of dulce de leche, the condensed-milk caramel spread used across South America, which is the same ingredient cooked to the same end by a slower route. Making it in an open pan, as this recipe does, is the safer and more controllable way to get there: you can see the colour changing minute by minute and stop it where you want it. Keep the heat modest and the spoon moving and it is a calm ten minutes, not a stressful one.

Not our caramel flapjack: how the two recipes differ

This site already has a caramel flapjack recipe, and the two are not the same bake with different names. The caramel flapjack is an everyday bar: a thinner layer of caramel over a base with the sugar reduced to make room for it, built to be cut and kept in a tin for the week. This millionaire's version changes nothing about the base — the sugar stays at the classic's full amount — and adds a full-thickness caramel layer and a chocolate lid on top of it. It is the full three-layer build, and it eats like one: richer, sweeter, and better suited to being cut small and served as a pudding or a treat than packed as a weekday bar. If you want chocolate more than layers, our chocolate flapjack recipe folds the chocolate into and over a plain base without the caramel in between.

Storing millionaire's flapjack

Keep the cut bars in an airtight tin with baking paper between the layers, in the coolest place you have — about 5 days at room temperature in a cool kitchen. Warmth is the enemy of this particular bake: in a hot room the caramel softens and the layers start to slide when the bars are picked up, so if your kitchen is warm, keep the tin in the fridge instead and bring the bars back to room temperature for 20 minutes or so before eating, so the caramel is soft rather than hard. They freeze well — wrap the bars individually, seal them in a bag or box and use within 3 months, defrosting in the fridge and then at room temperature; chocolate that has been frozen sometimes shows pale streaks when it thaws, which is only the surface and changes nothing about the taste. There is more detail in our guide to how to store flapjacks and how long they last.

Questions people ask

The flavour balance tips sweeter, because milk chocolate brings none of the bitterness that dark chocolate uses to stand up to the caramel, and the finished bar is very sweet indeed. And the set is softer: milk chocolate has more milk fat and less cocoa butter firmness in the eating, so the lid yields rather than snaps. If milk chocolate is what your household prefers, cut the bars a little smaller and keep the tin somewhere cool.

Caramel made from condensed milk sets in direct proportion to how far it was cooked: stop at a pale gold and it stays a thick sauce; take it to deep amber, thick enough to hold a trail from the spoon, and it sets into a layer. The other cause is simple impatience — cutting before the caramel has had its full chill. If your caramel is already on the base and clearly too soft, the bars will still eat well cut larger and kept in the fridge, but the fix belongs to the next batch, at the pan.

Because cold chocolate is brittle and a cold knife is a wedge, not a blade — pressed into a chilled slab, it splits the lid ahead of the cut. The hot, dry knife is the whole answer: run it under the hot tap, dry it completely, and press straight down so the blade melts a path through the chocolate. Two smaller habits help too — do not leave the slab in the fridge until the chocolate is rock-hard, and if the tin has been in there a long while, give it 10 minutes on the worktop before cutting.

Ready-made tinned caramel or dulce de leche can be warmed gently and spread in its place, but it sets softer than the pan-made version, so keep the finished bars somewhere cool.
