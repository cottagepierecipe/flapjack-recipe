Chocolate and Hazelnut Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

A chocolate and hazelnut flapjack folds toasted chopped hazelnuts and dark chocolate chips through the classic oat mixture. The nuts are toasted first — five to eight minutes in the oven while it heats — and that one short step is what makes the recipe. Raw hazelnuts are mild and a bit woody in a bake; toasted ones are deep, sweet and properly nutty, and they stand up to the dark chocolate instead of disappearing behind it.

The short answer: Toast 75g chopped hazelnuts for 5–8 minutes until fragrant. Melt 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado sugar in a pan, stir in 300g porridge oats, the toasted nuts and a pinch of salt, fold through 100g dark chocolate chips, press into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes. Cool completely in the tin and cut into 12 bars.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Chocolate and hazelnut flapjacks: the nuts carry the bar

Chocolate and hazelnut is a famous pairing mostly because of a certain spread, but the combination is older and better than any jar. Hazelnuts have a natural sweetness and a deep, almost roasted flavour of their own, and dark chocolate — bitter, rich — is the thing that sets it off. In a flapjack the pairing has to be handled a little differently from a cake or a torte. The oat base is sweet and buttery, and the nuts are folded through a mixture that binds around them, so they need to arrive in the pan with their flavour already developed. That is what the toasting does, and it is why this recipe starts with the nuts rather than the melt.

The base is our classic flapjack recipe with the oats reduced to 300g to make room for 75g of hazelnuts and 100g of chocolate chips, against the full classic 175g each of butter, golden syrup and light muscovado sugar. If you have made our mixed nut flapjacks, the method will feel familiar — but where that recipe spreads several nuts across the batch, this one backs a single nut and pushes it as far as it will go, which gives a cleaner, more deliberate flavour.

Toast the hazelnuts first: 5–8 minutes that change the whole bar

Spread the chopped hazelnuts on a baking tray and put them in the oven as it heats, for 5–8 minutes, until the pieces are a shade darker and the kitchen smells unmistakably nutty. Watch them in the last couple of minutes — nuts go from toasted to burnt quickly, because their oils are right at the surface once they are chopped. Toasting does three things: it deepens the flavour, it dries the surface of the nut so the pieces stay distinct in the crumb rather than turning soft, and it loosens the skins on any skin-on pieces, which can then be rubbed off in a clean tea towel if you want them off.

On skins: buy whichever hazelnuts are easiest to find. Blanched hazelnuts, with the skins already removed, give the cleanest, sweetest result and are the ones to choose if you are buying specially. Skin-on nuts are absolutely fine in a rustic bake like this — the skins add a slight bitterness that some people like against the sweet base, and after toasting, half of them rub off in the towel anyway if you give the nuts a good roll in it while they are warm. What matters far more than the skins is the chop: pieces small enough to distribute through the mixture, big enough to notice when you bite one. Aim for pieces roughly the size of a pea, and tip any dusty nut flour in too — it flavours the crumb.

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper, a baking tray for toasting the nuts, and a large pan for the mixture. A clean tea towel is useful if you want to rub the skins off the toasted nuts.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and toast the hazelnuts. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Spread 75g chopped hazelnuts on a baking tray and toast for 5–8 minutes, until a shade darker and smelling nutty. If you want the skins off, tip the hot nuts into a clean tea towel, fold it over and rub — the skins loosen and flake away. Set the nuts aside.
  2. Line the tin. Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides so the cold slab lifts out cleanly.
  3. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado in a large pan over a low heat. Stir until everything has melted into one smooth liquid. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil — boiled syrup sets hard and makes hard bars.
  4. Add the oats, nuts and salt. Take the pan off the heat and tip in 300g porridge oats, the toasted hazelnuts and a pinch of salt. Stir until every oat is coated and shiny and the nuts are spread evenly through the mixture.
  5. Rest the mixture, then fold in the chips. Leave the pan off the heat for a minute or two, then tip in 100g dark chocolate chips and fold them through with a few turns of the spoon. A short rest keeps the chips as pockets instead of letting them melt into the mixture.
  6. Press into the tin. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working well into the corners. Firm pressing is what holds the bars together once cut.
  7. Bake for 30–35 minutes. The top should be golden with the edges a shade darker, and the centre should look set but still feel slightly soft when pressed — it firms up as it cools.
  8. Cool completely in the tin. Leave the slab in the tin until it is fully cold. Nut flapjacks cut warm crumble at the nut pieces.
  9. Lift out and cut into 12. Use the paper overhang to lift the cold slab onto a board and cut it into 12 bars with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down.

The chocolate spread swirl, and other ways to vary these bars

The recipe as written keeps the chocolate as chips through the crumb. There is one variation worth knowing, because it is the one people ask for: the chocolate hazelnut spread swirl. It is a variation, not the recipe — make the mixture without the chips, press it into the tin, then drop 3–4 tablespoons of chocolate hazelnut spread in dollops over the top and draw a knife through it a few times to marble it. Bake as written. The swirl stays soft, so the bars are richer and less portable, and they need their full cooling time before they will cut. The table below sets out the main variations and what each one changes.

Serving chocolate and hazelnut flapjacks

These bars sit somewhere between a lunchbox flapjack and a pudding. The toasted nuts and dark chocolate make them taste grown-up, and they go particularly well with coffee, where the chocolate-and-nut combination is at home. Cut them into 12 for the standard bar, or into smaller squares for serving with coffee after a meal — the richness carries in a smaller piece. If you like the nut-and-chocolate direction generally, our double chocolate flapjacks push the chocolate further, and the chocolate flapjack recipe page covers the topped version of the classic.

Storing chocolate and hazelnut flapjacks: about 5 days in an airtight tin

Keep the cut bars in an airtight tin at room temperature. They keep about 5 days; the nuts stay distinct and the chocolate pockets stay firm. Nuts stale faster than oats once baked, so these are at their best in the first three or four days. The bars freeze well for up to 3 months, wrapped individually and sealed; defrost in the wrapping at room temperature. The general rules are in our guide to how to store flapjacks and how long they last.

Questions people ask

It is the one step in this recipe that earns its time. Raw hazelnuts folded straight into the mixture taste mild and slightly woody, and in a sweet, buttery base they can disappear altogether. Five to eight minutes in the oven deepens their flavour, dries their surface so the pieces stay distinct, and loosens the skins if you want them off. The oven is heating anyway — the nuts may as well be in it.

Either way, chop them to roughly pea-sized pieces — small enough to distribute, big enough to notice.

Leave the chips out, press the mixture into the tin, dollop 3–4 tablespoons of spread over the top and swirl it through the surface with a knife before baking. The spread stays soft, so the bars are richer and need to be completely cold before they cut cleanly. Do not try to melt spread into the base mixture itself — it loosens the set and the bars come out greasy.

Yes — walnuts, pecans or almonds all work at the same 75g weight, toasted the same way for 5–8 minutes. Walnuts give a softer, slightly more bitter bar; pecans are sweeter and richer; almonds sit between the two and keep the most crunch. The method does not change. Our mixed nut flapjack recipe combines several nuts in one batch if you would rather not choose.

Nuts and chips do not bind — every piece of nut in the slab is a small interruption in the oat mixture that holds the bar together, so nut flapjacks need a firmer press and a full cooling before cutting. Press hard into the corners, leave the slab until it is completely cold, and cut with a large knife pressed straight down. Chopping the nuts smaller also helps: big chunks break a bar's structure far more than pea-sized pieces do.
