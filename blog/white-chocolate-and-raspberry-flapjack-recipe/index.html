White Chocolate and Raspberry Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

These flapjacks fold white chocolate chips and fresh raspberries through a slightly less sweet version of the classic base. White chocolate is all sweetness and cocoa butter; raspberries are sharp and juicy. Baked together into an oat bar, each one makes the other taste better — the berries stop the chocolate cloying, and the chocolate rounds off the berries.

The short answer: Melt 175g butter, 150g golden syrup and 125g light muscovado sugar, stir in 325g porridge oats, a pinch of salt and 100g white chocolate chips, then fold in half of 125g fresh raspberries very gently and press the rest lightly over the top. Bake in a lined 20cm square tin at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 25–30 minutes (estimate). Cool completely in the tin and cut into 12 bars.

Not yet kitchen-tested: this recipe adapts our tested classic flapjack and has not been cooked by us yet. Michael tests every new recipe himself, and this page will be updated once he has cooked it. Times marked (estimate) are estimates.

White chocolate and raspberry flapjacks: sweet chips, sharp berries

White chocolate divides people. On its own it can taste of little but sweet milk; folded through a bake with something sharp against it, it comes into its own. Raspberries are the classic partner — this is the pairing behind a thousand cheesecakes and blondies — because their acidity and their perfume both cut straight through white chocolate's richness. In a flapjack, the oats help too: a plain, toasty base under two strong flavours, holding everything in a bar you can pick up.

The base is our classic flapjack recipe with the sweetness deliberately trimmed — 150g of golden syrup and 125g of light muscovado sugar instead of 175g of each, and 325g of oats instead of 350g. That is not caution; it is arithmetic. The 100g of white chocolate chips folded through the mixture brings its own sugar, and raspberries, for all their sharpness, are fruit. A full-sweetness base on top of both would bury the berry flavour under sugar, and the berry flavour is the point. The tin, the temperature and the pressing are the classic's, unchanged.

White chocolate scorches easily, so the oven stays at 160°C

White chocolate is the most heat-sensitive of the chocolates. It contains no cocoa solids — it is cocoa butter, sugar and milk solids — and the milk solids and sugar brown and catch at temperatures dark chocolate shrugs off. Scorched white chocolate turns grainy, then beige, then bitter, and there is no rescuing it. That is the whole reason this recipe bakes at 160°C and no hotter, and why the chips go through the mixture rather than on top of it: buried among the oats, they soften and slump into pockets instead of sitting exposed on the surface where they would colour and catch.

Chips or chunks both work. Chips are made to hold their shape in a bake, so they stay as distinct pockets; a chopped bar melts further and spreads its sweetness more evenly, which some people prefer. Either way, buy the best white chocolate you reasonably can — because there is no cocoa flavour to hide behind, cheap white chocolate tastes of sweetened fat, and good white chocolate tastes of vanilla and cream. The difference survives baking more clearly than it does with dark chocolate. Stir the chips in off the heat, with the oats, and do not be tempted to melt them into the butter: melted white chocolate re-sets grainy far more readily than dark.

Fresh raspberries hold better than frozen ones

Use fresh raspberries if you possibly can. A fresh raspberry is delicate but dry-skinned: folded gently through the mixture it softens in the oven, slumps a little, and holds most of its juice until you bite into it. A frozen raspberry is a small bag of juice with a weak skin. As it thaws in the heat of the mixture it bursts, bleeds pink through the oats and adds water the recipe has not allowed for — the bars around each berry bake softer, the colour streaks, and the set suffers. This is the same moisture problem that makes fruit flapjacks turn soggy generally, just concentrated in one ingredient.

Handle the fresh berries as little as possible. Half go into the mixture at the very end, folded in with two or three turns of the spoon — not stirred, which would crush them into jam before the tin. Half are pressed lightly over the top of the mixture once it is in the tin, where they bake down into jammy pockets on the surface and make the finished slab look like what it is. If your raspberries are wet from washing, spread them on kitchen paper for ten minutes first; surface water is the enemy of a good set. Big berries can be halved, which spreads the fruit more evenly through the 12 bars.

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper and a large pan. Because this mixture carries a little less syrup than the classic, and loose berries besides, lining the tin matters more than usual — the slab needs the paper overhang to lift out cleanly once it is cold.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides for lifting the finished slab out later. Pick over the raspberries and set them on kitchen paper if they are at all damp.
  2. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put 175g butter, 150g golden syrup and 125g light muscovado in a large pan over a low heat. Stir until everything has melted into one smooth liquid. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil — boiled syrup sets like toffee and makes hard bars.
  3. Add the oats, salt and white chocolate. Take the pan off the heat and tip in 325g porridge oats, a pinch of salt and 100g white chocolate chips. Stir until every oat is coated and the chips are spread through the mixture.
  4. Fold in half the raspberries. Add roughly half the raspberries and fold them through with two or three gentle turns of the spoon. You are distributing them, not stirring — crushed berries bleed juice and pink streaks through the whole batch.
  5. Press into the tin and top with the rest. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working into the corners. Scatter the remaining raspberries over the top and press them in lightly, just enough that they sit in the surface rather than on it.
  6. Bake for 25–30 minutes (estimate). The top should be pale golden with the edges a shade darker — this bake colours less than the classic because there is less sugar in it, so judge by the edges and by the centre feeling set but still slightly soft when pressed. The raspberries on top will have slumped and turned jammy.
  7. Cool completely in the tin. Leave the slab in the tin until fully cold — at least an hour. The white chocolate needs to re-set and the berries make the warm slab fragile; cutting early is how these bars fall apart.
  8. Lift out and cut into 12. Use the paper overhang to lift the cold slab onto a board and cut with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down. A berry sitting on a cut line will drag, so wipe the blade between cuts for the neatest edges.

White chocolate and raspberry problems: soggy middles and scorched chips

The two ingredients that make this recipe are also the two that cause its problems: raspberries bring water, and white chocolate hates heat. The table below is the short version. The longer version is to use fresh, dry berries, fold them in at the last moment, and keep the oven where the recipe puts it even if the top looks paler than you expect.

Variations: dark chocolate, freeze-dried berries, a white chocolate drizzle

The neatest variation swaps the white chocolate chips for dark ones and keeps everything else the same: dark chocolate and raspberry is a stronger, less sweet pairing, and the bars colour more deeply — the dark route is covered in full on our chocolate flapjack recipe page. A second option is freeze-dried raspberries — about 15g, lightly crushed — instead of fresh. They bring intense berry flavour with no juice at all, so the set is firmer and the keeping time longer, but you lose the jammy pockets that make the fresh version what it is. They are a good choice for lunchboxes and posting to people, where a drier bar is an advantage.

If you want a finish on top, melt an extra 50g of white chocolate and drizzle it over the cold slab in thin lines rather than spreading it as a layer — a layer of white chocolate on top sits in the full heat of your mouth and can tip the bars back into too sweet, while a drizzle gives the flavour and the look without the weight. Melt it gently, in a bowl over barely simmering water or in short microwave bursts: overheated white chocolate seizes into a grainy paste, and no amount of stirring brings it back.

Storing white chocolate and raspberry flapjacks: best within 3 days

Keep the cut bars in an airtight tin at room temperature with baking paper between the layers. The standard flapjack keeping time is about 5 days, but the fresh raspberries shorten the comfortable life of these bars: they are at their best within about 3 days, before the berry pockets soften the crumb around them. In a warm kitchen, keep the tin in the fridge and let the bars come back to room temperature before eating. They freeze well for up to 3 months, wrapped individually and sealed; defrost in the wrapping at room temperature. The general rules are in our guide to how to store flapjacks and how long they last.
