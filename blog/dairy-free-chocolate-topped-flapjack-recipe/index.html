Dairy-Free Chocolate-Topped Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

This dairy-free flapjack takes the coconut-oil base of our vegan flapjack and finishes it the way our chocolate flapjack is finished: with a layer of dark chocolate spread over the cooled bake. No butter, no milk, nothing dairy anywhere in it — provided you check one label, which this post explains properly, because the label is where dairy-free baking usually goes wrong.

The short answer: Melt 150g coconut oil, 150g light brown sugar and 4 tablespoons of golden syrup in a pan, stir in 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt, press into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 170°C (150°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 20–25 minutes. Cool completely, spread 150g melted dark chocolate (label checked — see below) over the top, leave to set, and cut into 12 bars.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Dairy-free chocolate-topped flapjacks: two tested recipes combined

This recipe is a combination of two things already on the site, put together because readers kept asking for it. The base is the coconut-oil mixture from our vegan flapjack recipe — coconut oil in place of butter, golden syrup as the binder — which happens to be dairy-free as well as vegan. The finish is the topping route from our chocolate flapjack recipe: the bake cooled completely, dark chocolate melted and spread over the top, left to set and cut through cleanly. What is new here is the pairing, and the care taken over the one ingredient that decides whether the result is genuinely dairy-free: the chocolate.

It is worth being precise about terms. Dairy-free means no milk and nothing made from milk — no butter, no milk powder, no whey, no milk fat. It is not the same claim as vegan, though this recipe is both: a product can be dairy-free without being vegan (it might contain egg or honey), and the golden syrup here, rather than honey, keeps this one on the right side of both lines. The base differs from the vegan page in one respect: there is no fruit or extra flavouring in the mixture, because the chocolate on top is doing that work.

Coconut oil as the fat: what changes against butter

Coconut oil replaces butter measure for measure in the melt, and the method is identical, but two differences are worth knowing. The first is setting. Coconut oil is solid at room temperature and firms up more decisively than butter as it cools — and in a cold kitchen or the fridge it sets noticeably harder. A dairy-free bar eaten straight from the fridge is a firm bite; the same bar at room temperature is chewy. Neither is wrong, but store the tin at room temperature and the texture will be the one the recipe intends.

The second difference is flavour. Refined coconut oil — the kind sold in jars with no coconut smell — tastes of almost nothing, and the bars taste of oats, sugar and chocolate, which is what most people want here. Unrefined (virgin) coconut oil carries a clear coconut flavour through the bake. That is pleasant if you like coconut and a surprise if you do not. A dairy-free baking block — the hard vegetable-fat blocks sold for pastry — also works measure for measure and gives the closest result to butter; our coconut oil flapjack post compares the fats in more detail. What all three share is the same structural job: carrying the melted sugar through the oats and setting as the slab cools.

The chocolate label check: ingredient, or "may contain"?

Here is the honest position on dark chocolate, because it is widely misunderstood. Most dark chocolate at around 70% cocoa is made without milk as an ingredient — the recipe is cocoa mass, cocoa butter (which, despite the name, is the fat of the cocoa bean and contains no dairy), sugar and sometimes vanilla and an emulsifier. So far, so dairy-free. But many of those same bars carry a warning on the back: "may contain milk". That warning is there because the chocolate is made in a factory that also handles milk chocolate, and traces can carry across. It is not a legal ingredient; it is a cross-contact warning.

What that means for you depends on why you are baking dairy-free, and the two cases must not be blurred. If you are baking for a preference — you eat dairy-free by choice, or a mild intolerance means a trace of milk is a discomfort rather than a danger — then a 70% dark chocolate with no milk in its ingredients is a reasonable, informed choice, and it is what most dairy-free home bakers use. If you are baking for someone with a milk allergy, the position is stricter and this post will not soften it: only a chocolate bar that is labelled dairy-free (and made in conditions the maker guarantees) is safe. A "may contain milk" warning means the risk is real for an allergic person, however small. Check the front of the pack for the dairy-free claim, not just the ingredients list, and if the person you are baking for has an allergy, ask them which brands they trust — they will know.

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper, leaving overhang at two sides, a large pan, and a heatproof bowl that sits over a pan of barely simmering water for melting the chocolate. One more check while you are reading labels: golden syrup and porridge oats are naturally dairy-free, but if you use a baking block instead of coconut oil, read its label too — most are purely vegetable fats, but formulations change, and the label on the pack in your hand is the one that counts.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Set the oven to 170°C (150°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides.
  2. Melt the coconut oil, sugar and syrup. Put 150g coconut oil, 150g light brown sugar and 4 tablespoons of golden syrup in a large pan over a low heat. Coconut oil melts quickly, well before the sugar dissolves — keep stirring until the mixture is one smooth, glossy liquid with no grains of sugar left. Keep it gentle and do not let it boil.
  3. Add the oats and salt. Take the pan off the heat, tip in 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt, and stir until every oat is coated and shiny.
  4. Press into the tin. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working well into the corners. Coconut-oil mixtures feel looser than butter ones while warm; firm pressing is what makes them set as one slab.
  5. Bake for 20–25 minutes. The top should be golden with the edges a shade darker, and the centre set-looking but still slightly soft to a press — it firms a great deal as it cools, more than a butter flapjack does.
  6. Cool completely in the tin. Leave the slab until it is fully cold and firm. The chocolate goes onto a cold bake: on a warm one it can sweat, set streaky or lift away from the base as it cools.
  7. Melt the chocolate. Break 150g of the label-checked dark chocolate into a heatproof bowl set over a pan of barely simmering water, the bowl not touching the water. Stir now and then until smooth and fully melted, then take the bowl off the pan. A microwave in short bursts, stirred between each, works too.
  8. Spread the chocolate and leave it to set. Pour the melted chocolate over the cold slab, still in its tin, and spread it to the corners in an even layer. Leave it at room temperature until firm and matte — about an hour. Coconut-oil bases set hard in the fridge, and fridge-cold chocolate cracks when cut, so room temperature is doubly right here.
  9. Lift out and cut into 12. Use the paper overhang to lift the set slab onto a board. Cut with a large sharp knife at room temperature, pressing straight down. If the chocolate threatens to crack, warm the blade under the hot tap and dry it between cuts.

Storing dairy-free chocolate-topped flapjacks: about 5 days in an airtight tin

Keep the cut bars in an airtight tin at room temperature, with baking paper between the layers so the chocolate tops do not mark each other, and they keep about 5 days. Room temperature is not just a preference with a coconut-oil base: in the fridge the bars set very hard and the chocolate can crack when you bite or cut them. If your kitchen is warm, a cool cupboard is the place — chocolate kept in heat or sunlight can develop a pale bloom, which looks worse than it is and changes nothing about the taste. The bars freeze well for up to 3 months, wrapped individually and sealed; defrost in the wrapping at room temperature so the chocolate does not sweat. The general rules are in our guide to how to store flapjacks and how long they last.
