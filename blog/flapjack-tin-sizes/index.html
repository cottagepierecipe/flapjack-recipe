What Size Tin Do You Need for Flapjacks?

By Michael

Most flapjack recipes, ours included, are written for a 20cm square tin. But the tin's name matters far less than most bakers think — what decides whether your bars bake evenly is the depth of the mixture inside it. Get the depth right and almost any tin in the cupboard can be made to work.

The short answer: The standard flapjack tin is a 20cm square tin, lined, which holds the classic mixture — 175g butter, 175g golden syrup, 175g light brown sugar and 350g porridge oats — at a depth of roughly 2.5 to 3cm and makes 12 bars. Whatever tin you use, aim for that same depth: scale the mixture up or down in proportion to the tin's base area, and the baking time stays about the same.

Depth decides the bake: aim for 2.5 to 3cm of mixture

Flapjacks fail in predictable ways when the mixture sits at the wrong depth, and tin size is really just a way of controlling that depth. Spread the mixture too thin and it dries out as it bakes: the edges caramelise hard, the middle turns crisp and biscuity, and the finished bars snap instead of chew. Pile it too deep and the opposite happens — the top and edges overbake while the centre stays soft and syrupy, and the bars fall apart when cut because the middle never set.

The classic proportions in our classic flapjack recipe are built around a depth of about 2.5 to 3cm in a 20cm square tin. At that depth, heat reaches the centre in the same sort of time the surface needs to turn golden, so the whole slab finishes together at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) in 30 to 35 minutes. Every adaptation below works the same way: change the tin, keep the depth, adjust the mixture by area, and the bake behaves itself.

Measure the inside base: the number on the box is the rim

Before scaling anything, find out what size your tin actually is — because the size printed on the label or stamped on the base is usually measured across the top rim, and most tins slope. A tin sold as 20cm can easily measure 18cm across the inside base where the mixture actually sits, and a 2cm difference across a square tin changes its area by a fifth. That is the difference between bars at the right depth and bars that are quietly too deep.

Turn the tin over if you like, but the measurement that counts is the inside base: lay a ruler flat in the bottom of the tin and measure wall to wall in both directions. Write the two numbers on a scrap of paper and keep it with the tin if it is one you use often. Square and rectangular tins are easy — multiply the two sides for the area. For a round tin, measure across the middle (the diameter) and use the table below rather than doing circle sums at the worktop.

The tin table: size, how much mixture, and how many bars

The classic mixture counts as ×1 — a total of about 875g of mixture, giving 12 bars in the 20cm tin. To keep the same depth in a different tin, scale every ingredient by the tin's area compared with the 20cm tin's 400 square centimetres. The times assume the depth stays the same; a deeper or thinner slab needs the adjustments in the next section.

Scaling is not fussy work. Round to the nearest 5g and do not worry about the odd gram of syrup left in the tin — flapjack mixture forgives small differences far more readily than it forgives the wrong depth. If you would rather not scale at all, the easy flapjack recipe uses rounder quantities that halve and double especially cleanly.

Using the unscaled recipe in the wrong tin: what actually happens

Sometimes you will not scale — the tin you have is the tin you have, and the mixture is already made. Here is what to expect, so you can correct with the oven rather than discover it at cutting time. Put the full classic mixture into a 23cm square tin and it spreads thinner, at around 2cm deep: the bars bake faster and crisper, so start checking five minutes early and expect a crunchier, drier result. Put it into an 18cm tin and it sits nearer 4cm deep: the edges will be dark before the centre sets, so give it a few extra minutes at a slightly lower temperature and judge the middle by the wobble described in our guide to how to tell when flapjacks are done.

Neither is a disaster, but both are avoidable, and the thinner version cannot be rescued after the fact — a dried-out bar stays dry. If the mixture is made and the only tin is far too big, the practical fix is to bake the mixture in one area of the tin, dammed into a rough 20cm square with a folded strip of foil or a double wall of baking paper, rather than spreading it thin across the whole base.

Round tins work too: cut wedges instead of bars

A 20cm round cake tin holds about four-fifths of the mixture a 20cm square holds, because the corners are missing. Use ×0.8 of the recipe to keep the depth right, or use the full mixture and accept a deeper slab with a slightly longer bake. The pressing needs a bit more care — work the mixture into an even layer with the back of a spoon, as there are no corners to judge the level by.

Cut a round flapjack the way you would a cake: score it while warm, then cut wedges once cold. Wedges portion perfectly well for a family tin and look deliberate rather than improvised. What a round tin does badly is lunchboxes — wedges do not stack — so if the batch is destined for packed lunches and bake sales, a square or rectangular tin earns its place.

A loaf tin suits a half batch: 6 thick bars, baked a little longer

For small households, the loaf tin is the most useful alternative of all. Half the classic mixture — roughly 90g each of butter, syrup and sugar with 175g oats — pressed into a standard 2lb loaf tin (about 21 × 11cm) gives six thick, satisfying bars, and it is the natural way to test a new variation without committing a full tin. Our page on scaling the classic down in a loaf tin is covered among the batch's technique guides; the principle is simply half quantities, same method.

One adjustment matters: the loaf tin's depth. The mixture sits deeper relative to its surface than in a square tin, so the centre needs a few extra minutes — judge by the cues, not the clock, and expect the edges to look done well before the middle. Line the loaf tin with a proper sling of baking paper, as shown in our guide to how to line a flapjack tin, because a deep, narrow slab is the hardest of all to tip out intact.

Bigger batches and mini tins: traybakes, two tins and muffin cups

For a crowd, scale up by area into a 20 × 30cm traybake tin at ×1.5, or simply make a double mixture and bake it in two 20cm tins side by side. Two tins bake more evenly than one very large one — a single outsized slab tends to set at the edges long before the centre — and they give you a spare tin if the bake needs swapping between shelves halfway. Either way, keep the depth at 2.5 to 3cm and the timing stays familiar; a traybake cuts neatly into 18 bars in a three-by-six grid.

At the other end of the scale, a muffin tin makes individual flapjack cups: press a heaped tablespoon of mixture into each lined or well-greased cup, to the same sort of depth, and start checking several minutes earlier than you would for a slab, because small portions bake through faster. They portion themselves, travel well, and the crisped edges on every piece are a feature rather than a flaw. Cool them in the tin until firm before turning them out, or they will leave their bases behind.

Material matters a little: metal, glass, ceramic and dark tins

Whatever the size, the tin's material nudges the bake. Plain metal — the standard — heats quickly and evenly enough for flapjacks and is what the recipe times assume. Glass and ceramic dishes are slower to heat and slower to cool, which softens the browning: expect to add a few minutes and to get paler, gentler edges. Dark-coated and black tins absorb more heat and push the base and edges ahead of the centre, so check early. None of this makes a tin wrong; it just means the first bake in a new tin is a calibration exercise, and the cues matter more than the clock until you know how yours behaves.
