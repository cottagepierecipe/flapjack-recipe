Peanut Butter and Jam Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

A peanut butter and jam flapjack is the American sandwich pairing rebuilt as a British oat bar: peanut butter melted into the base, and a layer of raspberry jam pressed between two layers of the mixture. One warning before anything else, stated plainly: this recipe contains peanuts. It is not suitable for anyone with a peanut allergy, and the bars should never go into a shared tin or a lunchbox where that could be a risk.

The short answer: Melt 125g butter, 125g smooth peanut butter, 150g golden syrup and 150g light muscovado sugar in a pan, stir in 325g porridge oats and a pinch of salt, press half into a lined 20cm square tin, spread 150g raspberry jam over it leaving a 1cm border, press the rest gently over the top and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes. Cool completely in the tin and cut into 12 bars.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Peanut butter and jam: the sandwich, rebuilt as an oat bar

Peanut butter and jam is the pairing American children grow up on, and it survives into this recipe for the same reason it survives in the sandwich: peanut butter is rich, salty and clinging, and it needs something sharp and fruity against it or it becomes heavy work. Raspberry jam is that thing. Its sharpness cuts the peanut butter the way lemon cuts a rich cake, and its colour sits in a clean red layer through the middle of the cut bar. The base itself is a close relation of our classic flapjack recipe, with the butter halved and peanut butter making up the difference, melted in with the syrup and sugar so the peanut flavour runs through every bite rather than sitting in a layer.

Two other peanut butter bars already live on this site, so here is how this one differs. Our peanut butter flapjack recipe is the plain version — peanut butter through the mixture, nothing else, no layers. The chocolate peanut butter flapjack recipe pairs the peanut butter with chocolate, in the mixture and on top. This one has no chocolate at all: the second flavour is jam, in a layer in the middle, and the bar is fruitier, sharper and less rich than either of the others. And the warning bears repeating once more in the body of the post, because it is the most important sentence in it: the recipe contains peanuts, as an ingredient and as its main flavour, and there is no version of it that is safe for someone with a peanut allergy.

The jam layer: spread it short of the edges, or it boils out

The jam is the only part of this recipe that can misbehave, and it misbehaves in one predictable way: it boils. Jam is fruit and sugar, and in a 160°C oven it liquefies and bubbles. Spread it right to the edges of the tin and the bubbling jam reaches the hot metal, welds itself to the paper and the tin, and boils out of the bar entirely — you lose the layer and gain a burnt, sticky rim that makes the slab hard to lift out. The defence is a border. Spread the jam over the first layer of mixture and stop a good 1cm short of the edges all the way round. The second layer of mixture then meets the first layer at the rim and seals the jam inside, where it can bubble as much as it likes without escaping.

Raspberry is specified for a reason. It is the sharpest of the common jams, and sharpness is what the layer is for; strawberry jam works but tastes noticeably sweeter and softer against the peanut butter. Seeded or seedless is a matter of taste — the seeds are authentic to the sandwich and disappear into the bar. Use a jam you would happily eat on toast, spread at room temperature so it goes on in an even layer without dragging the oat mixture underneath it around. Cold jam straight from the fridge is stiff enough to tear the first layer up as you spread, so take the jar out when you switch the oven on.

Ingredients

Allergy warning: this recipe contains peanuts, in the peanut butter — 125g of it, as a main ingredient. The finished bars are not suitable for anyone with a peanut allergy, and they should not be baked for a bake sale, a shared tin or a child's lunchbox where peanut allergy is a possibility. Our nut-free flapjack recipe is the one to make in those cases.

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides, and a large pan. Lining matters more than usual here: if any jam does escape, paper is the difference between lifting the slab out and chiselling it out.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven, line the tin and take the jam out of the fridge. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides. Set the jam on the counter to lose its fridge stiffness while you work.
  2. Melt the butter, peanut butter, syrup and sugar. Put 125g butter, 125g smooth peanut butter, 150g golden syrup and 150g light muscovado in a large pan over a low heat. Stir until everything has melted into one smooth, thick liquid — the peanut butter takes a minute longer than the butter to give way. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil.
  3. Add the oats and salt. Take the pan off the heat and tip in 325g porridge oats and a pinch of salt. Stir until every oat is coated and shiny. The mixture will be stiffer than the classic — peanut butter sees to that — and that is right.
  4. Press half into the tin. Spoon roughly half the mixture into the lined tin and press it down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working into the corners. This layer carries the jam, so it wants to be level.
  5. Spread the jam, leaving a border. Spoon 150g raspberry jam over the pressed layer and spread it gently to within 1cm of the edges all the way round. Do not press down as you spread — drag the jam across the surface, so the oat layer underneath stays where it is.
  6. Add the rest of the mixture. Spoon the remaining mixture over the jam in small dollops spread across the surface, then press it down gently to join the dollops up and seal the edges. Press more gently than you did the first layer: hard pressing pushes the jam sideways and out.
  7. Bake for 30–35 minutes. The top should be golden with darker edges, and you may see the jam bubbling faintly at any point where it has found a gap — a little is normal. The centre should look set but still feel slightly soft when pressed.
  8. Cool completely and cut into 12. Leave the slab to cool in the tin until it is completely cold — this matters more here than in any other flapjack on the site, because warm jam is liquid and the layers will slide if you cut early. Lift out by the paper overhang and cut into 12 bars with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down and wiping the blade between cuts so the jam layer stays clean.

Jam escapes and layers slide: the layered-bar problems

Layered bars fail in ways plain bars cannot, and nearly all of it comes back to the jam — how far it was spread, how hard the top layer was pressed, or how warm the slab was when it was cut. The table below covers the usual ones.

What happenedLikely causeThe fix next time
Jam boiled out and burnt on the tinThe jam was spread right to the edgesLeave a 1cm border of bare mixture all the way round, so the two oat layers seal together
The top layer slid off when the bars were cutThe slab was cut while still warmCool completely in the tin — the jam only holds the layers once it is cold and set
No visible jam layer in the cut barThe jam sank or was pressed into the basePress the first layer firmly, spread the jam without pressing, and lay the top on in dollops
Bars crumbly and dryThe mixture was pressed too lightly, or the bake ran longPress the first layer firmly; peanut butter mixtures feel stiffer but still need the full press
Jam layer too sweetA sweet jam, or too much of itUse a sharp raspberry jam and keep to 150g — the layer is a stripe, not a filling

Storing peanut butter and jam flapjacks: about 5 days in an airtight tin

Keep the cut bars in an airtight tin at room temperature, with baking paper between the layers so the jam stripes do not stick to the bar above. They keep about 5 days; the jam layer stays distinct and the peanut butter base stays chewy for most of that time. They freeze well for up to 3 months, wrapped individually and sealed, and defrost at room temperature in the wrapping. One storage point that is not about freshness: label the tin. These bars look like ordinary flapjacks, and they contain peanuts — anyone helping themselves from an unlabelled tin deserves to know what is in them. The general rules are in our guide to how to store flapjacks and how long they last.
