Fig and Almond Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

A fig and almond flapjack is the classic oat bar with dried figs chopped through the mixture and flaked almonds split two ways — half folded in, half scattered over the top to toast in the bake. The figs bring a deep, honeyed sweetness and a slight crunch from their seeds; the almonds bring the nutty, toasted flavour that stops the figs tasting one-note. It is, more or less, a fig roll rebuilt as an oat bar.

The short answer: Melt 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado sugar in a pan, stir in 300g porridge oats, a pinch of salt and 150g dried figs with the hard stalks pulled off and the fruit chopped, fold in half of 75g flaked almonds, press into a lined 20cm square tin, scatter the rest of the almonds over the top and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes. Cool completely in the tin and cut into 12 bars.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Fig and almond: the fig roll's flavours, in an oat bar

Figs and almonds belong together in the same way figs and pastry do. The fig roll — dried fig paste wrapped in a plain biscuit casing — has been the proof for over a century that figs want something bland, baked and slightly nutty around them. These bars make the same arrangement with oats instead of pastry. The base is our classic flapjack recipe with part of the oats given up to make room: 150g of dried figs through the mixture and 75g of flaked almonds divided between the mixture and the top. The fig flavour is not subtle. Dried figs are one of the sweetest fruits in the baking cupboard, and 150g of them perfumes the whole tin.

What the almonds do is worth being clear about, because it would be easy to treat them as decoration. Folded through, they break up the sweetness with something savoury and toasted. Scattered on top, they do the same job as the nuts on the tart in the photograph: they brown in the oven, and that browning is flavour the base cannot make on its own. A fig flapjack without the nuts is a pleasant, very sweet bar. With them, it tastes finished.

The figs: stalks off, chopped, no soaking unless they are hard

Dried figs need two minutes of attention before they go into the pan. Each fig has a hard little stalk at the top — pull it off or snip it off, because it does not soften in the bake and finding one in a finished bar is unpleasant. Then chop the figs into pieces about the size of a raisin or a small sultana. Fig flesh is sticky and clings to the knife; the cleanest way is to cut each fig into quarters with scissors, or to chop with a large knife and accept that you will have to scrape the blade clean a few times. If the pieces clump together in a sticky lump, toss them in a spoonful of the measured oats to separate them before they go in.

Soaking is the usual question with dried fruit, and the answer here is: only if your figs are genuinely hard. Most dried figs sold for baking are soft enough to chop and bake as they are, and the syrup in the mixture keeps them chewy. If the figs are stiff and dry, 10 minutes in hot water softens them — drain them well and pat them dry afterwards, because soaking and then adding them wet defeats the purpose. As with every fruit recipe on this site, the fruit is dried for a reason: fresh figs, like the halved ones in the photograph below, are soft, juicy and delicate, and they would collapse into the bake and loosen the set. Dried is the form that works in a bar.

Ingredients

Light muscovado sugar175gKept at the full amount; the figs add sweetness but no binding
Porridge oats300gReduced from the classic 350g to make room for the figs and almonds
Dried figs150gHard stalks pulled off, chopped to raisin size
Flaked almonds75gHalf folded through the mixture, half scattered over the top
Fine sea saltA pinchFigs are very sweet — the salt matters here

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides, a large pan, and scissors or a large knife for the figs. Nothing else — this is a one-pan mixture, like the classic.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven, line the tin and prepare the figs. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides. Pull the hard stalks off the figs and chop the fruit into raisin-sized pieces.
  2. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado in a large pan over a low heat. Stir until everything has melted into one smooth liquid. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil — boiled syrup sets like toffee and makes hard bars.
  3. Add the oats, salt and figs. Take the pan off the heat and tip in 300g porridge oats, a pinch of salt and all the chopped figs. Stir until every oat is coated and the fig pieces are spread through rather than sitting in one sticky clump.
  4. Fold in half the almonds. Add roughly half of the 75g flaked almonds and fold them through the mixture. Keep the rest back for the top.
  5. Press into the tin and scatter the rest of the almonds. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working well into the corners. Scatter the remaining flaked almonds evenly over the surface and press them very lightly with the spoon, just enough that they sit in the surface rather than on it.
  6. Bake for 30–35 minutes. The top should be golden, the almonds on top a shade darker and toasted-looking, and the centre should look set but still feel slightly soft when pressed — it firms up as it cools. If the almonds are browning much faster than the base is setting, cover the tin loosely with foil for the last 10 minutes.
  7. Cool completely and cut into 12. Leave the slab to cool in the tin until it is completely cold — the fig pieces stay soft while warm and the bars cut ragged. Lift it out by the paper overhang and cut into 12 bars with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down so the almond top is not dragged off.

The almonds on top: why half go through and half go over

Splitting the almonds is the one piece of technique in this recipe, and it earns its place. Almonds folded through the mixture stay pale and soft — they taste nutty but they never toast, because they are insulated by the oats around them. Almonds on the surface are exposed to the oven's heat and brown properly, and browned nuts are where the toasted flavour comes from. All through gives you no toast; all on top gives you a nutty crust over a bar that tastes only of fig. Half and half gives you both, in every bar.

The recipe specifies flaked almonds, and the flake is the right shape for the top layer: thin pieces lie flat, cover the surface evenly and toast quickly without burning at the edges the way whole nuts can. Chopped almonds, like the ones in the photograph, work perfectly well through the mixture. What does not work as a straight swap is ground almonds — they drink up the butter, disappear into the crumb and make the bar denser and more cake-like. Our mixed nut flapjack recipe covers the broader nuts-in-flapjacks ground, including which nuts hold up best in a bake.

Swaps: changing the nuts, changing the fruit

The structure of this recipe — dried fruit through the mixture, nuts through and on top — takes swaps well, because the weights stay the same and only the flavours move. Keep the totals at 150g of fruit and 75g of nuts or seeds and the set will not change.

Chopped hazelnuts for the almonds75g, split the same wayDeeper, more toasted flavour; the closest swap in spirit

Storing fig and almond flapjacks: about 5 days in an airtight tin

These keep like a standard flapjack, because both additions are dry ingredients: about 5 days in an airtight tin at room temperature, with baking paper between the layers so the almond tops do not scuff each other. The fig pieces stay chewy throughout. Keep the tin out of direct sunlight — the almonds on top can taste stale if they sit somewhere warm for days. The bars freeze well for up to 3 months, wrapped individually and sealed, and defrost at room temperature in their wrapping. The general rules are in our guide to how to store flapjacks and how long they last.

Questions people ask

Not in this recipe. A fresh fig is soft and full of juice; folded through a flapjack mixture it collapses in the heat, leaks moisture into the crumb and loosens the set, and its delicate flavour mostly disappears. Dried figs are concentrated — 150g of them flavours the whole batch — and they stay in chewy pieces. Fresh figs are for eating as they are, or on top of something cold.

The fig seeds. A dried fig contains hundreds of tiny seeds, and chopping and baking does not soften them away completely — they give the finished bar a faint, pleasant crunch that people often mistake for nuts. It is normal, it is part of what a fig tastes like, and fig rolls have exactly the same texture for the same reason.

Flaked almonds are thin and exposed, and ovens vary — in a hot oven they can go from toasted to scorched while the centre is still setting. Pressing them lightly into the surface before the tin goes in also helps, because a flake sitting in the mixture browns more slowly than one resting on top of it.
