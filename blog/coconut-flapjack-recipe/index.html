Coconut Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

A coconut flapjack is the classic oat bar with desiccated coconut stirred through the mixture — and one adjustment that matters: the oats drop from 350g to 300g, because coconut drinks up moisture and a full measure of oats would make the bars dry. Pressed coconut flakes on top are optional but worth it, toasting gently as the bars bake.

The short answer: Melt 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light brown sugar, stir in 300g porridge oats, 75g desiccated coconut and a pinch of salt, press into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C / 140°C fan / Gas Mark 3 for 30–35 minutes. Cool in the tin, cut into 12 bars.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Coconut Flapjack Ingredients: Why the Oats Drop to 300g

The classic flapjack ratio is equal weights of butter, golden syrup and sugar to double the oats. Coconut breaks that ratio, deliberately. Desiccated coconut is dry and absorbent — it soaks up syrup and butter as the mixture stands and keeps drinking while the bars bake. Stir 75g of it into a full 350g of oats and the mixture is short of liquid: the bars come out dry, crumbly at the edges and dull. Taking the oats down to 300g puts the moisture back in balance, and the coconut more than replaces the lost bulk in flavour.

The Method, Step by Step: One Pan, Then the Tin

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Heat the oven to 160°C / 140°C fan / Gas Mark 3 and line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving an overhang on two sides so the slab lifts out cleanly.
  2. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put the butter, golden syrup and light brown sugar in a large pan over a low heat and stir until melted and combined. Take it off the heat before it boils.
  3. Stir in the oats, coconut and salt. Tip in the porridge oats, desiccated coconut and salt and stir thoroughly. The mixture will look slightly drier than the classic at this point — that is expected, because the coconut absorbs more as it stands. Give it a full minute of stirring so no dry coconut hides at the bottom.
  4. Press into the tin. Scrape the mixture into the lined tin, spread it level and press down firmly with the back of a spoon, working into the corners. A firm press matters more with coconut in the mix, because the absorbent coconut leaves the surface looser.
  5. Add the optional flake topping. If you are using coconut flakes, scatter a small handful over the top and press them down lightly so they stick to the surface rather than scattering when the bars are cut.
  6. Bake for 30–35 minutes. Bake until the edges are golden and the top flakes, if used, are lightly toasted. The centre will still look a little soft — it firms as the tin cools.
  7. Cool in the tin, then cut. Leave the slab in the tin for at least 20 minutes. Mark into 12 bars while warm, cut through once cold, and lift out on the paper.

Which Coconut to Use: Desiccated, Flakes, Fresh and Toasted

Four forms of coconut turn up in baking, and they are not interchangeable in a flapjack. Desiccated coconut is the one this recipe is built on: finely shredded and dried, it spreads evenly through the oats and its dryness is exactly why the oats were reduced. Coconut flakes are wide, thin shavings — too big to mix through evenly, but good pressed on top, where they toast. Fresh grated coconut is wet; using it in place of desiccated coconut adds water the recipe has not allowed for and gives a softer, paler bar. Sweetened coconut, sold for some American-style bakes, adds sugar on top of the 175g already in the recipe and makes the bars cloying — avoid it here.

Toasting is the flavour lever. Desiccated coconut stirred in raw tastes mild and milky. Spread the same coconut on a tray and toast it in the oven for a few minutes until pale gold before mixing it in, and the flavour deepens noticeably — nuttier, warmer, closer to the toasted flakes on top. Watch it closely: desiccated coconut colours fast and burns faster, in as little as a minute past golden. If you toast the coconut, the bake itself stays at 30–35 minutes; only the topping flakes toast further in the oven.

Coconut Oil Instead of Butter: The Dairy-Free Swap

Replacing the butter with 175g of coconut oil makes the recipe dairy-free and doubles down on the coconut flavour. Coconut oil melts more completely than butter, so the warm mixture looks looser — it still sets, but the finished bars are a touch crumblier at room temperature and noticeably firmer straight from the fridge, because coconut oil sets harder when cold. Our vegan flapjack recipe is built on the same swap without the coconut stirred through; combining the two gives a fully coconut bar. Use refined coconut oil if you want the dairy-free behaviour without the extra coconut taste competing with the desiccated coconut.

Coconut in Other Oat Bakes: The Anzac Biscuit Connection

Oats and coconut have been baked together far beyond flapjacks. Anzac biscuits, the Australian and New Zealand oat biscuits, are built on the same partnership — rolled oats and desiccated coconut bound with butter and golden syrup — only spread thin and baked crisp as biscuits rather than pressed thick as bars. If you like the flavour of this flapjack, the biscuit version is worth knowing about; if your flapjack mixture ever spreads too thin in a larger tin, you will see the family resemblance. The comparison also explains the texture difference between the two bakes: the biscuits are crisp because they are thin and baked dry, while these bars stay chewy because the thick slab keeps its moisture in the middle.

Chocolate and Coconut: Adding Chips Without Unbalancing the Bar

Coconut and chocolate are old partners, and 75g of dark chocolate chips can be stirred through with the coconut if you want them. Add them after the oat mixture has stood for a minute off the heat — stirred into a pan that is still too hot, the chips melt into streaks rather than staying as chips, which is a different (also good) result, but worth choosing rather than stumbling into. Keep the total additions in view: coconut plus chips plus any dried fruit all compete for the same butter and syrup, so if you add chips and fruit together, take the oats down a further 25g or the bars will crumble at the corners. A plainer way to get the same pairing is to drizzle melted chocolate over the cooled slab and let it set before cutting — the chocolate sits on top instead of drinking moisture from inside the bar.

Porridge Oats or Jumbo Oats with Coconut: Porridge Wins Here

The classic flapjack tolerates jumbo oats for a chunkier texture, but the coconut version is better on porridge oats. The fine desiccated coconut and fine porridge oats pack down together into an even slab, while jumbo flakes leave pockets that the coconut fills loosely — those bars cut raggedly and shed crumbs. If jumbo oats are what you have, use them and press the tin especially firmly, and expect a more rustic cut. What matters more than the oat size is the salt: a pinch sounds like nothing across 12 bars, but coconut without salt tastes flat and sweet, and with it the flavour sharpens noticeably.

Storing Coconut Flapjacks: 5 Days in the Tin, 3 Months in the Freezer

Keep the bars in an airtight tin at room temperature for up to 5 days. Coconut flakes on top soften gradually, so the topping is at its best in the first two days. The bars freeze well for up to 3 months, wrapped individually; thaw at room temperature for about an hour.
