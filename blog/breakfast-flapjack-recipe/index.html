Breakfast Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

This is the classic flapjack with breakfast things folded through it — mixed seeds and chopped dried fruit stirred into the oats, and the sugar trimmed a little because the fruit brings sweetness of its own. Make them the night before, cut them big or small, and eat them at the table with fruit or yoghurt, or wrapped up and carried out of the door. They are flapjacks, plainly — butter, syrup and oats — just dressed for the morning.

The short answer: Melt 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 150g light muscovado sugar. Stir in 300g porridge oats, 50g mixed seeds, 100g chopped dried fruit and a pinch of salt. Press into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes. Cool completely in the tin and cut into 12 bars — or 8 bigger ones.

Not yet kitchen-tested: this recipe adapts our tested classic flapjack and has not been cooked by us yet. Michael tests every new recipe himself, and this page will be updated once he has cooked it. Times marked (estimate) are estimates.

The classic base, with 100g of dried fruit and 50g of seeds folded through

The starting point is our classic flapjack recipe, the version we have kitchen-tested: butter, golden syrup and light muscovado melted together, porridge oats stirred in, pressed into a 20cm tin and baked. This breakfast version keeps that skeleton exactly — the same butter, the same syrup, the same tin, the same oven — and changes only the dry ingredients and the sugar. Of the classic's 350g of oats, 300g stay as oats. Into the mixture go 50g of mixed seeds and 100g of chopped dried fruit, and the sugar drops from 175g to 150g, because apricots and raisins sweeten as they bake and the full amount would push the bars too sweet.

Nothing here turns a flapjack into a health food, and this page will not pretend otherwise. There is still butter in it, still syrup, still sugar. What the seeds and fruit change is the eating: the bars chew differently, with the seeds giving little crunches through the soft oats and the fruit giving soft, sweet pockets. That makes them sit comfortably at breakfast, in a way a plain flapjack — which is really a teatime bake — sometimes does not. They are a make-ahead bar for the morning, not a claim about anything.

What the seeds and fruit do in the mixture

Seeds and dried fruit behave very differently in a hot syrup mixture, and it is worth knowing which job each one is doing before you start swapping them. Seeds — pumpkin, sunflower, sesame, or a ready-mixed bag of all three — stay dry and crisp through the bake. They do not absorb the syrup, so they do not loosen the mixture or change the set. Their contribution is texture: small, hard crunches scattered through a soft bar.

Dried fruit does the opposite. Apricots and raisins soften in the heat and give up a little of their sugar into the mixture around them, which is why the added sugar can come down. They also bring moisture, and that is why the amounts matter: 100g of fruit against 300g of oats keeps the balance the classic is built on. Much more fruit than that and the bars turn damp and bendy rather than set, and loose fruit sitting on the surface scorches in the oven — dried fruit sugars burn faster than oats do, so the fruit needs to be stirred through, not sprinkled on top.

One thing the additions do not change is the method. The seeds and fruit go in with the oats, off the heat, and are stirred until everything is coated in the melted butter and syrup. There is no soaking, no pre-cooking and no extra step — if you can make the classic, you can make these, because it is the same bake wearing different clothes.

A bar, not a bowl: how this differs from baked oats

Baked oatmeal turns up in breakfast conversations a lot, and it is worth being clear that it is a different thing from this recipe, made a different way. Baked oats are porridge oats bound with milk and usually egg, baked in a dish and eaten warm with a spoon — a porridge-style bake, soft all the way through, that keeps in the fridge and is reheated. These flapjacks are bound with butter and golden syrup, contain no milk and no egg, are cut cold into bars, and keep in a tin like a biscuit. Neither is a substitute for the other: one is breakfast in a bowl, the other is breakfast you can hold.

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper and a large pan — seeds and fruit make this a bulkier mixture than the plain classic, and a big pan makes the stirring far easier. If you are chopping apricots, a heavy knife or kitchen scissors both work; the fruit is sticky, and wiping the blade between cuts stops it gluing itself into a lump.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides for lifting the slab out later.
  2. Chop the dried fruit. Cut the apricots into small pieces, roughly the size of the raisins, so the fruit spreads evenly through the mixture instead of landing in a few big lumps. Raisins and smaller fruit go in as they are.
  3. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 150g light muscovado in a large pan over a low heat. Stir until everything has melted into one smooth liquid. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil — boiled syrup sets like toffee and makes hard bars.
  4. Add the oats, seeds, fruit and salt. Take the pan off the heat and tip in 300g porridge oats, 50g mixed seeds, the 100g chopped dried fruit and a pinch of salt. Stir until every oat is coated and shiny, the seeds and fruit are spread through rather than clustered, and there are no dry patches hiding at the bottom of the pan.
  5. Press into the tin. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working well into the corners. Firm pressing is what stops flapjacks crumbling later — and it tucks most of the fruit below the surface, where it cannot scorch.
  6. Bake for 30–35 minutes. The top should be golden brown with the edges a shade darker, and the centre should look set but still feel slightly soft when pressed — it firms up as it cools. Any fruit that is sitting exposed on top will look darker than the oats around it; that is the fruit's sugar, and a shade darker is fine, but blackened is too far.
  7. Score while warm. About 5 minutes after the tin comes out, mark the portion lines with a table knife while the top is soft enough not to crack — 12 bars, or 8 if you are cutting the bigger breakfast size.
  8. Cool completely in the tin. Leave the slab until it is cold and set before cutting; warm flapjack cuts raggedly and can fall apart. An hour at least, and longer does no harm — overnight is fine, which suits a recipe made for the next morning anyway.
  9. Lift out and cut. Use the paper overhang to lift the cold slab onto a board and cut through along the scored lines with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down.

Twelve bars or eight: the only thing that changes is the portion

The recipe makes the same slab either way, and cutting it into 8 instead of 12 changes nothing about the baking — same tin, same mixture, same time in the oven. What changes is simply the size of the piece you pick up. Eight bars are the sit-down size: a substantial piece next to fruit and yoghurt, or the whole of a carried breakfast on its own. Twelve are the smaller size: something to go alongside other breakfast things, or a bar for a lunchbox later in the day. Score the lines before the slab goes cold, four-by-three for 12 bars or four-by-two for 8, and the choice is made while cutting is still easy. There is no third difference hiding anywhere — the mixture does not know how you sliced it.

Eating them: at the table, or wrapped for the road

At home, the natural plate is a bar with fresh fruit or a spoonful of yoghurt beside it — the bar brings the butter and syrup, the fruit and yoghurt bring the freshness, and neither is pretending to be the other. They are good cold, exactly as they come out of the tin, and there is no need to warm them. Out of the house, a cold bar travels well: wrap one in baking paper or foil and it will survive a bag or a pocket until mid-morning without crumbling, provided it was pressed firmly and cut cold. That portability is the whole point of the recipe — it is baked once, in the evening, and then breakfast is a thing you pick up rather than a thing you cook.

If your household already leans on oats in the morning, two near neighbours on this site are worth knowing about. Our granola flapjack recipe bakes granola-style clusters into bar form, and the muesli flapjack recipe folds a muesli mixture — oats, fruit, nuts and seeds already combined — into the same classic base. This breakfast version sits between them: plainer than both, and the one to make when the cupboard has a bag of seeds and a bag of dried fruit but no granola or muesli in it.

Making ahead and storing breakfast flapjacks

These are a make-ahead recipe by design, and they are honestly better for it: like most flapjacks they eat best once they have sat for a day, when the set has firmed and the flavours have settled together. Bake in the evening, cool the slab in the tin, cut it in the morning — or cut it the same night and store the bars ready to grab. Keep the cut bars in an airtight tin at room temperature with baking paper between the layers; they keep about 5 days, which covers a working week of breakfasts from one bake.

They also freeze well — wrap the bars individually, seal them in a bag or box and use within 3 months, defrosting at room temperature in the wrapping; a frozen bar taken out the night before is ready by morning. There is more detail in our guides to how to store flapjacks and how long they last and whether you can freeze flapjacks.
