Cornflake Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

Cornflake flapjacks take the classic butter, syrup and sugar mixture and fold in lightly crushed cornflakes alongside the oats. The result is a bar with two textures: the chew of a flapjack and a proper crunch from the flakes, which toast as they bake and turn the top deep gold.

The short answer: Use the classic binders — 175g each of butter, golden syrup and light muscovado sugar — with 225g porridge oats and 125g plain cornflakes, lightly crushed so plenty of flakes stay whole. Bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 25–30 minutes, watch the edges because the flakes brown faster than oats, and eat within 2–3 days while the crunch lasts.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Cornflake flapjacks: the classic method with a crunchy swap

This recipe adapts the tested classic flapjack from our homepage. The melted mixture — butter, golden syrup and muscovado in equal weights — stays exactly the same. What changes is the dry side: instead of 350g of oats, the tin gets 225g of porridge oats and 125g of cornflakes. The oats are still needed. They soften into the syrup, bind the bar and give it body. The flakes are there for texture, and on their own they would never hold a slice together.

Cornflakes behave differently from oats in the oven, and the recipe is built around that. They are already cooked and crisp, they absorb very little syrup, and they brown quickly — much more quickly than oats do. That is why the bake is a few minutes shorter than the classic and why the edges need watching: the difference between deep gold and burnt is smaller with flakes in the tin. In return, the bars have a toasted, malty flavour and a crunch no oat-only flapjack can match.

Texture is a live argument on this site — our guide to chewy vs crunchy flapjacks sets out what makes a bar one or the other. This recipe is the crunchy camp's best answer: a bar that chews in the middle and crackles at the edges.

Ingredients

IngredientAmountNotes
Butter175gSalted or unsalted
Golden syrup175gWeigh it — the syrup is the glue that coats the flakes
Light muscovado sugar175gLight brown sugar works in its place
Porridge oats225gStandard porridge oats — these give the bar its body
Cornflakes125gPlain and unfrosted, lightly crushed (see below)
SaltA pinchBalances the sweetness and lifts the toasted flavour

125g of cornflakes is a bigger pile than it sounds — flakes are mostly air, so the mixture will look flake-heavy before it goes into the tin. That is right. Once pressed and baked, the volume settles to the same slab as the classic.

Choosing the cornflakes: plain, unfrosted and dry

Buy the plainest cornflakes you can find. Frosted or sugar-coated flakes bring extra sugar that melts, changes the set and makes the edges catch and burn sooner. Honey or nut-flavoured crunch cereals change the recipe in ways this page has not tested. Plain flakes, the kind eaten with cold milk, are what the recipe is written for.

Keep the flakes dry until the moment they are used. Cornflakes go stale and soft quickly once a box is open, and soft flakes bake into a leathery bar rather than a crunchy one. If your box has been open a while, spread the flakes on a tray in a low oven for a few minutes to crisp them up again, and let them cool before they meet the syrup.

Crushing the flakes: light is the whole trick

The flakes need crushing, but only just. Tip them into a bowl and press down with the end of a rolling pin, or squeeze them gently by the handful, until roughly half are broken and half are still whole flakes. You are aiming for a rubble of flakes, not a powder.

It matters because the fine dust is what lets the recipe down. Flake dust drinks syrup greedily, turns pasty, and gives you a dense, soggy bar with no crunch at all — the very thing you added cornflakes to avoid. Whole and half flakes, by contrast, keep their shape, trap little pockets of air, and come out of the oven crisp. If in doubt, crush less. You can always break a few more; you cannot un-crush them.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Heat the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving paper overhanging two sides so the slab lifts out easily.
  2. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put the butter, golden syrup and muscovado in a large pan on the hob over a low heat and stir until melted into one glossy liquid. Never let it boil — boiled syrup sets hard, and hard syrup plus crisp flakes makes a bar that fights back.
  3. Stir in the oats first. Take the pan off the heat and stir in the porridge oats and the pinch of salt until the oats are coated. Doing the oats first gives them the syrup they need to bind the bar.
  4. Fold in the cornflakes last. Add the lightly crushed flakes and fold them through gently with a spoon, turning the mixture rather than stirring hard. Folding keeps the flakes whole; rough stirring at this point shatters them into dust.
  5. Press gently into the tin. Tip the mixture into the lined tin and press it level with the back of a spoon — firmly enough to hold together, but more gently than the classic. Pressing with full force crushes the flakes you have just been careful with.
  6. Bake, and watch the edges. Bake for 25–30 minutes. The top should be deep golden and the edges a shade darker. Because flakes brown faster than oats, start checking a few minutes early; if the edges are racing ahead, the bake is nearly done.
  7. Cool in the tin. Leave the slab in the tin on a wire rack until completely cold. The crunch develops as the bars cool and the syrup sets around the flakes, so a warm bar will taste softer than the finished one.
  8. Score while warm, cut when cold. Mark 12 bars with a table knife after about 10 minutes, then lift the cold slab out on its paper and cut through along the marks.

The texture to expect: crisp edges, chewy middle

Cut cold, a cornflake flapjack should slice cleanly and hold its shape. The first bite gives you crunch from the flakes at the top and edges, then the chew of the oats underneath. The flakes keep the colour deep and the flavour toasty. If your bar bends before it bites, it was probably cut warm or slightly underbaked; if it is hard all the way through, the syrup boiled or the bake ran long — the troubleshooting table below covers both.

The classic bars behind this recipe

Everything here rests on the classic. The pan, the melted mixture, the tin and the cooling are unchanged — only the dry ingredients differ, which is why the method should feel familiar if you have made the original. If you have not, make the classic first; it is the tested benchmark this crunchier version is measured against.

Can you reduce the sugar or syrup?

Not without changing what the bar is. In a flapjack the sugar and syrup are not just sweetening — the syrup is the glue that coats the oats and flakes, and the sugar helps the mixture set firm as it cools. Cut the syrup and there is not enough to coat the flakes, so the bar crumbles. Cut the sugar and the set turns soft and the toasted flavour flattens. If you want a less sweet bar, the honest options are to cut the slab into smaller bars, or to make the classic and serve it plainly rather than rebalancing this recipe and hoping. What you can do without harm is swap the muscovado for light brown sugar, which tastes a touch milder, and make sure the pinch of salt goes in — salt makes the same amount of sugar taste less flat, not more sweet.

How to store cornflake flapjacks (and keep the crunch)

Store the cut bars in a truly airtight tin at room temperature, and be realistic about the clock: the crunch fades as the flakes slowly take up moisture from the bar itself and from the air, so these are best within 2–3 days. The classic keeps longer; this version trades keeping time for texture. A tin that seals well matters more here than with any other flapjack — a loose lid can cost you the crunch overnight. Do not refrigerate them; the fridge is damp and softens flakes faster. Freezing is possible — the method in our freezing guide works — but expect the crunch to be dulled when they defrost, so freeze only if the alternative is wasting them.

Questions people ask

It is not recommended for this recipe. The sugar coating melts into the syrup mixture, makes the bars sweeter and more likely to catch at the edges, and changes the set. The recipe is written and balanced for plain, unfrosted flakes.

Not with this method. Oats soften into the syrup and bind the bar; cornflakes stay separate and crisp, so a flakes-only mixture does not hold together as a sliceable flapjack. The 225g of oats in this recipe is what makes the bar a bar.

Usually storage: flakes absorb moisture from the bar and the air, so a loose-lidded tin softens them quickly. Stale flakes from a long-open box, or flakes crushed to dust before baking, also lose their crunch from the start. Use an airtight tin and eat them within 2–3 days.

Lightly — about half broken, half still whole flakes, and as little dust as you can manage. Dust drinks the syrup and turns pasty, which makes the bar dense and soggy. If you are unsure, crush less; whole flakes keep their crunch.

Because cornflakes are already toasted and thin, they brown much faster than oats, especially at the edges where the tin is hottest. This recipe already bakes for less time than the classic — check from around 22 minutes and take the tin out when the top is deep golden, not dark brown.

Bran flakes and malted wheat flakes behave similarly and can be swapped like for like, crushed lightly. Puffed rice cereals are much lighter and more fragile — they tend to soften rather than crunch — so they are not a like-for-like swap in this recipe.
