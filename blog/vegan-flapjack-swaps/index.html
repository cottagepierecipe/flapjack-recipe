Vegan Flapjack Swaps: What to Use Instead of Butter, Honey and Milk

By Michael

A flapjack is nearly vegan already: oats, sugar and syrup are plant foods, and the animal products hide in just a few places — the butter, sometimes honey, and the milk or condensed milk in the richer recipes. This guide works through each one in turn: what to swap it for, how much to use against our classic quantities, and what the swap does to the set and the flavour, so a vegan bake is a decision rather than a gamble.

The short answer: Swap butter for refined coconut oil or a dairy-free baking block at 150g for the classic's 175g of butter. Swap honey for golden syrup or maple syrup, measure for measure. Golden syrup is already vegan — it needs no swap at all — while honey is not vegan. Swap sweetened condensed milk for coconut condensed milk, milk for oat milk, and ordinary chocolate for dairy-free chocolate. Every swap, its quantity and its effect on the bake are in the table below.

The rule behind every swap: replace the job, not just the ingredient

Swaps fail when they replace an ingredient's name rather than its job. In a flapjack, butter does three things: it is the fat that carries flavour and keeps the bar moist, it is part of the liquid that coats the oats, and — because butter is only about four-fifths fat, with water and milk solids making up the rest — it brings a little water with it. A good butter swap has to be a fat that melts the same way and sets again as the bar cools. Honey's job is different: it is a liquid sweetener and a flavouring, so its swap must pour and bind like a syrup. Milk in the protein recipe is mostly moisture and a little protein. Condensed milk is sugar, milk and thickness in one tin. Keep the jobs straight and the swaps in this guide are straightforward; mix them up — oil where a syrup should be, water where a fat should be — and the bars will tell you about it.

Two facts are worth stating plainly at the start, because they settle the most common questions. Golden syrup is vegan: it is made from sugar, with nothing animal in it, so the classic flapjack's syrup needs no substitute. Honey is not vegan: it is made by bees, and any recipe that uses it — our honey flapjack included — needs a syrup swap to become vegan. Everything else in the classic recipe, the oats, the light muscovado sugar and the salt, is plant-based as it stands.

Butter: coconut oil or a dairy-free block, at 150g for 175g

This is the swap that matters most, because butter is the largest animal ingredient in the tin. The vegan flapjack recipe on this site uses 150g of refined coconut oil or dairy-free block in place of the classic's 175g of butter, and the reduction is deliberate. Butter is roughly 82% fat and the rest mostly water; coconut oil and baking blocks are effectively all fat. Use a full 175g of pure fat and the bars come out greasy, with oil weeping into the paper as they cool. At 150g the fat content of the mixture lands where the butter version sits, and the bars set cleanly.

Between the two, choose by flavour. Refined coconut oil is the neutral one — "refined" is the key word, because unrefined (virgin) coconut oil tastes of coconut and will flavour the whole bake. A dairy-free baking block behaves most like butter: it melts at a similar rate, sets firmly in the fridge of the mixture's cooling, and gives the closest texture to the original. Coconut oil sets harder than butter when cold, so coconut-oil bars are firmest straight from a cold kitchen and soften more as they warm; block-based bars hold the classic texture most faithfully. Our post on flapjack recipes without butter works through the same swap from the dairy-free side.

Sweetness from fruit: dates instead of some sugar and syrup

Not every vegan swap is a packet product. Dates, soaked and blended or finely chopped, can take over part of the sugar and syrup's work — sweetness and stickiness together — which is the route our healthy flapjack recipe takes with dates and banana. Fruit sweetness changes the bar more than any swap in the table: the set is softer, the keeping time shorter, and the flavour fruity rather than caramel. Treat it as a variation in its own right rather than a like-for-like swap, and keep at least some syrup in the mixture if you want bars that cut and travel well. It is, though, the only swap on this page that reduces the refined sugar instead of just replacing the animal products.

Honey: golden syrup or maple syrup, measure for measure

Where a recipe uses honey — as the main sweetener in our honey flapjack recipe, or a spoonful for flavour elsewhere — the swap is a pouring syrup of the same weight. Golden syrup is the closer match in behaviour: it binds firmly, sets the same way, and its mild caramel flavour sits where honey's was. Maple syrup is thinner and less sweet per spoonful, with a flavour of its own that comes through clearly in the finished bar; the mixture will be a little looser, so press it firmly and give the slab its full cooling time. Either way, swap by weight, not by volume, because syrups are denser than honey and a cup-for-cup swap quietly short-changes the recipe.

What you lose is honey's particular flavour — there is no plant ingredient that tastes like honey, and anything sold as tasting like it is a flavouring, not a swap. What you do not lose is structure: honey and syrup do the same binding job, so the bars set and keep exactly as the original recipe says. The comparison of the two as sweeteners, vegan question aside, is in our post on honey versus golden syrup for flapjacks.

Golden syrup: the swap that needs no swap

It is worth giving golden syrup its own short section, because it is the ingredient people most often write to us about. Golden syrup is a sugar syrup — an inverted sugar syrup made from sugar refining — and it contains nothing of animal origin. It is vegan as it stands, in every flapjack recipe on this site, including the classic. The same goes for light muscovado and other plain sugars in the UK: British sugar is not filtered through bone char, so the sugar in these recipes is suitable for vegans without any swapping at all. If you are converting a recipe from elsewhere and its syrup is golden syrup, that line of the ingredients list is already done.

Condensed milk, milk and chocolate: the smaller swaps

Sweetened condensed milk appears in our condensed milk flapjack and the chewy recipes, where it brings sugar, milk and a thick, fudgy body. The vegan swap is coconut condensed milk — condensed coconut milk with sugar, sold in similar-sized tins — used measure for measure. It sets a little softer and carries a coconut note that suits the chewy texture. Oat cream with extra sugar stirred in is a poorer second: it brings the moisture but not the body, and bars made with it stay loose. If neither is available, the honest answer is to choose a different recipe rather than force this one.

Milk turns up in our protein flapjack recipe, where 100ml loosens the peanut butter mixture. Oat milk swaps in measure for measure and is the natural flavour match in an oat bake; any unsweetened plant milk works, but thin ones like rice milk loosen the mixture more, so add them a splash at a time. Chocolate is the easiest swap of all: much plain dark chocolate is made without milk — check the ingredients, because recipes differ and some dark bars contain milk fat — and dairy-free chocolate swaps for ordinary chocolate in equal weights in toppings, chips and chunks, melting and setting the same way. Our dairy-free chocolate-topped flapjack is built on exactly that swap.

Read down the last column and the pattern is consistent: the swaps hold the structure and nudge the flavour. A vegan flapjack made this way is not a lesser version of the classic — it is the classic with its fat and its minor ingredients changed, which is why the vegan recipe page can use the same tin, the same temperatures and the same timings as the original.

What swapping does to set and keeping

Two practical differences are worth knowing before you bake. The first is the set: coconut oil melts at a lower temperature than butter and sets harder in the cold, so a coconut-oil flapjack is firmest straight from a cold tin and softens noticeably in a warm room or a warm lunchbox. Bars made with a dairy-free block track the butter original most closely at every temperature. Neither is a fault — but if the bars are travelling somewhere warm, the block is the safer fat. The second is keeping: vegan flapjacks keep the same way as the classic, about 5 days in an airtight tin at room temperature and up to 3 months frozen, wrapped. The fruit-sweetened versions are the exception — banana and date mixtures are moister and are best eaten within 3 to 4 days, or frozen on the day of baking. The full storage rules are in our guide to how to store flapjacks.
