Banana and Date Flapjack Recipe (No Golden Syrup or Table Sugar)

By Michael

This flapjack is sweetened entirely by fruit: ripe bananas mashed into the mixture and dates soaked, drained and stirred through, with cinnamon for warmth and just 75g of butter or coconut oil to bind it. There is no golden syrup and no table sugar in the tin at all. There is, however, plenty of sugar — it arrives inside the fruit rather than out of a packet, and this post is plain about that difference, because it is the whole recipe.

The short answer: Soak 150g chopped dates in hot water for 10 minutes and drain well. Melt 75g butter or coconut oil, stir in 200g mashed ripe banana, the drained dates, 300g porridge oats, 1 teaspoon of ground cinnamon and a pinch of salt. Press into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 170°C (150°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 25–30 minutes (estimate). Cool completely and cut into 12 soft, fruity bars.

Not yet kitchen-tested: this recipe adapts our tested classic flapjack and has not been cooked by us yet. Michael tests every new recipe himself, and this page will be updated once he has cooked it. Times marked (estimate) are estimates.

Sweetened by fruit: what "no syrup or table sugar" honestly means

Start with the honest part, because this recipe is easy to oversell. Bananas and dates are both sweet because they contain sugars — a ripe banana is roughly an eighth sugar by weight, and dried dates are well over half sugar. A bar from this recipe is a sweet bar. What "no golden syrup or table sugar" means, precisely, is that no refined sweetener is added: every gram of sugar in the tin arrived inside a whole fruit, bringing the fruit's fibre, water and flavour with it. For bakers who are cutting down on refined sugar, or who simply like the deep, caramel-fruit taste that dates give a bake, that is a real and worthwhile difference. It is not a sugar-free flapjack, and it is not a low-sugar one either — dates see to that — and any version of this recipe that claims otherwise is not being straight with you.

The recipe is built on the formula of our healthy flapjack recipe, which already uses banana and dates in place of most of the sugar. This version takes the last step that page does not: the remaining syrup and sugar come out entirely, the oats settle at 300g, and a teaspoon of cinnamon steps into the gap where the caramel flavour of syrup used to be. Fruit-sweetened mixtures taste less sweet than the same weight of sugar — banana and date sugars come packaged with water and fibre that blunt them — so the cinnamon matters more than it looks on the list. Warm spice reads as sweetness to the palate, and it is doing quiet work in every bite.

The bananas: ripe, mashed smooth, weighed after peeling

The bananas are not a flavouring here; they are half the binding. Mashed banana is wet, starchy and sticky, and in the oven it sets around the oats the way syrup does in the classic — less firmly, which is why these bars are soft, and why the dates' chew matters alongside it. Use properly ripe bananas: yellow skins well freckled with brown, fruit that mashes to a purée under a fork without lumps fighting back. Under-ripe bananas are starchy rather than sweet, mash badly, and will leave the bars both less sweet and less bound — the two failures this recipe can least afford. Very ripe, nearly black bananas are ideal and are often the cheapest in the shop.

Weigh the banana after peeling and mashing: 200g is about two large bananas, peeled. Mash it thoroughly — the smoother the purée, the more evenly it coats the oats — and expect the mixture to look wetter and looser than a classic flapjack mixture. That is correct. Banana brings water with it, and the oats absorb it as the slab bakes and cools; a mixture that looks right in the bowl by classic standards would bake dry. If banana is the part of this recipe that interests you most, our banana flapjack recipe uses it alongside ordinary sugar for a sweeter, firmer bar, and our guide to the best bananas for flapjacks covers ripeness in full.

The dates: soaked, drained and chopped small

Dates do the opposite job to the banana. Where banana binds softly, dates bring chew and the deepest sweetness in the recipe — a dark, caramel-like flavour that is the closest thing fruit offers to the taste of golden syrup. They need ten minutes in hot water first. Dried dates straight from the packet are firm and leathery; stirred into a flapjack mixture as they are, they stay in hard pieces and the bars cut unevenly around them. Soaked, they soften, chop easily, and spread through the mixture in small pieces that bake into tender, jammy pockets. Drain them thoroughly — the soaking water is not part of the recipe, and tipping it in would loosen a mixture that is already moist from the banana.

Chop the drained dates small, roughly raisin-sized, and toss them through the oats before the wet ingredients go in if you find they clump — dates are sticky and like to travel through a mixture in one lump if they get the chance. Pitted dates are the sensible buy; if yours have stones, the soaking makes them easy to slip out before chopping. Our date flapjack recipe uses dates with a conventional syrup base, if you want their flavour in a firmer, sweeter bar.

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper, leaving overhang at two sides, and a large pan. There is deliberately less fat in this recipe than in any other on the site — 75g against the classic's 175g — because the banana purée takes over much of the coating work. That is also why the press into the tin and the full cooling time matter so much in the method: with this little syrup-style binding, the bars hold together by being well pressed and properly set, not by glue.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven, line the tin and soak the dates. Set the oven to 170°C (150°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving overhang at two sides. Chop 150g of dates, cover with hot water and leave for 10 minutes, then drain thoroughly.
  2. Mash the banana. Mash 200g of ripe banana in a bowl with a fork until it is a smooth purée with no lumps of any size — lumps bake into wet patches.
  3. Melt the butter or coconut oil. Melt 75g of butter or coconut oil in a large pan over a low heat. There is no syrup or sugar to melt with it, so this takes under a minute; take the pan off the heat as soon as it is liquid.
  4. Stir in the banana and dates. Add the mashed banana to the pan and stir until evenly mixed, then stir in the drained, chopped dates, breaking up any clumps with the spoon.
  5. Add the oats, cinnamon and salt. Tip in 300g porridge oats, 1 teaspoon of ground cinnamon and a pinch of salt. Stir until every oat is coated and the dates are spread through the mixture. It will look soft and moist — that is right for this recipe.
  6. Press into the tin. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down very firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working into the corners. With no syrup to glue the slab, this press is doing the binding work — take your time over it.
  7. Bake for 25–30 minutes (estimate). The top should be golden and feel set and dry to a fingertip, though softer than a classic flapjack. The edges colour faster than a syrup bake's — check at 25 minutes.
  8. Cool completely and cut into 12. Leave the slab to cool fully in the tin; fruit-bound bars cut warm will crumble. Lift out using the paper overhang and cut into 12 bars with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down.

Choosing dates: what to buy and how they differ

Any dried date works in this recipe, but they do not all work the same way. Soft, dark dates — the expensive, toffee-like kind sold in boxes — soak quickly, chop into a near-paste, and give the bars their richest flavour; they are the best choice if the dates are the point. Firmer, lighter dates — the common baking dates sold in bags — need their full ten minutes of soaking and chop into distinct pieces, which gives a chewier bar with dates you can find in each bite. Both are good; they are different bars. Deglet Noor style dates sit between the two and are what most supermarkets sell as their standard pitted date. Avoid pre-chopped dates coated in flour or oil if you can: the coating dulls their stickiness, and their sticking is half their job here.

Choose between them by what you are actually after. If you want a proper flapjack, bake the classic. If you want the fruit flavours with a firmer set and a touch more sweetness, the healthy flapjack is the middle point. This one is for the baker who wants the sweetening to come from fruit alone and is happy with a softer bar as the price.

Texture, keeping and serving: a softer bar, honestly described

These bars do not eat like classic flapjacks, and pretending otherwise would set you up to think you had failed. They are soft, moist and fruity, closer to a baked oat-and-fruit bar than to the chewy, syrupy classic — they bend a little rather than snap, and the edges do not crisp the same way. Eaten within that expectation, they are good: the date pieces are jammy, the cinnamon warms the banana, and the oats give the whole thing body. They are at their best on the first two days. Keep them in an airtight tin at room temperature for about 5 days — the banana keeps them moist, and past that the fruit moisture makes them progressively softer rather than stale — or freeze them for up to 3 months, wrapped individually, and defrost as needed; freezing suits this soft bar particularly well. The general rules are in our guide to how to store flapjacks.
