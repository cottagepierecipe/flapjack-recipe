Salted Caramel Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

A salted caramel flapjack is built in two bakes. The oat base goes into the oven first, on its own, for 15 minutes; then a thick condensed-milk caramel — salted as it cooks — is poured over it and the whole tray goes back in until the caramel sets at the edges. A scatter of flaky sea salt over the top finishes it. There is no chocolate anywhere in this recipe, and that is the point: nothing stands between the caramel and the salt.

The short answer: Press a base of 150g butter, 150g light brown sugar, 3 tbsp golden syrup, 325g porridge oats and a pinch of salt into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 15 minutes. Boil a 397g tin of sweetened condensed milk with 100g butter, 100g light brown sugar, 1 tbsp golden syrup and ½ tsp fine sea salt to a thick caramel, pour it over the base and bake 10–12 minutes more. Scatter flaky sea salt, cool completely and cut into 12 bars.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Salted caramel flapjacks: caramel and salt, with no chocolate in the way

This site already has two caramel bars, and this one is different from both — it is worth being plain about how. Our caramel flapjack recipe pours caramel over a flapjack base and finishes it as a sweet, plain caramel bar. Our millionaire's flapjack adds a chocolate layer over the caramel, in the millionaire's shortbread style. This salted caramel version keeps the caramel layer but replaces the chocolate with salt: fine sea salt cooked into the caramel itself, and flaky sea salt scattered over the top. Without chocolate in the way, the caramel is the whole flavour of the topping, and the salt has something real to work against.

The base is a slightly leaner version of our classic flapjack recipe — 150g each of butter and light brown sugar with 3 tablespoons of golden syrup to 325g of oats — because the caramel layer brings its own butter and sugar to the bar. Baking the base on its own first is what makes the construction work: a raw base under wet caramel would cook unevenly and the layers would merge. Fifteen minutes firms the oats into a platform that the caramel can sit on as a distinct layer.

Salt in the caramel and salt on top: two different jobs

The salt appears twice in this recipe, and the two additions are not interchangeable. The ½ teaspoon of fine sea salt goes into the caramel as it cooks. Fine salt dissolves completely, so it seasons the whole layer evenly — you will not taste it as salt, exactly; what it does is take the raw, one-note sweetness off the condensed milk and make the caramel taste deeper and more of itself. Caramel without any salt tastes flat and sugary once it is on a sweet oat base. Caramel with salt cooked in tastes like caramel.

The flaky sea salt scattered over the top does the opposite job. Flakes do not dissolve into the set caramel, so they stay as tiny, distinct crystals that crunch faintly and deliver a small, bright hit of salt in some bites and none in others. That contrast — sweet caramel, then a spark of salt — is the whole character of the recipe. Use proper flaky sea salt for the top, not table salt: table salt is fine-grained, dissolves on contact and tastes harsh in comparison, and it is easy to overdo. A pinch of flakes, scattered from a height so they spread evenly, is all it takes.

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper, with the paper coming up the sides — caramel bubbles up as it bakes and will weld itself to a bare tin. A heavy-based pan for the caramel, because condensed milk catches and scorches in a thin one, and a large pan for the base mixture. The caramel itself is a close relative of the one in our condensed milk flapjack recipe, which folds the same tin into the mixture rather than layering it.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving overhang at two sides and making sure the paper covers the sides of the tin.
  2. Make the base mixture. Put 150g butter, 150g light brown sugar and 3 tbsp golden syrup in a large pan over a low heat and stir until melted into one smooth liquid. Take the pan off the heat and stir in 325g porridge oats and a pinch of fine sea salt until every oat is coated.
  3. Press in the base and bake it alone. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin, press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, and bake for 15 minutes, until the surface looks set and is just turning golden at the edges.
  4. Make the caramel while the base bakes. Put the 397g tin of sweetened condensed milk, 100g butter, 100g light brown sugar, 1 tbsp golden syrup and ½ tsp fine sea salt in a heavy-based pan over a low heat. Stir until the butter has melted and the sugar has dissolved, then bring it to a gentle boil. Boil gently, stirring all the time and scraping the base of the pan, until the caramel thickens, darkens a shade and leaves a clear trail when the spoon is drawn across the base of the pan — several minutes of steady stirring. It will keep thickening as it stands, so do not chase a very stiff set in the pan.
  5. Pour the caramel over the base. Take the tin out of the oven and pour the hot caramel over the part-baked base, tilting the tin gently so it spreads into the corners in an even layer. Work reasonably quickly — the caramel starts to set as it cools.
  6. Bake again for 10–12 minutes. Return the tin to the oven until the caramel is set at the edges and the surface has lost its wet shine, with perhaps a few slow bubbles around the rim. The middle will still look soft; it firms as it cools.
  7. Scatter the flaky salt. As soon as the tin comes out, scatter a good pinch of flaky sea salt evenly over the hot caramel from a height. The surface is still tacky, so the flakes will stick where they land.
  8. Cool completely in the tin. Leave the slab in the tin until it is fully cold. Warm caramel is a sauce; cold caramel is a layer. Cutting before it has set drags the caramel off the base.
  9. Lift out and cut into 12. Use the paper overhang to lift the cold slab onto a board. Cut with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down; warming the blade under the hot tap and drying it between cuts helps it through the caramel cleanly.

Serving salted caramel flapjacks

These are dessert bars. The caramel layer makes them richer than a plain flapjack, and they are best served in the standard 12-bar cut with tea or coffee, or in smaller squares as something sweet after a meal. The salt flakes sit on the surface, so serve the bars flake-side up and handle them by the base — a thumb dragged across the top takes the finish with it. They need no accompaniment, though a spoonful of crème fraîche alongside cuts the sweetness well if you are serving them as a pudding.

Storing salted caramel flapjacks: about 5 days in an airtight tin

Keep the cut bars in an airtight tin at room temperature, in a single layer if you can — the caramel tops can stick to anything stacked on them, so use baking paper between layers. They keep about 5 days; the caramel stays firm and the base stays chewy. Keep the tin out of the fridge: cold caramel turns hard and dull, and the bars eat much better at room temperature. They freeze well for up to 3 months, wrapped individually and sealed, and should be defrosted in the wrapping at room temperature. The general rules are in our guide to how to store flapjacks and how long they last.

Questions people ask

It was not boiled for long enough. Condensed-milk caramel thickens as it boils and then firms further as it cools and during the second bake, so it should leave the pan thick enough to leave a clear trail behind the spoon — if it runs straight back together, it needs more time at a gentle boil, stirred constantly. Give the finished bars their full cooling time too: caramel cut warm always looks runnier than the same caramel cold.

Not for the layer. A pouring sauce is made to stay liquid, and spread over the base it stays liquid — the bars would weep caramel when cut and the layer would never set. The condensed-milk caramel in this recipe is a different substance: boiled until it thickens, it sets to a firm, sliceable layer as it cools. A jar of sauce is fine served alongside the finished bars, but the layer itself has to be the boiled kind.

Yes, and this recipe uses each where it belongs. Fine sea salt goes into the caramel, where it dissolves and seasons the whole layer evenly. Flaky sea salt goes on top, where the flakes stay whole and give small, bright hits of salt against the sweet caramel. Table salt on top is the one to avoid: its grains are small and harsh-tasting, it dissolves on contact with the warm surface, and it is very easy to scatter too much.

Caramel is a sugar syrup before it is anything else, and hot sugar welds itself to bare metal and to any gap in the lining. Line the tin so the paper comes up the sides with no gaps, and let the slab cool completely before lifting it out on the paper overhang — a warm slab flexes and tears at the corners, a cold one lifts out whole. If a corner does stick, slide a thin knife under the paper, not under the bar.
