Microwave Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

A microwave flapjack is the same simple mixture as the classic — butter, golden syrup, sugar and oats — cooked in minutes instead of half an hour in the oven. It comes out paler, softer and chewier, with nothing crisp about it, but it is a proper oat bar all the same, and it is the fastest route there is from deciding you want flapjacks to cutting them.

The short answer: Melt 100g butter, 100g light muscovado sugar and 3 tablespoons of golden syrup in a large microwave-safe bowl on high for 1–2 minutes (estimate) until runny, stir in 200g porridge oats and a pinch of salt, and press the mixture into a lined microwave-safe dish about 18–20cm across. Cook on high in an 800W microwave for 4–5 minutes (estimate) until the edges look set and the centre is just set, then cool completely in the dish before cutting into 8 bars. The times are estimates for 800W — a lower-wattage microwave needs longer — and the bars firm up as they cool.

Not yet kitchen-tested: this recipe adapts our tested classic flapjack and has not been cooked by us yet. Michael tests every new recipe himself, and this page will be updated once he has cooked it. Times marked (estimate) are estimates.

A microwave flapjack is a different bake, not just a quicker one

It helps to know what you are making before you start, because a microwave flapjack is not the oven version produced faster. Our classic flapjack recipe — 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado sugar to 350g porridge oats, baked in a 20cm tin at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes — gets its character from the oven: golden edges, a top that dries and firms, a centre that sets as it cools. A microwave heats the mixture through quickly and evenly but cannot brown it. There is no dry, hot air and no fierce surface heat, so the top stays pale, the edges stay soft, and the finished bar is chewy all the way through rather than crisp at the edges and soft in the middle.

Plenty of people prefer it that way, and there are honest reasons to choose the microwave even when an oven is available. It is quick — under fifteen minutes from weighing to cooling dish. It makes a small batch without heating a whole oven. It suits kitchens without an oven at all: a rented room, a caravan, a student hall with a shared microwave. And it is forgiving about timing in one direction — because nothing browns, nothing burns at the edges in the way an oven batch can if you turn your back. What it asks in return is that you judge the set by eye and by cooling, not by colour, because colour is never coming.

The dish matters more here than the mixture does

The mixture is the classic's ingredients in smaller quantities, and it behaves itself. The dish is where a microwave flapjack is won or lost. It must be microwave-safe — glass, ceramic, silicone or a plastic tub sold as microwave-safe — and on no account metal: a metal tin in a microwave sparks, and that ends the bake and possibly the microwave. A dish about 18–20cm across suits this quantity. Much smaller and the mixture sits deep, so the centre stays wet long after the edges are cooked; much larger and the layer spreads thin, cooks before you have checked it, and gives you brittle bars.

Line the dish with baking paper, with a little overhang to lift by. Microwaved flapjack mixture sets where it lands, and an unlined dish holds onto it in a way that makes scrubbing the reward for your baking. Paper also lets you lift the cooled slab out whole and cut it on a board, which is cleaner than cutting in the dish. Press the mixture down firmly and evenly before it cooks — in the microwave there is no melting-and-settling stage to level things out for you, so the surface you press is the surface you get.

You will also need a large microwave-safe bowl for melting and mixing — larger than looks necessary, because the syrup mixture can bubble up as it heats — and a microwave-safe dish about 18–20cm across, lined with baking paper. If your dish is square or round makes no difference here; the area is what matters, and both shapes at that width hold this quantity at a sensible depth.

The method, step by step

  1. Line the dish. Line a microwave-safe dish about 18–20cm across with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides for lifting the slab out later. Check the dish carries a microwave-safe mark — and never use a metal tin.
  2. Melt the butter, sugar and syrup. Put 100g butter, 100g light muscovado sugar and 3 tablespoons of golden syrup in a large microwave-safe bowl. Cook on high for 1–2 minutes (estimate) until everything is runny. Stir well: the sugar should be dissolved into the butter and syrup, with no gritty layer at the bottom. If it is not quite there, give it short bursts of 20–30 seconds (estimate), stirring between each, rather than one long blast — hot syrup keeps cooking between bursts.
  3. Stir in the oats and salt. Tip in 200g porridge oats and a pinch of salt and stir until every oat is coated and glossy and there are no dry patches. The mixture will be looser than an oven flapjack mixture looks; that is right at this stage.
  4. Press into the dish. Spoon the mixture into the lined dish and press it down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working into the corners and edges. A level, well-pressed surface cooks most evenly and holds together best when cut.
  5. Cook on high. Cook on high (800W) for 4–5 minutes (estimate). The edges should look set and slightly drier than the middle, and the centre should look just set — still soft to a fingertip, with a slight sheen. It will not brown and it will not look baked in the oven sense. If your microwave has no turntable, pause halfway and turn the dish half a turn, so both sides get the same treatment from the hot spots.
  6. Cool completely in the dish. Leave the dish on a heatproof surface until the slab is completely cool — at least 30 minutes. This is not a serving suggestion; the bars genuinely firm up as they cool, and a slab cut warm falls apart and seems undercooked even when it is not.
  7. Lift out and cut into 8. Lift the cold slab out by the paper overhang and cut into 8 bars with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down. The bars will be soft and chewy rather than crisp — that is the nature of the bake.

Wattage changes the timing, so treat the clock as a guide

Every microwave recipe carries the same warning, and this one is no exception: the cooking time depends on the power of your machine, and microwaves vary far more than ovens do. The times in this recipe are for an 800W microwave, and they are estimates. A 700W machine needs longer for the same result; a 900W or 1000W machine gets there sooner and overshoots sooner too. The wattage is usually printed on a label inside the door or on the back of the machine. Use the table as a starting point, then go by the look of the slab — edges set, centre just set — rather than by the beep.

Your microwaveCooking time (estimate)What to do
700W5–6 minutesCheck at 5 minutes; add short bursts if the centre still looks wet
800W4–5 minutesThe times this recipe is written for
900W3½–4½ minutesCheck a minute early; a stronger machine overshoots quickly
1000W3–4 minutesCheck early and often — the gap between set and overcooked is short

Two habits make any wattage behave better. The first is standing time: a microwaved bake keeps cooking gently for a few minutes after the power stops, as the heat evens out through the slab, so "just set" out of the microwave becomes "properly set" on the worktop — judge it after a few minutes' stand, not the second the door opens. The second is stopping early rather than late. An underdone centre can have another 30 seconds; an overcooked slab cannot have anything taken back, and overcooked is where the texture troubles in the next section come from.

What can go wrong: rubbery, hard, soggy or scorched in patches

Microwave flapjacks fail in a small number of predictable ways, and nearly all of them are about time, dish size or hot spots rather than the recipe. The honest summary is that the mixture is the classic's and is not the problem; the cooking is the variable.

What happenedLikely causeThe fix next time
Rubbery, bendy barsCooked a little too long — the oats toughen as the mixture dries outStop when the centre is just set and let standing time and cooling finish the job
Hard barsCooked well past set; microwaved syrup sets firmly as it cools, like boiled syrup in an oven bakeShorten the cook and check earlier — a soft-looking centre firms up a great deal as it cools
Soggy, wet centreMixture too deep (dish too small) or not cooked long enough for a lower-wattage machineUse an 18–20cm dish so the layer is shallow, and add short bursts until the centre is just set
One scorched or rock-hard patchA hot spot — microwaves heat unevenly, and one corner of the dish got more than its shareTurn the dish halfway through if your microwave has no turntable, and keep the layer level
Bars fell apart when cutCut while still warmCool completely in the dish first — the set only arrives as the slab cools

Additions that work in a microwave batch

The plain bar is the recipe, but a small batch is the natural place to try additions, because a failed experiment costs you 200g of oats rather than a full tin. A handful of raisins or sultanas — about 50g — stirred in with the oats comes through well, plumping slightly in the heat. A tablespoon of mixed seeds adds crunch that the soft bake otherwise lacks. Chocolate chips are better scattered over the top the moment the slab comes out of the microwave: the residual heat melts them just enough to sit, where chips stirred through the mixture melt entirely and streak. Keep additions modest and dry; anything wet — fresh fruit, jam — adds water to a bake that has no oven heat to drive it off, and tips the centre towards soggy. For fruit flavours done properly, the oven versions on this site, like our apple and banana bakes, are the better route. And for another no-conventional-oven batch, our no-bake flapjack recipe sets in the fridge instead.

Storing microwave flapjacks

Once cut, keep the bars in an airtight tin at room temperature; they keep about 5 days, staying soft and chewy throughout — arguably at their best on the first day, before the edges dry at all. They freeze for up to 3 months wrapped individually and sealed in a bag or box; defrost at room temperature in the wrapping. A defrosted bar that has gone a little firm comes back with 10 seconds in the microwave — the same machine that made it. The full rules are in our guide to how to store flapjacks and how long they last, and the freezing detail in can you freeze flapjacks.
