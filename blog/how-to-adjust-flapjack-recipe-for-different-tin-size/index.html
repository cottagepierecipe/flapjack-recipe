How to Adjust a Flapjack Recipe for a Different Tin Size

By Michael

Your tin is not the tin in the recipe. Perhaps it is the good round one, or the big tray that fits your oven shelf, or the loaf tin that happens to be clean. The recipe's quantities were worked out for one particular tin, and if you change the tin without changing the quantities, you get flapjacks too thick, too thin, or baked for the wrong length of time. The good news is that the correction is a single sum, and it works for every tin you own.

The short answer: Scale by base area, not by the tin's name or diameter. Our classic recipe fills a 20cm square tin — a base of 400cm². Measure your tin's base, work out its area, divide by 400, and multiply every ingredient by that factor: an 18cm square or a 20cm round tin takes ×0.8, a 23cm square takes ×1.3, a 20×30cm tray takes ×1.5. The temperature stays at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Keep the mixture at the same depth and the baking time stays roughly the same too — a deeper mixture needs longer, a shallower one less, so check early either way.

Why a "Slightly Bigger" Tin Is Hungrier Than It Sounds

Tin sizes mislead because they are given as a length, while what a tin holds is an area — and area grows much faster than length. Move from a 20cm square tin to a 23cm square and the tin sounds 15% bigger. Its base, though, goes from 400cm² to 529cm², which is nearly a third more mixture to fill it to the same depth. Put the standard recipe into that tin unscaled and it spreads a third thinner: it bakes faster, dries more, and cuts into bars that are flapjack in name and biscuit in behaviour.

Shapes mislead too. A 20cm round tin and a 20cm square tin share a headline number and almost nothing else: the square's base is 400cm², the round's is about 314cm². The "same size" round tin holds roughly a fifth less. This is why recipes that work in the writer's tin fail in yours even when you followed every word — nobody was careless, the arithmetic was hidden. Our classic flapjack recipe is written for the 20cm square tin, 175g butter, 175g golden syrup, 175g light muscovado sugar and 350g porridge oats, making 12 bars. Every other tin needs those numbers translated, and translation is all this page is.

The Three Sums: Measure the Base, Divide by 400, Multiply Everything

Sum one is the area of your tin's base. Measure inside the tin, at the base rather than the rim — many tins slope, and the rim measurement flatters them. For a square or rectangular tin, multiply length by width: a 20×30cm tray is 600cm². For a round tin, halve the diameter to get the radius, multiply the radius by itself, then by 3.14: a 20cm round is 10 × 10 × 3.14, which is 314cm². A springform or loose-based tin is measured the same way, across its base.

Sum two is the factor: your tin's area divided by 400, the area the recipe was written for. A 23cm square gives 529 ÷ 400, which is 1.3. Sum three is to multiply every single ingredient by that factor — butter, syrup, sugar and oats alike, in the same proportion. The recipe's balance lives in its ratios, so nothing is exempt and nothing gets rounded in a different direction from the rest. If a factor produces awkward numbers, round each ingredient to the nearest 5g, all the same way; a few grams either way makes no difference to a flapjack, but a lopsided rounding does.

Depth Is the Sum Behind the Sum

What the area factor is really protecting is depth. Flapjack mixture is meant to sit in the tin at a particular thickness — thick enough to stay moist and chewy in the middle, thin enough to bake through evenly. Scale by area and the depth comes out the same in any tin, and with it the character of the bake. Ignore the factor and depth is where you pay: the full recipe in a small tin sits deep, and a deep slab is the hardest bake of all, darkening at the edges while the centre is still deciding. The same recipe spread over a tray sits thin, and thin slabs race ahead, crisping through before their time is up.

This is why the answer to "can I just use my bigger tin?" is yes, with a condition. The tin is never the problem. The unscaled mixture in it is. Keep the depth right — with the factor, or by honestly accepting a thinner or thicker bake and adjusting the time to match — and almost any sound tin will bake you a decent flapjack.

What Changes With the Tin, and What Does Not

The quantities change. Almost nothing else does. The oven temperature stays at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) whatever the tin, because the mixture itself has not changed — only how much of it there is and how it is spread. A bigger batch does not want a hotter oven; it wants the same oven for about the same time. Turning the heat up to "compensate" for a large tin just burns the edges of a slab whose centre is still raw.

The lining stays the same, and matters the same: parchment with an overhang to lift by, whatever the shape — our guide to how to line a flapjack tin covers the method, and round and loaf tins take the same approach with a strip cut to fit. Pressing the mixture down firmly and evenly stays the same, and arguably matters more in an unfamiliar tin, where an uneven press gives an uneven depth and therefore an uneven bake. Cooling in the tin stays the same. Tin material is a separate variable — metal and glass conduct heat differently and can nudge the timing — but it adjusts the bake by minutes, not by the wholesale changes that area demands.

Baking Time Follows Depth, Not Quantity

The rule worth remembering is that the clock follows the depth of the mixture, not the weight of it. A ×1.5 batch spread over a 20×30cm tray to the standard depth takes about the standard time — 30 to 35 minutes at the classic temperature — because each square centimetre of it is having the same bake as the original. A ×0.6 batch crammed into a small, deep tin can need longer than the full-size original, despite containing barely half the mixture.

So treat any scaled bake the way you would a first bake: start checking a few minutes before you expect it to be done. The signs do not change with the tin — edges golden and pulling very slightly from the sides, the centre set and no longer glossy-wet, a gentle firmness when the middle is pressed. Our post on how to tell when flapjacks are done goes through those cues in full. A deeper mixture needs longer and a shallower one less — that is the whole law, and within it, your eyes outrank any number printed anywhere, including here.

A Worked Example: The 23cm Square Tin, Start to Finish

Suppose the tin you actually own is a 23cm square. Its base is 23 × 23, which is 529cm². Divided by the recipe's 400cm², that gives a factor of 1.3, near enough. Multiply through: the butter, golden syrup and light muscovado sugar each go from 175g to 227.5g, which you weigh as 225g without a qualm; the porridge oats go from 350g to 455g exactly. The pinch of salt stays a pinch — seasonings are the one thing that does not scale in straight lines, and a pinch judged by eye at ×1.3 is the same pinch. The temperature is the same 160°C, the depth comes out the same, the bake takes roughly the same 30 to 35 minutes with an early check, and the slab cuts into about 16 bars instead of 12.

That is the entire method, and it generalises to any recipe on this site and any tin in your cupboard: measure the base, divide by the base the recipe was written for, multiply everything by the answer. Write the factor on the recipe the first time you work it out for a tin you use often. Future you, mid-recipe with buttery hands, will be grateful.
