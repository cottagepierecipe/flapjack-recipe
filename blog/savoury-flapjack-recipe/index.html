Savoury Cheese and Vegetable Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

Take the syrup out of a flapjack and something interesting happens: the oats stop being a sweet treat and become a sturdy, cheesy bake that works in a lunchbox, alongside soup, or as a savoury snack in its own right. This version is bound with egg and melted Cheddar rather than golden syrup, filled with grated carrot or spring onions, and baked until the edges crisp. It is closer to a savoury oat slice than to the classic bar — and all the better for being honest about it.

The short answer: Mix 250g porridge oats with 125g grated mature Cheddar, 50g melted butter, 1 beaten egg, 150ml milk, 1 tsp mustard, 100g finely grated carrot or finely chopped spring onions, ½ tsp smoked paprika or dried herbs, salt and pepper. Press into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 180°C (160°C fan, Gas Mark 4) for 25–30 minutes (estimate). Cool, then cut into 8 squares. Refrigerate and eat within 3 days (estimate).

Not yet kitchen-tested: this recipe adapts our tested classic flapjack and has not been cooked by us yet. Michael tests every new recipe himself, and this page will be updated once he has cooked it. Times marked (estimate) are estimates.

What holds a savoury flapjack together: egg and cheese, not syrup

In the classic flapjack, golden syrup does two jobs at once: it sweetens, and it glues. Melted with butter and sugar, it coats every oat, and as the slab cools it sets into the firm, chewy bind that lets you cut clean bars. Remove the syrup and you remove the glue — so a savoury version has to find its binding elsewhere, or it is just warm granola in a tin.

Here, two ingredients share the job. The beaten egg sets as it bakes, holding the oats in a soft, sliceable crumb, much as it does in a frittata. The Cheddar melts into the mixture, then firms again as the bake cools, giving richness and a second, savoury kind of stickiness. The result is a different texture from a sweet flapjack — more cake-like, less chewy, with crisp edges and a tender middle. Calling it a savoury oat slice in your head, rather than expecting a cheese-flavoured version of the classic bar, sets the right expectation: it is its own bake, and a good one.

You also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper — the same tin as the classic, which keeps the depth, and therefore the baking behaviour, familiar. Our guide to flapjack tin sizes covers what changes if your tin is bigger or smaller.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Heat the oven to 180°C (160°C fan, Gas Mark 4) — a touch hotter than the sweet classic, because this mixture is wetter and benefits from crisper edges. Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving paper overhanging two sides for lifting out.

  2. Mix the dry ingredients. Put the porridge oats in a large mixing bowl with the grated Cheddar, the smoked paprika or dried herbs, a small pinch of salt and a good grinding of black pepper. Toss everything together with your hands or a spoon so the cheese is distributed through the oats rather than clumped.

  3. Prepare the vegetables. Finely grate the carrot, squeezing it lightly in your hand if it seems very wet, or finely chop the spring onions, green parts and all. Add them to the bowl and mix through.

  4. Combine the wet ingredients. Melt the butter in a small pan on the hob or in a jug in the microwave, and let it cool for a minute so it does not scramble the egg. Beat the egg with the milk and mustard in a jug, then stir in the melted butter.

  5. Bring it together and rest the mixture. Pour the wet mixture over the oats and stir until everything is evenly damp with no dry oats at the bottom of the bowl. Leave it to stand for 5 minutes: the oats drink in some of the milk, and the mixture firms up enough to press properly.

  6. Press into the tin. Tip the mixture into the lined tin, spread it into the corners and press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon. A firm, even press matters here as much as in a sweet flapjack — the egg can only bind oats that are actually touching each other.

  7. Bake for 25–30 minutes (estimate). Bake until the top is golden, the edges are crisping and slightly darker, and the centre feels set when pressed gently — it should spring back rather than leave a wet dent. A little bubbling cheese at the edges is a good sign, not a problem.

  8. Cool in the tin, then cut into 8 squares. Leave the bake to cool in the tin for at least 20 minutes. The egg and cheese firm up as it cools, and cutting too early gives ragged squares. Lift out by the paper and cut into 8 squares with a sharp knife. Serve warm, at room temperature, or cold from the fridge.

Cheese choice: mature Cheddar, grated finely

Cheese is the main flavour here, so it has to be one that survives being diluted by 250g of oats. Mature or extra mature Cheddar is the reliable choice: sharp, salty and firm enough to grate cleanly. Grate it finely rather than coarsely — fine strands melt and spread through the mixture, while coarse gratings leave a few cheesy pockets and a lot of plain oat in between. A strong Red Leicester works and gives a deeper colour; a mixture of Cheddar and a little Parmesan-style cheese, if you have some, adds a savoury depth that suits the oats. What does not work is mild, rubbery cheese: it melts, disappears, and leaves you wondering where the flavour went.

The contrast with the chewy sweet flapjack is the point: chewiness in the classic comes from syrup setting as it cools, and no savoury ingredient replicates that exactly. What the savoury version offers instead is tenderness and crisp edges — closer to a cheese scone crossed with an oat slice.

Vegetables and variations: keep them fine and dry

The vegetable's job is flavour and a little moisture, not bulk. Grated carrot adds sweetness and colour; spring onions add a gentle sharpness. Either way, the pieces must be fine — chunks of raw vegetable will still be crunchy when the oats around them are done, and wet vegetables are the enemy of the set. If you swap in courgette, grate it and squeeze it hard in a clean tea towel first; sweetcorn (a small handful, defrosted and drained) works well; finely chopped sun-dried tomatoes, well drained of oil, make it feel like a different recipe entirely. Beyond vegetables, a teaspoon of curry powder in place of the paprika, or a handful of chopped cooked bacon folded through, both suit the base — keep totals modest so the binder is not overwhelmed.

The oatcakes in the photograph are worth a mention because they are Britain's other famous savoury oat bake. A Staffordshire oatcake is a yeasted oat pancake, cooked on a griddle and folded around a filling — a completely different method from pressing oats into a tin. The savoury flapjack on this page shares their spirit (oats as a savoury staple, not just a sweet one) but none of their technique.

Storing savoury flapjacks: the fridge, because of the egg and cheese

Unlike the sweet classic, which lives happily in a tin on the counter, this bake contains egg and cheese and belongs in the fridge. Cool the squares completely, keep them in an airtight container in the fridge, and eat within 3 days (estimate). They are good cold, at room temperature (take them out half an hour before eating), or warmed through in a low oven for a few minutes to crisp the edges again — warming also revives the cheese flavour, which dulls when cold. They freeze adequately, wrapped well, for up to a month, though the texture softens a little on defrosting. The general rules in our guide to how to store flapjacks apply to the sweet bars; for this version, the fridge rule above overrides them.
