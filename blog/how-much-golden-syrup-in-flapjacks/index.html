Golden Syrup in Flapjacks: How Much Should You Use?

By Michael

Golden syrup is the ingredient that holds a flapjack together — literally. It is the glue that coats the oats, binds them into a slab and sets as the bake cools. Get the amount wrong in either direction and the texture goes with it: too little and the bars crumble, too much and they stay soft and bendy. Here is the ratio the classic recipe is built on, what happens either side of it, and how to weigh the sticky stuff without a fight.

The short answer: Work to about half the weight of the oats. The classic recipe on this site uses 175g golden syrup to 350g porridge oats — the same weight as the butter and the sugar — and that balance gives bars that hold together, chew properly and set firm. Drop much below 150g for that amount of oats and the bars crumble; push past 200g and they turn soft, very sweet and slow to set.

The classic ratio: 175g of syrup to 350g of oats, and why it works

The classic flapjack recipe on this site is built on a simple balance: 175g butter, 175g golden syrup, 175g light brown sugar and 350g porridge oats, with a pinch of salt — equal weights of butter, syrup and sugar, and twice that weight of oats. Baked in a lined 20cm square tin at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes, it makes 12 bars that cut cleanly and hold their shape in a lunchbox.

That 1:2 ratio of syrup to oats is not a magic number, but it sits in the middle of the range that works. Golden syrup does four jobs in a flapjack. It binds: melted with the butter and sugar, it coats every oat, and as the bake cools it firms up and locks the oats together. It moistens: syrup holds water in the bake, which is a large part of why flapjacks stay chewy for days rather than drying out like a biscuit. It sweetens and flavours, with a light caramel taste plain sugar does not give. And it colours: syrup browns in the oven, which is where much of the deep golden top comes from.

Because the syrup does all four jobs at once, changing the amount never changes just one thing. Less syrup also means less binding, less moisture and a paler bake; more means softer, damper and darker. The table further down maps out what to expect as you move away from 175g.

Too little syrup: the crumbly end of the scale

Reduce the syrup and the first thing you lose is the set. The butter and sugar still coat the oats, but there is not enough syrup to bridge the gaps between them, so the slab comes out fragile: it breaks along the cut lines and sheds crumbs from every edge. Bars that snap rather than bend slightly have usually been short on syrup or baked too long — the syrup fault shows in the mixing bowl too, because the mixture looks dry and will not hold when you squeeze a handful.

A modest reduction is fine. Down to about 150g against 350g of oats, the bars still hold, especially if you press the mixture into the tin very firmly and cool the slab completely before cutting — pressing and patience do some of the binding work syrup would otherwise do. Below that, you are asking the butter to do a job it cannot do alone, because butter on its own sets brittle rather than chewy.

If you are cutting syrup to reduce sweetness, cut the sugar a little too: sugar adds sweetness without binding, syrup adds both. Halving the syrup while keeping the sugar gives bars barely less sweet but far more fragile — the worst trade available. The easy flapjack recipe on this site keeps the syrup proportion sensible for exactly this reason.

Too much syrup: soft centres, dark edges and a long wait for the set

Go the other way and the bake stays soft. Extra syrup means extra liquid in the tin: the centre stays damp and bendy long after the edges are done, the slab slumps when you lift it, and the extra sugar darkens the edges faster, so a high-syrup batch can look burnt outside while still under-set in the middle. Cut too soon and the bars ooze and stick to the knife.

There is a point of diminishing returns on chew. A little extra syrup, up to about 200g against 350g of oats, gives a softer, chewier bar many people prefer, provided you cool it thoroughly — and chill it for half an hour if your kitchen is warm. Beyond roughly 225g the texture stops being chewy and becomes simply soft, and the flavour tips over: too much syrup drowns the oats and the butter.

One more symptom: a flapjack that looks greasy on top is often blamed on the butter, but excess syrup can do it too. Syrup that cannot be absorbed sits on the surface as the bake cools, giving a sticky, shiny film that reads as grease. There is a fuller rundown in why flapjacks turn out greasy.

Use the table as a dial, not a set of separate recipes. Move one step at a time and keep everything else — tin, temperature, bake time, pressing — the same, so you know what the syrup change did. And write the amount down: the difference between a good batch and a soft one can be two spoonfuls, and nobody remembers two spoonfuls.

Weigh the syrup, do not spoon it: two methods that actually work

Golden syrup is sold in tins and squeezy bottles, recipes are written in grams, and between those two facts sits the stickiest job in baking. Spooning and guessing is how amounts drift — and as the table shows, drifting by 50g changes the bake. These two methods keep it accurate.

Method one: weigh it straight into the pan. Put the saucepan on the scales, add the butter and sugar, zero the scales (the tare button) and spoon or pour the syrup in until the display reads the amount you need. Syrup pours slowly, which helps: you can creep up on the weight rather than overshooting. The squeezy bottle is easier still — squeeze slowly and stop at the number. This is how the classic recipe on this site is written, and it is the single easiest accuracy upgrade available.

Method two: the water-dip trick for tablespoons. Some recipes give syrup in tablespoons — a level tablespoon weighs roughly 20g, for reference — and if you are following one of those, dip the spoon in hot water first, shake off the drops, then take your spoonful. The hot, wet spoon slides through the syrup and lets go of it: the spoonful drops cleanly into the pan instead of half staying welded to the spoon. Re-dip between spoonfuls. The usual scrape-and-shake can leave a third of each spoonful behind, quietly turning a four-tablespoon recipe into a three-tablespoon one — the classic route to the crumbly end of the table above.

Swapping some of the syrup: honey, and recipes with no syrup at all

Golden syrup is not the only binder in the cupboard. Honey swaps in by weight — use the same grams — and brings a stronger flavour and a slightly softer set; the side-by-side comparison is in honey vs golden syrup in flapjacks. Half syrup, half honey keeps the classic texture and adds a honey note on top.

If there is no syrup in the house at all, the classic recipe still needs a liquid sweetener of some kind to bind — sugar and butter alone will not hold the oats — but there are workable routes using honey, maple syrup or a homemade sugar syrup, set out in the flapjack recipe without golden syrup. Maple syrup is much thinner than golden syrup, so a straight swap gives a wetter mixture that needs the cooling taken seriously.

Treacle is the swap to be careful with. A tablespoon of black treacle in place of one of golden syrup deepens the flavour pleasantly; a full swap makes bars that are dark, bitter-edged and slow to set. Think of treacle as a seasoning for the syrup, not a replacement.

Adjusting for add-ins: fruit, chocolate and seeds change the balance

The 175g figure assumes plain oats. Dried fruit — raisins, sultanas, chopped dates, dried apricots — absorbs moisture as it bakes, so a fruity flapjack can take a touch more syrup without going soft; an extra tablespoon or so for a batch carrying 100–150g of dried fruit is reasonable, and the fruit brings its own sweetness too. Fresh fruit is the opposite: it releases juice, so hold the syrup at the classic level or a fraction below.

Chocolate firms the set as it cools, so a chocolate-heavy batch can carry a little extra syrup and still slice cleanly. Nuts and seeds add bulk without absorbing or releasing much; treat them as extra dry ingredient and nudge the syrup up in proportion — roughly 15g more for every extra 50g of add-ins keeps the coating ratio where the classic puts it.

The reliable check is coating, not arithmetic. The mixture is right when every oat is glossy with the melted mixture and a squeezed handful holds its shape for a moment. If it looks dry and matte, it will bake dry and crumbly, whatever the numbers said; add syrup a tablespoon at a time until it shines.

Questions people ask

About 50g — half the weight of the oats — scales the classic ratio to any batch size. So 200g of oats takes roughly 100g of syrup, and 500g of oats roughly 250g. Keep the butter and sugar near the syrup weight too if you want the classic texture; heavy dried-fruit add-ins can take a small extra spoonful on top.

You can, but halve the sugar too and expect a firmer, more fragile bar — syrup is the binder, so cutting it alone removes glue as well as sweetness. A gentler step is to drop the syrup to about 150g against 350g of oats, cut the sugar by a spoonful or two as well, then press the mixture in extra firmly and cool it completely before cutting.

Both, in sequence. A modest increase — from 175g towards 200g against 350g of oats — gives a chewier, softer-centred bar. Past that the chew gives way to plain softness: the centre stays bendy and the extra sweetness starts to hide the oats. Chew also comes from not overbaking, so for chewy bars, try pulling the bake a couple of minutes earlier before reaching for more syrup.

Stand the nearly empty tin in a bowl of hot water for a few minutes; the warmth thins the syrup so it runs to one side and pours out instead of coating the tin. A flexible spatula takes most of the rest. Syrup from a warm tin also pours in a controlled stream, which makes weighing it easier.

A level tablespoon of golden syrup weighs roughly 20g, so 4 tablespoons is about 80g — but the word "roughly" is doing real work there, because spoon sizes and heaped versus level spoons vary a lot with something this thick. If the recipe matters to you, weigh it once, note the gram figure next to the recipe, and use grams from then on.

It keeps for a very long time — unopened tins sit happily in a cupboard for years. Once opened, keep the rim clean (dried syrup on the rim is what makes lids stick fast) and use a clean spoon. If crystals form in an old tin, standing it in warm water and stirring usually dissolves them back in, and the syrup is still fine to bake with.
