Lemon Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

A classic flapjack is all butter, syrup and caramel sweetness, and lemon is the flavour that cuts straight through it. This version stirs the zest of two lemons into the melted mixture — the zest carries the flavour without adding a drop of liquid — bakes and cools exactly like the classic, then finishes the cold slab with a thin, sharp lemon drizzle that sets on top.

The short answer: Melt 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado sugar. Stir in the finely grated zest of 2 lemons, then 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt. Press into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes. Cool completely in the tin, then spoon over a drizzle made from 100g icing sugar and about 1½ tablespoons of lemon juice, leave it to set (estimate), and cut into 12 bars.

Lemon flapjacks: the tested classic, with zest through it and a drizzle on top

The base here is our classic flapjack recipe, the version we have kitchen-tested: equal weights of butter, golden syrup and light muscovado sugar, twice that weight of porridge oats, a pinch of salt, baked in a lined 20cm square tin. This lemon version changes none of that. The weights are the same, the tin is the same, the oven temperature and the bake are the same. The only additions are lemon zest in the mixture and a lemon drizzle over the top once the slab is cold — flavour changes, not structural ones, which is why the classic's timings carry over unchanged.

Lemon suits a flapjack for a simple reason: the bar itself is rich and sweet from end to end, with nothing sharp in it. Lemon is sharp. The zest brings a bright, oily citrus flavour that sits against the caramel of the muscovado and syrup instead of being buried by it, and the drizzle adds a second, fresher hit of lemon on top — uncooked, so it tastes of the raw fruit. It works the way our ginger flapjack recipe works: the classic stays exactly as tested, and one strong flavour is layered through it.

The zest of two lemons does the flavouring — and adds no liquid at all

Almost all of a lemon's flavour lives in its skin. The yellow zest is full of aromatic oils, and those oils are what make lemon taste like lemon rather than just sour. Finely grated and stirred into warm melted butter, the zest gives up its oils into the fat, and the fat carries them through every oat in the pan. Two lemons give roughly two tablespoons of finely grated zest, which is enough to flavour the whole batch clearly without turning it bitter or perfumed.

Technique matters more than quantity here. Zest the lemons while they are whole and firm, before you cut them for juice — a floppy half is hard to grate and easy to grate your knuckles on. Use a fine grater and take off only the yellow part, turning the lemon as you go. The white pith underneath is bitter, and bitter is the one flavour that will spoil these bars: if you can see white on the back of your gratings, you have gone too deep. Work over a bowl so the oils that spray off the skin land in the mixture rather than on the worktop.

Why the juice goes in the drizzle and not in the mixture

It is tempting to pour lemon juice into the pan as well — more lemon, more flavour, surely. In practice it works against you twice. First, a flapjack sets because the melted syrup and sugar firm up as they cool, and that set is balanced for a mixture with no added water. Lemon juice is mostly water. Juice in the mixture means extra liquid to bake off, a softer slab, and bars that stay bendy and sticky instead of setting firm — the same problem as any flapjack that comes out too soft in the middle.

Second, heat dulls lemon juice. The fresh, sharp top notes of raw juice are delicate, and half an hour in a hot oven flattens them into a vague sourness. Zest oils survive baking far better, which is why the mixture gets zest and the juice is saved for the one part of the recipe that is never cooked: the drizzle, mixed and spooned over a cold slab, where the juice tastes exactly as it did when it left the fruit.

Choosing lemons: unwaxed fruit, heavy for their size

Because you eat the skin in this recipe — grated, but still the skin — unwaxed lemons are the better buy when you can get them. Waxed lemons zest less cleanly and the wax dulls the grater; if waxed fruit is all you have, scrub it well in hot water and dry it before zesting. Beyond that, pick lemons that feel heavy for their size, which usually means juicy fruit, with smooth, thin-looking skin rather than thick, knobbly rind: thick-skinned lemons give plenty of zest but are mostly pith and less generous with juice.

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper, a large pan for the mixture, and a fine grater — a microplane-style grater is ideal, but the fine side of a box grater does the same job more slowly. Have a small bowl ready for the zest, and juice the lemons only when the slab is cool and you are about to make the drizzle, so the juice is as fresh as it can be.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides for lifting the slab out later.
  2. Zest the two lemons. While the lemons are whole and firm, finely grate the yellow skin of both into a small bowl, turning the fruit as you go and stopping before the white pith. Set the zest aside and keep the zested lemons for juicing later.
  3. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado in a large pan over a low heat. Stir until everything has melted into one smooth liquid. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil — boiled syrup sets like toffee and makes hard bars.
  4. Stir in the lemon zest. Take the pan off the heat and stir in the grated zest. Adding it now, while the fat is warm and liquid, lets the oils in the zest spread through the whole mixture instead of clumping with the dry oats.
  5. Add the oats and salt. Tip in 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt. Stir until every oat is coated and shiny and there are no dry patches hiding at the bottom of the pan.
  6. Press into the tin. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working well into the corners. Firm pressing is what stops flapjacks crumbling later.
  7. Bake for 30–35 minutes. The top should be golden brown with the edges a shade darker, and the centre should look set but still feel slightly soft when pressed — it firms up as it cools. Lemon zest does not change the colour; judge it exactly as you would the classic.
  8. Score while warm. About 5 minutes after the tin comes out, mark the portion lines with a table knife — 12 bars — while the top is soft enough not to crack. Score before the drizzle goes on, or you will be cutting blind through it later.
  9. Cool completely in the tin. Leave the slab until it is fully cold and set — an hour at least. This matters more here than in the classic: drizzle spooned onto a warm slab melts, thins and soaks in instead of setting on top.
  10. Make the drizzle. Juice the two zested lemons. Sift 100g icing sugar into a small bowl and stir in the juice a spoonful at a time — about 1½ tablespoons in total — until the icing pours off the spoon in a slow, thick ribbon. Too thick, add juice a few drops at a time; too thin, stir in another spoonful of icing sugar.
  11. Drizzle over the cold slab and leave it to set. Spoon the icing over the cold slab in thin lines across the top, or spread it in one thin layer with the back of the spoon. Then leave the slab alone for about 30 minutes (estimate) until the surface feels dry and slightly crisp to a fingertip.
  12. Lift out and cut. Use the paper overhang to lift the set slab onto a board and cut through along the scored lines with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down. Wipe the blade between cuts so the drizzle stays in place rather than dragging.

Lemon curd: the other way to finish them

If you would rather skip the drizzle, a thin layer of lemon curd spread over the cold slab is the other natural finish. Curd is cooked lemon — eggs, butter and sugar — so it tastes richer and sweeter than the raw-juice drizzle, and it is softer: it never sets crisp, which makes curd-topped bars messier to stack and wrap. Keep the layer thin, spread it only once the slab is completely cold, and treat the bars as ones to eat from a plate rather than carry in a pocket. A jar of curd also covers you if your lemons turned out dry and the drizzle is short on juice.

Storing lemon flapjacks: eat the drizzled ones first

Keep the cut bars in an airtight tin at room temperature with baking paper between the layers. They keep about 5 days. One honest warning: icing drizzle slowly absorbs moisture from the air and from the bar beneath it, so over a few days in the tin it can turn from crisp to tacky. The bars still taste fine, but if appearance matters, serve the drizzled batch sooner rather than later. They freeze well for up to 3 months — wrap the bars individually, seal them in a bag or box and defrost at room temperature in the wrapping; the drizzle survives freezing and comes back set. There is more in our guides to how to store flapjacks and whether you can freeze flapjacks.

Questions people ask

Almost always because the slab was still warm. Drizzle on a warm flapjack melts, thins and disappears into the top layer of oats. The fix is patience: the slab needs to be fully cold — an hour at least — before any icing goes near it. A drizzle mixed too thin does the same thing on a cold slab, so aim for a slow, thick ribbon off the spoon rather than a runny stream.

Bitterness comes from the white pith under the zest, not from the lemon itself. If your gratings have white showing on the back, you pressed too deep. Zest with light strokes, turn the fruit often, and stop as soon as the yellow is off each patch. Two lemons' worth of clean yellow zest flavours the batch without any bitterness; bitter bars cannot be rescued afterwards, so care at the grater is the whole answer.

For the drizzle, bottled juice works at a pinch, though it tastes flatter and more one-note than fresh. For the mixture there is no bottled equivalent at all — the flavour there comes from zest, and bottled juice contains none. If fresh lemons are what is holding you back, know that this recipe really needs them: the zest of two fresh lemons is the main flavouring, and juice alone will not do its job.

Add the zest of a third lemon to the mixture — zest adds flavour with no liquid, so you can raise it without touching the set. Keep the drizzle as it is, made with juice only and no water, so it stays sharp. What does not work is adding juice to the mixture: it softens the bars and its flavour fades in the oven anyway, so you get soggier flapjacks that taste less of lemon, not more.

Yes, with small adjustments. Lime zest works at the same weight of fruit but limes are smaller, so use the zest of three limes and lime juice in the drizzle; the result is sharper and a little more bitter-edged. Orange is the gentle swap: the zest of one large orange in the mixture and orange juice in the drizzle gives a sweeter, softer citrus bar. Lemon stays the brightest of the three against the caramel base.

Yes. Wrap the bars individually, seal them in a bag or box and use within 3 months. The drizzle sets hard in the freezer and comes back fine if you defrost the bars at room temperature still in their wrapping, which keeps condensation off the surface. Freeze them on the day they are made if you can — the drizzle is at its crispest then, and it is the part that fades first in the tin.
