Ways to Use Broken Flapjacks and Leftover Crumbs

By Michael

Every flapjack baker knows the moment: a slab that cracks as it is cut, a batch that crumbles at the corners, a tin with one bar broken in half and a drift of golden crumbs at the bottom. None of that is waste. Broken flapjack is an ingredient — sweet, buttery, oaty and already baked — and some of the best things to do with it are things whole bars cannot do at all.

The short answer: Sort the breakage into chunks, rough pieces and fine crumbs. Chunks go over ice cream and into trifles and yoghurt pots; fine crumbs, mixed with a little melted butter, press into a cheesecake or banoffee-style base that is arguably better than a biscuit one. Small pieces re-bake into crisp granola-style clusters. Store crumbs in an airtight jar and use them within about five days, like the bars they came from — and keep them for the kitchen, not the bird table: flapjack is not suitable food for birds or pets.

First, Sort the Pieces: Chunks, Rubble and Crumbs

The uses below divide by size, so spend one minute sorting. Big chunks — half-bars, thick corners, pieces that hold together when picked up — are for spooning over things, where their chew is the point. Rubble, the pea-sized and thumbnail-sized bits that fall off every slab, is the all-purpose grade: toppings, layers, clusters. Fine crumbs, the sandy drift at the bottom of the tin and on the cutting board, are for pressing into bases and stirring into things where they should disappear into a texture rather than stand out.

The sorting takes care of itself if you work over the tin: cut the next slab on a board, tip the board's crumbs into a jar, shake the tin's rubble into the same jar, and keep the presentable chunks in a separate tub. If a batch broke because it was genuinely underbaked and soft rather than just mishandled, the pieces are still fine for bases and re-baked clusters, where they get cooked again — our post on why flapjacks fall apart covers the causes, if it keeps happening. And if a batch is burnt rather than broken, be honest: burnt crumbs carry their bitterness into everything they touch, and those ones really are for the bin.

Crumb Bases: Cheesecake and Banoffee-Style Pies

This is the finest use for fine flapjack crumbs, and the one that has converted many bakers to breaking a bar on purpose. A biscuit base is crumbs plus melted butter, pressed and chilled — and flapjack crumbs are biscuit crumbs with more to say: oats for texture, golden syrup for a deeper sweetness, and butter already in them. Pressed under a cheesecake or a banoffee-style pie, the base comes out firmer, darker and more flavourful than a plain digestive base, and it suits both fillings particularly well because the toffee note in the flapjack rhymes with the caramel in one and the richness of the other.

The method is the biscuit-base method with one adjustment: because flapjack crumbs already contain butter, they need less added. Blitz or crumble the pieces to a fine, even crumb — a food processor, or a bag and a rolling pin — then stir in melted butter a spoonful at a time until the crumbs hold together when squeezed in your fist. As a guide, start with about 25g of melted butter to 100g of fine crumbs (estimate) and add more only if the mixture still looks dry; too much butter makes a base that sets greasy and hard. Press the mixture firmly into the base of the tin with the back of a spoon, chill it until solid, and fill as your recipe directs. The base keeps its manners under both baked and chilled cheesecakes.

Over Ice Cream: The Quickest Pudding There Is

Warm chunks of flapjack over cold ice cream is a contrast that works every time: the pieces soften slightly at the edges where they meet the ice cream and stay chewy in the middle, and their syrupy sweetness does the job of a sauce as well as a topping. Vanilla is the classic partner, but anything plain — and most chocolate — works. Break the pieces small enough to eat in the same spoonful as the ice cream, and warm them for ten seconds in a microwave or a few minutes in a low oven if you want the full warm-cold effect; cold chunks straight from the tin are nearly as good and take no time at all.

The same trick stretches further. Rubble scattered over a baked fruit crumble topping adds chew to the crunch; crumbs folded through a soft-scoop ice cream before it fully freezes give a ripple of texture; and a handful of chunks pressed onto the top of a scoop in a cone, ice-cream-parlour style, makes a plain scoop feel made. None of these needs a recipe. They need a jar of pieces within reach of the freezer door, which is a fine place for the jar to live.

Yoghurt Pots, Porridge and Breakfast Layers

Flapjack is made of oats, so its crumbs are at home at breakfast. Spoon yoghurt into a glass, add a layer of rubble or chunks, a layer of fruit, and repeat: a flapjack parfait that takes two minutes and eats like a pudding. Greek-style yoghurt suits it best, because its sharpness stands up to the sweetness of the crumbs, and berries or stewed fruit between the layers keep it from being one-note.

Beyond the glass, a spoonful of fine crumbs stirred into porridge at the table adds a sweet, buttery crunch that plain oats never give themselves — add them after cooking, so they keep their texture. Crumbs scattered over baked or stewed fruit under a blanket of custard do the same job. And the rubble grade makes an excellent top layer for overnight oats: the bottom of the crumb layer softens into the oats while the top stays crisp, if you add it in the morning rather than the night before.

Trifle Layers and Pudding Toppings

A trifle is built from layers, and flapjack chunks make a layer that holds its place. Where sponge soaks up jelly and custard until it disappears, flapjack pieces absorb a little, keep their chew, and give the spoon something to meet. Use chunks rather than crumbs, keep the layer thinner than the sponge layer it replaces, and pair flavours with a thought: classic flapjack chunks under custard and cream suit a banana or pear trifle particularly well, and a chocolate-bar crumble over the top of the cream finishes it in keeping.

The crumbs also top other puddings. A fruit fool or a mousse, which can be all one soft texture, gains a proper finish from a spoonful of rubble scattered over at serving — add it at the last moment, because crumbs on anything wet soften within the hour. Baked apples and pears take a stuffing of fine crumbs mixed with a little extra dried fruit: the crumbs swell with the fruit juices as they bake and come out tasting like the inside of a flapjack that grew up. Even a plain bowl of custard is improved by a drift of crumbs across the top, which is less a recipe than a reminder that the jar on the shelf counts as an ingredient, not a tidying failure.

Re-Baked Clusters: Granola-Style, From Rubble

Rubble and small pieces can be baked a second time into something new: crisp, knobbly clusters in the style of granola. Spread the pieces in a single layer on a lined baking tray, breaking any big chunks down to thumbnail size, and bake at a moderate oven — around 150°C (130°C fan, Gas Mark 2) — for 10 to 15 minutes (estimate), turning the tray once, until the pieces are deeper gold and feel dry and crisp at the edges. They crisp further as they cool, so judge them by colour rather than hardness in the oven.

The second bake drives off moisture and re-toasts the oats, and the result keeps like a bought granola: in an airtight jar, a couple of weeks at room temperature (estimate), and longer if the pieces were baked thoroughly dry. Stirred through with dried fruit and a few seeds once cold, the clusters go over yoghurt and porridge, into trail mix for walks, or into the top of a lunchbox as a sweet-salty handful. Watch the tray near the end of the bake: crumbs have a great deal of sugar in them and move from golden to burnt quickly. A batch that catches is not rescued by picking out the dark bits — the flavour goes through it — so set a timer and stay in earshot.

Storing Crumbs, and What Crumbs Are Not For

Keep the crumb jar somewhere you will see it — a jar at the back of a cupboard is a jar that gets discovered next spring. Our guide to how to store flapjacks gives the general picture: airtight, room temperature, away from heat and damp. Crumbs dry out faster than whole bars because they have so much more cut surface, so the five days is a real guide rather than a cautious one; past that, re-baked clusters and bases are the uses that forgive staleness best.

One clear "not for" list, because it comes up whenever crumbs are discussed. Do not put flapjack crumbs out for birds, and do not feed them to pets. Flapjack is high in sugar and butter fat and is made for people; it is not suitable food for birds, whose digestion and energy needs are different, and a sticky, sugary crumb does dogs and other pets no favours either. The compost heap or the bin takes whatever the kitchen genuinely cannot use. And one kitchen honesty note: crumbs from an old batch should not be re-baked and passed off as fresh baking for a gift or a stall — re-baked clusters for your own household are a pleasure, but anything given or sold should be what its label says it is.
