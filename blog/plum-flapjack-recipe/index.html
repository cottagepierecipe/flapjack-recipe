Plum Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

Late summer brings more plums than anyone quite knows what to do with, and a flapjack tin is one of the better answers. This version keeps the tested classic exactly as it is — butter, golden syrup, muscovado sugar and oats — and lays wedges of ripe plum over the top, so the fruit softens into the bars as they bake and every slice carries a seam of sharp, jammy fruit against the sweet oats.

The short answer: Make the classic flapjack mixture: melt 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado sugar, then stir in 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt. Press into a lined 20cm square tin. Stone about 300g ripe plums, cut them into wedges and arrange them over the top, pressing them in lightly. Bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 35–40 minutes (estimate) — a few minutes longer than the classic, because the plums give off juice. Cool completely in the tin and cut into 12 bars.

Plum flapjacks: the classic recipe with about 300g of fruit on top

A plain flapjack is sweet, buttery and caramel-like, and it has no sharpness in it at all. Plums fix that. A ripe plum is sweet at the skin and properly tart near the stone, and that tartness is exactly what a flapjack mixture lacks. Baked on top of the bars, the wedges slump and soften, their edges catch a little, and their juice runs down into the top layer of oats, so the first bite of each bar is fruit and the rest is the familiar buttery mixture underneath.

The base here is our classic flapjack recipe, the version we have kitchen-tested: equal weights of butter, golden syrup and light muscovado sugar, twice that weight of porridge oats, baked in a lined 20cm square tin. The plums are an addition, not a redesign — the ratio that binds, presses and sets the classic is untouched, which matters, because fruit on top of a flapjack is a moisture question, not a structure question. Get the fruit right and the base looks after itself.

There are two ways to place the fruit. The first, and the one the method below follows, is to arrange all the wedges over the pressed mixture, where they bake in the open and some of their juice evaporates. The second is to fold half the plums through the mixture and arrange the other half on top. Folding fruit through puts more juice inside the bars, where it cannot escape, so those bars set softer still. Both work; the arranged version is the more reliable set, and the folded version is the one to try once you know how your plums behave.

Choosing plums: ripe but firm, and Victoria if you can get them

The single most important choice in this recipe is the state of the fruit. A plum that is ripe but still firm — it gives slightly when you press it, but it is not soft — will hold its wedge shape in the oven, sweeten as it bakes and give off a manageable amount of juice. A plum that is already soft and squashy is lovely eating but poor baking for this: it collapses early, floods the surface with juice and turns the top layer of oats wet rather than jammy. An under-ripe plum goes the other way — hard, sour and stubbornly pale even after baking. If your plums are under-ripe, leave them in a bowl at room temperature for a day or two rather than baking with them and hoping.

As for variety, the classic British choice is the Victoria plum: oval, red-yellow, sweet when ripe and the plum most people picture when they picture a plum at all. It bakes well and its flavour survives the oven. Greengages are sweeter and softer — delicious, but use them a touch under-ripe if you can, because they slump fast. Dark dessert plums of the kind sold in punnets all year round are the reliable everyday option: firm, tart-skinned and consistent. Sour cooking plums and damsons are a different matter — they are too sharp to sit on a sweet flapjack in wedges, though a small amount folded through a very sweet batch is a different, deliberately tart bake.

The juice question: why plum flapjacks set softer

Everything slightly tricky about this recipe comes back to one fact: plums give off juice as they bake. Heat breaks down the fruit's flesh, the juice runs, and some of it lands on and in the flapjack. That juice is water, and water in a flapjack means a softer set — the syrup that should firm up as the bars cool is diluted at the top, and the top layer of oats steams rather than crisps. This is the same behaviour we look at in our guide to why fruit flapjacks turn soggy, and the fixes there apply here too.

Three things keep the juice under control. First, put the fruit on top, in the open air of the oven, rather than buried — surface juice can evaporate as it bakes. Second, give the bake a few extra minutes: 35–40 minutes (estimate) instead of the classic's 30–35, so more of that moisture has time to leave. Third, and most important, cool the slab completely in the tin and be patient about cutting. A plum flapjack that seems too soft when warm is often simply a flapjack that has not finished setting; the syrup firms as it cools, and the fruit settles into the top rather than sitting on it. Cutting early is how a soft set becomes a fallen-apart one — if bars do crumble, our guide to why flapjacks fall apart covers the other usual causes.

One more placement point: press the wedges in lightly, cut side up. Pressing them halfway into the mixture anchors them so they do not slide when you cut the bars, and cut side up shows the fruit off and lets the surface of each wedge dry and catch slightly rather than stewing against the oats.

Stoning and cutting the plums

About 300g of plums is the right amount for a 20cm tin — enough for a wedge on every bite without piling fruit so deep that the juice overwhelms the base. Depending on size, that is roughly six to eight plums. Run a knife around each plum along its natural seam, twist the two halves apart, and pick or lever the stone out with the tip of the knife. Then cut each half into two or three wedges. Wedges, not slices: thin slices disintegrate, while a wedge keeps a piece of skin along its back, and the skin is what holds the fruit together in the heat.

If a stone clings — some varieties are clingstone rather than freestone — do not wrestle with it. Cut the flesh away from the stone in thick slabs instead, working around it with the knife. You lose a little fruit and gain wedges that are, if anything, sturdier. Check each batch of wedges over as you go and set aside any that are bruised or blemished; bruised fruit leaks more juice than sound fruit.

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper and a large pan — 350g of oats makes a bulky mixture, and a big pan makes the stirring far easier. Everything except the plums is the classic recipe exactly as tested, so if you have made the classic before, the only new work here is preparing the fruit.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides for lifting the slab out later.
  2. Stone and wedge the plums. Halve about 300g of ripe plums along the seam, twist apart and remove the stones. Cut each half into two or three wedges, keeping the skin on — the skin holds each wedge together as it bakes.
  3. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado in a large pan over a low heat. Stir until everything has melted into one smooth liquid. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil — boiled syrup sets like toffee and makes hard bars.
  4. Add the oats and salt. Take the pan off the heat and tip in 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt. Stir until every oat is coated and shiny and there are no dry patches hiding at the bottom of the pan.
  5. Press into the tin. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working well into the corners. Firm pressing is what stops flapjacks crumbling later, and a level surface gives the plums an even bed.
  6. Arrange the plum wedges. Lay the wedges over the surface cut side up, in rows, and press each one lightly into the mixture so it is anchored. Cover the surface well but do not overlap the wedges — overlapping fruit traps juice between the layers. If you prefer the half-and-half version, fold half the wedges through the mixture before pressing it into the tin, and arrange the rest on top; expect a softer set.
  7. Bake for 35–40 minutes (estimate). A few minutes longer than the classic, because the plums give off juice that needs time to bake away. The oats at the edges should be golden brown, the plum wedges softened and slumped, and any juice bubbling at the surface should look thick rather than watery. The centre will still feel soft — it firms as it cools.
  8. Score while warm. About 5 minutes after the tin comes out, mark the portion lines with a table knife — 12 bars — cutting between the rows of wedges where you can, so each bar keeps its fruit.
  9. Cool completely in the tin. Leave the slab until it is completely cold and set before cutting. This matters more here than in the classic: the fruit layer and the top oats finish setting only as the syrup cools. An hour at least, and longer does no harm.
  10. Lift out and cut. Use the paper overhang to lift the cold slab onto a board and cut through along the scored lines with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down rather than sawing, so the wedges stay in place.

Plums among the other fruit flapjacks

Plums sit at the juicy end of the fruit flapjack family. Dried fruit — raisins, apricots, dates — adds sweetness and chew and takes moisture out of a mixture rather than adding it, which is why our fruit flapjack recipe can carry a generous 200g of dried fruit without any change to the bake. Fresh fruit works the other way: it brings water with it, and in that respect plums behave more like fresh apple or rhubarb than like anything dried. If you are choosing fruit for a first fresh-fruit flapjack, plums are a good place to start precisely because the wedges sit on top where you can see them — you can watch the juice, judge the bake by eye, and learn how your oven handles fruit before trying a version where the fruit is folded out of sight.

The plum season also leaves a second option. If your tree or your market stall gives you more plums than one tin can take, the same wedges bake just as well on the next batch — but if the glut is bigger than your appetite for flapjacks, plums freeze well for later baking. Halve, stone and freeze them on a tray before bagging, and thaw and drain them thoroughly before they go anywhere near a flapjack tin, because frozen fruit releases the most juice of all.

Storing plum flapjacks

Keep the cut bars in an airtight tin at room temperature, with baking paper between the layers. They keep about 5 days, though the fruit-topped bars are at their best in the first three — the plum wedges slowly give their moisture to the oats beneath them as the bars sit, so a bar on day four is softer and jammier than the same bar on day one. That is a change in texture, not a fault, but it is worth knowing if you are baking ahead for something. They freeze well: wrap the bars individually, seal them in a bag or box and use within 3 months, defrosting at room temperature in the wrapping. There is more detail in our guides to how to store flapjacks and whether you can freeze flapjacks.
