Flapjack Toppings and Glazes: Chocolate, Caramel, Icing and Jam

By Michael

A plain flapjack is a finished thing, but a topping changes what the bar is for. Chocolate turns it into a teatime slice that cuts like a biscuit; caramel makes it a dessert; a lemon icing glaze makes it taste like a different bake altogether. The catch is that toppings are not interchangeable: each family sets differently, cuts differently and keeps differently, and the one that behaves worst in a lunchbox is not always the one you would guess. This post works through the six topping families we use — chocolate, caramel, yoghurt, icing glaze, nut butter drizzle and jam glaze — with how each one sets, cuts and stores.

The short answer: Melted chocolate is the reliable topping — it sets firm at room temperature and keeps almost as well as a plain bar. Caramel stays soft and sticky and needs an oiled knife. A simple icing glaze sets to a thin, matte shell. A jam glaze stays glossy and tacky. A nut butter drizzle never fully sets at all. And a yoghurt topping is the one exception to tin storage: it sets in the fridge and is eaten first. The table further down puts all six side by side.

Chocolate: the topping that sets

Chocolate is the topping most flapjacks meet, and it earns the popularity: it melts predictably, spreads over a whole slab in one go, and sets at room temperature into a firm layer that protects the bars underneath and makes them feel like a bought slice. About 150g of chocolate covers a 20cm tin's worth of flapjack in a layer thick enough to taste and thin enough to cut. Melt it gently — a bowl over a pan of barely simmering water, or the microwave in short bursts with stirring between — because overheated chocolate thickens and dulls, and a spoonful of water splashed into it will make it seize into grainy paste. Spread it over the cooled slab, or over one that is just warm; a hot slab melts the chocolate too thin at the edges and pools it in the middle.

Which chocolate — milk, dark or white — changes the sweetness balance, the set and the cutting, and that comparison has its own post: chocolate-topped flapjacks works through the three properly. The short version is that dark chocolate cuts the flapjack's sweetness and sets firmest, milk is the crowd-pleaser, and white is the sweetest and softest-setting of the three. Our chocolate flapjack recipe page carries the full method with the topping included.

Cutting a chocolate top without wrecking it

More chocolate toppings are spoiled at the cutting stage than at the melting stage. The two failure modes are a layer that cracks into shards because it was fully cold and hard when the knife went in, and a layer that lifts away from the slab in one sheet — a separate problem with its own causes, covered in why chocolate topping lifts off flapjacks. The reliable routine is to score the chocolate with the tip of the knife while it is setting but not yet hard, marking the portion lines through the topping only; then, once it has set, cut along the scores with a large knife warmed under the hot tap and wiped dry. The warm blade melts its way through the chocolate instead of pressing it, and the oat slab beneath cuts as it always does.

Caramel: the topping that stays soft

Caramel plays by different rules. Whether it is a toffee layer cooked from sweetened condensed milk, in the style of our millionaire's flapjack, or a softer sauce spooned over, caramel never sets hard at room temperature. It stays glossy, sticky and giving, which is glorious to eat and awkward to handle: the knife drags, the layers stick to each other in the tin, and the bars slump into each other on a warm day. Work around it rather than against it — cut with a lightly oiled knife, wipe the blade between cuts, and store the bars in a single layer or with baking paper between them. Our salted caramel flapjack and toffee flapjack posts give the two cooked versions; a bought caramel sauce warmed and spread thin is the shortcut, and it stays softer still, so treat those bars as eat-this-week baking.

Icing glazes, nut butter drizzles and jam glazes

The simplest topping of all is a plain icing glaze: icing sugar mixed with just enough water — or lemon juice, which is better — to make a paste that pours slowly off the spoon. Drizzled in zigzags or spread thin over the cooled slab, it sets within about half an hour to a matte, slightly crisp shell that cracks pleasantly when you bite. Lemon icing over a plain flapjack is a genuinely different bar: the sharpness does the work that a whole flavour change would. Keep the glaze thin; a thick one turns the top into fondant and overwhelms the oats.

A nut butter drizzle is the glaze that never sets. Warm peanut or almond butter loosened with a spoonful of syrup until it pours, zigzag it over the slab and that is the finished state — soft, sticky and rich. It is at its best over a chocolate-based or banana flapjack, and it is the topping to choose when the bars will be eaten the same day, because it transfers onto everything it touches in a tin. A jam glaze sits between the two: sieved jam, warmed until it runs, brushed over the slab gives a glossy, fruity coat that stays tacky rather than setting. Apricot jam is the baker's traditional glaze and the least sweet-tasting; raspberry or blackcurrant bring colour and sharpness. All three of these toppings go on a fully cooled slab — warm oats melt icing into the crumb and thin jam to nothing.

Yoghurt topping: the fridge exception

Every topping above lives happily in a tin at room temperature. The yoghurt topping does not. Made from thick yoghurt sweetened and stiffened, it sets in the fridge rather than on the worktop, and the finished bars are kept cold and eaten first — they are the ones to make for this weekend, not for next week's lunchboxes. In exchange it brings something none of the others can: a cool, tangy layer that cuts the flapjack's richness the way lemon icing does, only creamier. The full method, including how to stop it weeping, is in our yoghurt-topped flapjack recipe. Do not freeze yoghurt-topped bars — the topping splits and weeps as it defrosts.

Underneath all of them, the keeping rules for the flapjack itself do not change: an airtight tin at room temperature for about 5 days, and up to 3 months in the freezer wrapped — the full picture is in how to store flapjacks. Choose the topping by the job. For a bake sale or a gift, chocolate — it travels, stacks and survives. For a dessert, caramel or a yoghurt top. For the lightest possible change to a plain bar, a lemon glaze. And when in doubt, drizzle rather than cover: half the topping, most of the effect, and the oats still taste like the main event.

Questions people ask

Cooled, or nearly. A just-warm slab is fine for chocolate — the last of the heat helps it spread — but a hot one thins chocolate out at the edges, melts icing glaze straight into the crumb and turns jam to syrup. Caramel cooked in a pan goes on warm by its nature. For everything else, let the slab cool in the tin first; the topping will sit on top of the bar instead of soaking into it, which is the difference between a topped flapjack and a sticky one.

Cutting straight from the fridge is the other common cause: ten minutes on the worktop first makes a real difference.
