Why Do Flapjacks Stick to Baking Paper?

By Michael

You lined the tin so this would not happen. Yet when the slab comes out, the paper is welded to the bottom of the bars, tearing away in strips or refusing to move at all. Flapjacks are harder on baking paper than almost any other bake — and once you know why, the fix is mostly about which paper you reach for.

The short answer: Flapjacks stick to baking paper because their binding is hot sugar syrup, and hot syrup behaves like glue: it soaks into the fibres of the paper and sets hard around them as it cools. The problem is worst with greaseproof paper, which has no non-stick coating, and with slabs peeled while still warm and soft, or left sitting on their paper in the tin for days. Use proper baking parchment — the silicone-coated sort — with an overhang to lift by, cool the slab completely, and peel the paper away from the slab rather than the other way round.

Hot Syrup Is Glue: It Soaks Into the Paper and Sets Around the Fibres

Most bakes release from paper because what touches the paper is batter or dough, and both set into a crumb that has no interest in the paper at all. A flapjack is different. What touches the paper is the syrup mixture itself — melted butter, golden syrup and sugar — pressed down into every corner in a thin, even layer. While it is hot, that mixture is a runny, sugary liquid, and paper is absorbent. The liquid wicks into the paper's fibres, and as the slab cools the sugar sets solid around those fibres. The paper is no longer under the flapjack; it is embedded in it.

This is why sticking is always worst at the bottom of the slab and barely happens at the sides. The base carries the full weight of the mixture pressing the syrup into the paper for the whole bake, and it is where the syrup collects. It is also why our classic flapjack recipe — 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado sugar to 350g porridge oats — will stick a little to almost anything if it is handled warm: the glue is built into the recipe. The aim is not to stop the syrup bonding at all, but to give it a surface it cannot grip, and to separate the two at the moment the slab is firm enough to allow it.

Greaseproof Paper Is Not Baking Parchment, and Syrup Knows the Difference

The commonest cause of a welded slab is the packet. Greaseproof paper and baking parchment look similar, sit next to each other in the shop, and are treated as the same thing in many kitchens. They are not. Greaseproof paper is paper treated to resist grease soaking through it, but its surface is still paper — absorbent, fibrous, and perfectly capable of grabbing hot syrup. Baking parchment is paper coated with a thin layer of silicone, and that coating is the non-stick part: syrup can sit on it for a whole bake and still let go when cold, because it never got into the fibres.

With a sponge cake, you may never notice the difference. With a flapjack, you will notice it the first time. If your paper tears away in scraps and leaves half of itself on the base of the bars, check what the packet actually says — a large share of "baking paper" sold is greaseproof or only lightly treated, and flapjacks find it out. Our guide to how to line a flapjack tin goes through the lining itself, folds, corners and all; this page is about what the lining is made of.

Peeling at the Wrong Moment: Warm Slabs Tear, Stored Slabs Weld

Timing does the rest of the damage. A flapjack slab is at its softest and stickiest in the first hour out of the oven, while the butter is still liquid and the syrup has not finished setting. Try to peel paper away then and two things go wrong at once: the paper pulls soft flapjack apart with it, and the unset syrup smears and re-grips. The slab needs to be completely cold and set before the paper moves. Pressed and scored while warm, yes — that is the right time to mark the portions — but the paper stays put.

The opposite mistake also sticks paper fast: leaving the slab sitting on its paper for days. A cold slab stored in the tin on the paper it baked on keeps taking up and giving off tiny amounts of moisture, the sugar at the interface softens and re-sets, and the paper gradually welds itself to the base. Lift the slab out once it is cold, peel the paper off there and then, and store the bars without it — paper between the layers of stored bars is clean, dry paper, not the sheet that came out of the oven.

No Overhang, No Lift: Paper Cut to Size Traps the Slab in the Tin

Some sticking is really a lifting problem. If the paper is cut to fit the base exactly, there is nothing to grip, and the only way out is to prise at an edge with a knife — which digs into the soft bottom corner, breaks the seal unevenly, and tears paper and flapjack together. Lining with a sheet that runs up two opposite sides and hangs over the edges turns the paper into a sling: the cold slab lifts straight out in one piece, and peeling then happens on a board, where you can see what you are doing.

The sling only works if the paper stays where you put it while the mixture goes in. Dry paper in a dry tin skids around and folds into the corners, and a fold of paper baked into the side of the slab sticks as surely as the base does. A light greasing of the tin under the paper — a smear of butter on your fingers or a brush of oil — holds the sheet in place while you press the mixture in. You are greasing the tin to anchor the paper, not greasing the paper to release the bars; the parchment's coating does that job on its own.

Very Syrupy or Underbaked Batches Stick Worst of All

Paper choice and timing cover most cases, but the mixture itself has a vote. A batch with more syrup than the recipe calls for — or a heavy hand with honey or treacle swapped in — puts more free sugar liquid against the paper for longer, and it grips harder. An underbaked slab sticks for a different reason: the syrup at the base never reached its set, so it is still a tacky glue when you peel, and it takes paper fibres with it whichever paper you used. If a slab was soft in the middle as well as stuck at the base, the sticking was a symptom. Our post on why flapjacks are too soft covers that bake; a properly set slab releases from parchment almost as a matter of routine.

The reverse — a slab baked long and hard — releases easily and is no bargain either, since you have traded sticking for hardness. The target is the ordinary one: edges golden, centre just set, at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3), cooled right down. That slab, on parchment, peels clean.

How to Rescue a Slab That Is Stuck Fast

Start with warmth, applied from underneath. A stuck slab is held by set sugar, and set sugar softens with heat: sit the tin on a hot, damp tea towel for five minutes, or put the whole tin back into a low oven — 120°C (100°C fan, Gas Mark ½) — for three or four minutes. You are not baking it again, just softening the thin layer of syrup at the interface. Then lift the slab out by its overhang if it has one, turn it paper-side up on a board, and peel slowly, keeping the paper bent back low and flat against itself so the slab stays level.

The technique matters more than the force. Peel the paper away from the slab — paper bending, slab flat — rather than pulling the slab off the paper. Work from one corner, steadily, and if a patch resists, warm that corner again rather than tearing. Small scraps that stay behind on a bar can be picked off with a fingernail once the bar is cold; anything stubborn usually lets go after the bars have had an hour in the fridge, when the syrup is at its hardest and least tacky. And be honest about the outcome: a bar with a picked-at base tastes exactly the same as a clean one. Sticking is a cosmetic failure, not a baking one. If pieces broke away during the struggle, our post on why flapjacks fall apart has ways to put the crumbs to use.

How to Stop Flapjacks Sticking to the Paper Next Time

The checklist is short. Buy baking parchment and check the packet for a silicone coating — that one swap ends most sticking. Line the tin with an overhang on two sides so the paper works as a sling. Anchor the sheet with a light greasing of the tin underneath it, so it cannot skid or fold into the mixture. Press the mixture in firmly, bake the slab until the edges are golden and the centre is just set, and score the portions while it is warm.

Then wait. Let the slab cool completely in the tin — properly cold, right through — lift it out by the overhang, turn it over, and peel the paper away from the slab in one slow movement. Take the paper off before the bars are stored, and store them with fresh paper between the layers instead. Do that, and the paper becomes what it was always supposed to be: the thing that makes flapjacks easier, not the last obstacle between you and them.

Questions people ask

Pick the paper off — that is the practical answer. Baking paper is made for contact with food at oven temperatures, so a stray scrap is a texture problem rather than a danger, but it is unpleasant and chewy in all the wrong ways, so it is worth the few seconds with a fingernail. Chilling the bar for an hour makes stuck scraps brittle and much easier to flick away cleanly.

With genuine silicone-coated parchment, you do not need to — the coating is the release layer, and the slab peels from it when cold. Greasing does no harm and a few bakers do it out of habit, but if your paper needs greasing to let go, that is a sign it is greaseproof rather than parchment. Greaseproof paper is the one that genuinely benefits from a thorough greasing.

Pressure and gravity. The base carries the weight of the whole slab pressing syrup into the paper for the full bake, and runny syrup collects at the bottom before it sets. At the sides the paper only touches the mixture lightly, and the slab also shrinks very slightly away from the tin as it cools, which breaks what little grip there was. All the glue ends up underneath, which is why the bottom is where peeling technique matters.

You can, greased well — foil is the traditional stand-in and many older recipes assume it. Grease it all over, including up the sides, and avoid pressing the mixture hard into folds and corners, because syrup baked into a fold of foil locks there. Expect a slightly firmer, darker base than paper gives, since foil conducts heat straight into the slab. Parchment is still the kinder option.

A sheet of silicone-coated parchment that released cleanly can usually line one more bake — the coating survives, and a few syrup marks do no harm. Greaseproof paper that has had a flapjack on it is done; once syrup has soaked into the fibres and set, the sheet is stiff, patchy and more likely to stick next time. Never reuse the sheet the slab was stored on, if it sat there — that one has welded and torn already.

Work through the short list: the slab was probably peeled while still warm in the middle, or it was underbaked so the syrup at the base never set, or — commonest of all — the packet said parchment but the paper was a lightly treated greaseproof. A properly baked slab, cooled right through, peels from real silicone-coated parchment almost without resistance. If all three were right and it still stuck, chill the slab for an hour and peel again; cold, hard syrup lets go more cleanly than syrup at room temperature.
