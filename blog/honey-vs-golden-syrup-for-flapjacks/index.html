Honey vs Golden Syrup in Flapjacks

By Michael

They pour alike, they weigh alike, and they sit next to each other in most baking cupboards — so can honey simply stand in for golden syrup in a flapjack? Up to a point, yes, but the two sweeteners bake very differently, and knowing how is the difference between a bar with a delicate floral chew and one that catches at the edges and never quite sets.

The short answer: Golden syrup is the reliable classic: mild caramel flavour, a firm, clean set, and forgiving behaviour in the oven. Honey gives a flapjack a flavour of its own — floral, sometimes almost fruity — but it is sweeter-tasting, runnier, browns faster and sets softer, so it is not a gram-for-gram swap. A recipe built for honey, like ours, uses a little less of it and keeps some sugar in the mix: 200g of honey plus 75g of sugar for 325g of oats.

Golden Syrup in Flapjacks: The Binder the Recipe Was Built On

Golden syrup has been part of British baking since the 1880s, and the flapjack grew up with it. It is a partly inverted sugar syrup, which in kitchen terms means two things: it stays smooth instead of crystallising as it cools, and it sets to a firm, clean chew rather than a sticky pull. In our classic flapjack recipe it works alongside butter and light brown sugar — 175g of each — to coat 350g of porridge oats and glue them into a slab that cuts neatly and keeps about a week.

Its flavour is deliberately mild: a light, buttery caramel that sweetens without competing. That neutrality is why golden syrup is the default in so many versions of the bar — fruit, chocolate, nuts or spices can be added and the syrup simply holds them. It is also forgiving in the oven: at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) it colours slowly and evenly, giving you a wide window between "pale and soft" and "too dark".

Two more practical points. Golden syrup is vegan — it is made from sugar, full stop — where honey is not. And it is consistent: one tin behaves like the next, while honeys vary from jar to jar, which matters when you are trying to repeat a result.

Honey in Flapjacks: Sweeter, Runnier and Quicker to Brown

Honey is not one flavour but hundreds. A mild supermarket blend gives a gentle floral sweetness; a dark heather or chestnut honey can be almost malty. That character is the whole reason to use it — a honey flapjack tastes of honey in a way a syrup flapjack never tastes of syrup. But honey brings three baking behaviours you have to manage.

It tastes sweeter. Honey's sugars are largely fructose and glucose rather than sucrose, and fructose registers sweeter on the tongue. The same weight of honey makes a bar that tastes noticeably sweeter than the syrup version, which is why honey recipes trim the total sweetener rather than swapping straight across.

It is runnier, and sets softer. Honey carries more water than golden syrup and holds on to moisture in the finished bar. A honey flapjack is a softer, bendier bar that never firms up to the classic's clean snap at the edges — lovely if you like a chewy bar, disappointing if you wanted a firm one.

It browns faster. Honey's natural sugars colour quickly in the oven, so a honey batch at classic temperature can be dark at the edges while the middle is still loose. Honey flapjacks want a few degrees less heat and an earlier check — our honey flapjack recipe bakes at the same 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) but is watched from 25 minutes and taken out while the centre is still soft.

Read down the table and the pattern is clear: golden syrup is the steadier ingredient, honey the more characterful one. Neither is "better" — they are answers to different questions, which is why the verdict at the end of this page is about what you want from the bar, not which tin wins.

Swapping Honey for Golden Syrup: Why It Is Not Gram-for-Gram

The commonest failed swap goes like this: a baker replaces the 175g of golden syrup in the classic with 175g of honey, keeps everything else, and gets bars that are too sweet, too soft and too dark at the edges. Every one of those faults is predictable from the table above. To swap successfully, change three things at once.

Use a little less honey. Because honey tastes sweeter, you do not need the full weight. Our honey recipe is built on 200g of runny honey with the sugar cut right back to 75g, for 325g of oats — compare the classic's 175g of syrup plus 175g of sugar for 350g of oats, and you can see how much sweetener honey lets you leave out.

Keep some sugar in the recipe. Sugar is what firms the set as the slab cools. A honey-only mixture stays loose; the 75g of light brown sugar in our recipe is there to give the bars back their structure. This is the step most swaps skip, and the one that matters most.

Bake it gently and judge it early. Take the tin out when the edges are deep golden and set but the centre still looks soft and slightly underdone — it firms as it cools. If your oven runs hot, drop the temperature by 10°C for honey batches. If you would rather not rebuild a recipe at all, our flapjack recipe without golden syrup works through the alternatives one by one.

Honey in Flapjacks: The Two Rules That Are Not About Taste

First, and firmly: babies under 12 months must not have honey in any form, including baked into a flapjack. Honey can contain bacteria linked to infant botulism, a rare but serious illness, and ordinary baking does not remove that risk. This is NHS guidance, and it applies to every honey recipe on this site without exception. Children over 12 months can have honey flapjacks as an occasional treat, like any sweet bar.

Second, honey is not a health swap. It is still a sugar, and a honey flapjack is a sweet treat in exactly the same way a syrup one is. Swapping changes flavour and baking behaviour; it does not turn the bar into a health food. If a lighter bar is what you are after, our healthy flapjack recipe — sweetened with banana and dates rather than syrup or honey — is the honest route.

Measuring and Melting Honey and Syrup: Two Very Different Pours

Both sweeteners are famously awkward to measure, and the weighing trick is the same for both: set the tin or jar on the scales, note the weight, pour or spoon out what you need, and weigh again — the difference is what left the container, and nothing sticky has touched a spoon or a jug. If you prefer a spoon, dip it in hot water first and the sweetener slides off cleanly. Set honey that will not pour at all can be spooned straight into the pan once the butter has begun to melt; the warmth frees it from the spoon.

In the pan they part company. Golden syrup is thick and unhurried — it takes a minute to loosen and merge with the melting butter and sugar, and it tolerates that minute calmly. Honey loosens almost at once and, being thinner and higher in fast-colouring sugars, is the one to watch: keep the heat low, stir steadily, and take the pan off the moment everything is liquid and glossy. The rule that governs both is the oldest one in flapjack making — melt, never boil. A boiled butter mixture, whether it was sweetened with honey or syrup, sets like toffee and turns the finished bars hard; it is the first cause in our guide to why flapjacks turn out hard.

The Verdict: Which Sweetener for Which Flapjack

Choose golden syrup when you want the classic bar: firm-chewy, evenly golden, the flavour of oats and butter to the front, and a recipe that behaves the same way every time. It is also the only choice of the two for vegan baking. Choose honey when the honey itself is the point — when you have a jar with real character and want a softer, more fragrant bar, baked gently and eaten within a few days while its aroma is freshest. And consider the honest middle path: half honey, half syrup in the classic gives a bar with a whisper of honey flavour, most of syrup's reliable set, and none of the swapping headaches. Many experienced bakers land there and stay.
