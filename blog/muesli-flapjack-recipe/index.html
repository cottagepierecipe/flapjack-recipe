Muesli Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

Muesli is already most of a flapjack before you start: rolled oats mixed with dried fruit, nuts and seeds. This recipe simply takes the tested classic flapjack mixture and swaps its plain porridge oats for the same weight of muesli, so the fruit and nuts arrive built in. It is the quickest route to a varied, chewy bar — provided you choose the right muesli and press the tin harder than you think you need to.

The short answer: Melt 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 125g light muscovado sugar. Stir in 350g oat-heavy muesli and a pinch of salt. Press very firmly into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes. Cool completely in the tin and cut into 12 bars.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Muesli flapjacks: the classic base with a breakfast mix in place of the oats

The base here is our classic flapjack recipe, the version we have kitchen-tested: butter, golden syrup and light muscovado sugar melted together, oats stirred in, pressed into a 20cm tin and baked until golden. The classic uses plain porridge oats, which bake into a smooth, even bar. Muesli changes the texture, not the structure. The syrup and butter still do the binding, the tin is the same, and the bake is the same — but every bite now meets something different: a raisin here, a hazelnut there, a seed or a flake of coconut. It is less uniform than the classic and, to most people who try both, more interesting.

There is one real adjustment, and it is to the sugar. The classic carries 175g of light muscovado, balanced for plain oats, which bring almost no sweetness of their own. Muesli is a different starting point: its dried fruit is concentrated sugar, and many boxed mueslis are sweetened on top of that. Keeping the full 175g on top of all that fruit makes a bar that tastes of sugar first and everything else second. So this recipe trims the muscovado to 125g. That is a judgement, not a tested rule — mueslis vary too much for one fixed number — but 125g is the sensible middle: sweet enough to read as a flapjack, restrained enough that the fruit and nuts still taste of themselves. If your muesli is an unsweetened, oat-and-seeds style with little fruit, you can put the sugar back up towards 150g. If it is a sweetened, fruit-heavy mix, 125g is plenty and some bakers will want less.

Choosing the muesli: oat-heavy, loose, and with no big clusters

Not every muesli bakes well, and the differences are easy to spot before you buy. The best choice is an oat-heavy muesli where rolled oats are clearly the main ingredient and the fruit, nuts and seeds are mixed through in modest pieces — that kind behaves most like the porridge oats it replaces. Look at the bag or the ingredient list: oats first, and a mix that pours loosely rather than sitting in lumps.

The one to avoid is a muesli built around big clusters. Clusters are oats and nuts already glued together with sugar, oil or honey before they reach your tin, and they cause two problems. They are sweet in patches, so the finished bar is uneven, and they are chunky, so the mixture will not press into a flat, even slab — the cluster pieces hold the surface up in bumps and leave weak, crumbly gaps between them. Very nut-heavy mueslis have a version of the same problem: whole nuts are large, hard inclusions in a bar that sets by syrup gripping flakes, and too many of them interrupt the set. A muesli with chopped or flaked nuts bakes far better than one full of whole almonds and Brazil nuts.

If you are curious about the oats themselves — porridge oats versus jumbo, and why the size of the flake changes how a bar binds — our guide to the best oats for flapjacks covers it in full. The short version matters here too: small, thin flakes bind best, which is why an oat-heavy muesli with ordinary rolled oats outperforms a chunky one.

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper and a large pan — 350g of muesli is bulky, and a big pan makes it far easier to coat every piece. Before you weigh the muesli, give the bag or box a good shake or stir: the fruit and seeds sink and settle in storage, and a scoop taken from the top of an unshaken bag is mostly oats, while the last scoop is mostly raisins. Shake first so what you weigh is the mix as intended.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides for lifting the slab out later.
  2. Shake and weigh the muesli. Shake or stir the muesli well so the fruit, nuts and seeds are evenly spread through the oats, then weigh out 350g. Break up any clusters with your fingers as you go.
  3. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 125g light muscovado in a large pan over a low heat. Stir until everything has melted into one smooth liquid. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil — boiled syrup sets like toffee and makes hard bars.
  4. Take the pan off the heat. The melted mixture should be glossy and pourable, with no grains of sugar left. If any sugar is still visible, give it another minute of gentle heat and stirring.
  5. Add the muesli and salt. Tip in the 350g of muesli and a pinch of salt. Stir thoroughly until every flake, fruit piece and nut is coated and shiny and there are no dry pockets at the bottom of the pan. This takes a little longer than with plain oats — the mixture is lumpier, and it is worth folding it over several times.
  6. Press into the tin, harder than you think. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin, spread it level, then press down very firmly with the back of a spoon or the base of a glass, working into the corners. Plain oats pack into a smooth mat; muesli pieces do not, and firm pressing is what closes the gaps between them.
  7. Bake for 30–35 minutes. The top should be golden, the edges a shade darker, and the centre should look set but still feel slightly soft when pressed — it firms up as it cools. Fruit pieces sitting on the surface may look quite dark; that is the fruit's own sugar colouring, not the bar burning.
  8. Score while warm. About 5 minutes after the tin comes out, mark the portion lines with a table knife — 12 bars — while the top is soft enough not to crack.
  9. Cool completely in the tin. Leave the slab until it is cold and set before cutting. A muesli flapjack has more weak points than a plain one, and cutting it warm is where bars come apart. An hour at least, and longer does no harm.
  10. Lift out and cut. Use the paper overhang to lift the cold slab onto a board and cut through along the scored lines with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down rather than sawing — sawing drags nuts and fruit through the bar and tears it.

Pressing: the step that decides whether a muesli flapjack holds together

A plain oat flapjack is forgiving about pressing because porridge oats are thin, flat flakes that overlap like tiles; even a lightly pressed slab has thousands of contact points for the syrup to grip. Muesli is not built that way. A hazelnut touches the mixture at a few points. A raisin is round. A seed is tiny and smooth. Between all these rounded, uneven pieces there are air gaps, and a gap in the raw mixture becomes a fault line in the cut bar — the place where it snaps or crumbles when lifted.

That is why step 6 asks for more pressure than the classic does, and why the cooling step matters even more here. Press in two passes: once to spread and level, then again, firmly, corner to corner, once the mixture has settled for a minute. If the top still shows loose pieces sitting proud, press those down individually with the back of the spoon. And when the bars do cut, if the odd one crumbles at a nut, that is the muesli being muesli rather than a fault in the bake — the crumbs are excellent over yoghurt. Our guide to why flapjacks fall apart works through the other causes, most of which come back to under-baking or cutting too soon.

Storing muesli flapjacks

Keep the cut bars in an airtight tin at room temperature with baking paper between the layers, and they keep about 5 days. One muesli-specific point: exposed nuts and seeds slowly soften in the tin as they take up moisture from the bar around them, so these bars are at their most varied in texture in the first two or three days. They freeze well too — wrap the bars individually, seal them in a bag or box and use within 3 months, defrosting at room temperature in the wrapping. There is more detail in our guide to how to store flapjacks and how long they last.
