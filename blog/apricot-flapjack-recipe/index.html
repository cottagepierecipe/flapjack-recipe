Apricot Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

Dried apricots are the flapjack fruit that fights back. Where raisins and dates melt quietly into the sweetness, apricot keeps a gentle sharpness that cuts through all that golden syrup, so every bite tastes balanced rather than simply sweet. This recipe adds 175g of chopped dried apricots to our tested classic and changes almost nothing else.

The short answer: Add 175g of dried apricots, chopped small, to the classic flapjack recipe and bake as usual for 30 to 35 minutes. Chop the fruit small so it spreads through every bar. If your apricots are firm rather than soft, soak them for 10 minutes in hot water first, then drain them and dry them well before they go in.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Why apricots work in flapjacks: sharp fruit against sweet syrup

A classic flapjack is a sweet thing — butter, sugar and syrup around plain oats — and most dried fruits only make it sweeter. Apricots are different. Even dried, they keep a real tang, and in the oven that tang softens into something jammy and bright rather than sugary. Set against the deep caramel of muscovado and golden syrup, it is the difference between a bar that tastes of one note and a bar that tastes of two.

The fruit behaves well in the bake, too. Chopped small, the pieces soften in the syrupy mixture without melting away, so you get distinct pockets of apricot with a slight chew of their own. Because the pieces are spread through the whole slab, the sharpness lands in every bar rather than in occasional mouthfuls. And it does one practical job: the edges of exposed fruit caramelise and darken a little faster than the oats, which is worth knowing about when you judge the bake — there is a section on that below.

Drying is what concentrates all of this. A fresh apricot is mostly water; a dried one is the same fruit with the water gone and the flavour — sweet and sharp alike — packed into half the size. It is also why dried is the right form for this recipe, however good the fresh fruit looks in the shops.

Choosing dried apricots: soft, firm, orange or brown

Any dried apricots you would happily eat from the bag will bake well. The soft, ready-to-eat kind can go straight in after chopping. Firmer, chewier apricots — the sort sold loose or in baking packs — benefit from the short soak in the method, which plumps them up so they stay tender in the finished bars instead of turning leathery at the edges.

Colour is the other thing you will notice on the shelf. The bright orange apricots are treated with sulphur dioxide, which preserves the colour as they dry. Unsulphured apricots are a deep brown, sometimes almost chocolate-coloured, with a slightly richer, more toffee-like flavour. Both work in this recipe at the same weight; the unsulphured ones simply give you darker flecks in the bars rather than orange ones. Check the bag for any "ready to eat" wording — that is your clue that no soak is needed.

What not to use: fresh apricots, tinned apricots, or apricot jam stirred through the mixture. All three bring water with them, and water is the enemy of a flapjack's set. If you have fresh apricots to use up, eat them as they are and buy the dried sort for baking.

Preparing the fruit: chop small, soak only if firm

Size is the one detail that decides how good these bars are. Aim for pieces roughly the size of a large raisin — about 5mm to 1cm across. At that size the fruit spreads evenly, every slice of the slab gets some, and the knife passes through cleanly when you cut. Left in halves, the apricots form big chewy lumps, drag under the knife, and leave whole bars with no fruit in them at all. A pair of kitchen scissors is often easier than a knife for the job, because dried apricots are sticky and cling to a blade.

The soak is optional and depends entirely on your fruit. If the apricots feel firm and dry, cover the chopped pieces with hot water for 10 minutes, then drain them and pat them dry with kitchen paper or a clean tea towel. That drying-off step matters: fruit that goes in wet carries water into the mixture, and the bars will set softer for it. Soft ready-to-eat apricots skip the soak altogether — they are already where the soaked ones end up.

Ingredients

This is the tested classic flapjack — same butter, syrup, sugar and oats, same 20cm tin — with the apricots stirred through at the end. Twelve bars, as the classic makes.

IngredientAmountNotes
Unsalted butter175gThe classic fat, unchanged
Golden syrup175gWeigh it — it is the glue of the recipe
Light muscovado sugar175gLight brown soft sugar is the closest swap
Porridge oats350gStandard rolled porridge oats
Dried apricots175gChopped small, about raisin-sized; soaked and dried first if firm
Fine sea saltA pinchBalances the sweetness

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving an overhang on two sides so the finished slab lifts out easily.
  2. Chop the apricots. Cut the 175g dried apricots into small pieces, roughly raisin-sized. Scissors make light work of it. If the fruit is firm, soak the pieces in hot water for 10 minutes now, then drain and pat them properly dry.
  3. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put the 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado in a large saucepan over a low heat. Stir until everything is liquid and the sugar has dissolved. Keep it gentle and do not let it boil — boiled syrup sets hard and will take the bars with it.
  4. Take the pan off the heat. Once the mixture is smooth and glossy, the heat's work is done.
  5. Stir in the oats, salt and apricots. Add the 350g porridge oats, the pinch of salt and all the chopped apricots. Stir thoroughly, turning the mixture over until the fruit is spread evenly and every oat is coated — check the bottom of the pan, where dry patches like to hide.
  6. Press into the tin. Tip the mixture into the lined tin and press it down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, right into the corners. A level surface bakes evenly, and a firm press is what holds the bars together.
  7. Bake for 30 to 35 minutes. Bake until the edges are golden and the centre looks set but still a touch soft — it firms as it cools. Look at the fruit as well as the oats: apricot pieces at the edges darken faster than everything else, so judge the bake by the oats and the overall colour, and check from 25 minutes if your oven runs hot.
  8. Mark the cutting lines while warm. Five minutes out of the oven, score twelve bars with a table knife while the top is soft. Do not cut through yet — warm flapjack crumbles.
  9. Cool completely in the tin. Leave the slab until it is fully cold and firm. The fruit pieces set into the bars as they cool, and cutting too early drags them out of place.
  10. Lift out and cut into 12. Lift the slab out on its paper and cut firmly along the scored lines with a sharp knife. Small pieces of apricot may catch the blade — a steady sawing motion deals with them.

Edges and colour: fruit sugars brown faster

The single most likely hiccup with this recipe is mistaking dark fruit for a burnt bake. Apricots carry a lot of natural sugar, and pieces sitting at the surface — especially near the edges and corners, where the tin is hottest — caramelise sooner than the oat mixture around them. A few deep-brown apricot pieces are normal and taste pleasantly toffee-like; they are not a reason to pull the tin out early and leave the middle underbaked.

Judge doneness the way you would for the classic: the slab should look set and golden overall, with the centre just slightly soft. If your oven has a hot spot and one corner always races ahead, turn the tin round halfway through the bake. And if the fruit on top is darkening quickly while the middle still looks pale, the chop was probably too big — smaller pieces sit down in the mixture instead of proud of it, and behave much better.

Variations: zest, almond and a second fruit

The apricot version takes kindly to small additions. The finely grated zest of an orange, stirred in with the oats, leans into the fruit's sharpness. A tablespoon of flaked almonds scattered over the top before baking gives a gentle crunch, in the direction of a Bakewell. And apricot pairs happily with a second dried fruit at the same total weight — half apricot, half date is particularly good, the dates adding back some of the deep sweetness the apricots hold in check; our date flapjack recipe works the same trick from the other direction.

Keep any variation modest. The ratio of syrup, sugar and oats is what sets the bars, and the fruit is already an addition the classic was not built around — much more than 175g of fruit starts to crowd out the oats that give the bars their body.

Storing apricot flapjacks

Keep the cut bars in an airtight tin at room temperature. The fruit helps them stay moist, and they should keep well for about 5 days — like most fruit flapjacks, they are often at their best on the second day, once the flavours have settled into each other. There is no need to refrigerate them; a cool cupboard is fine. If a bar feels at all dry after a few days, ten seconds in the microwave brings the syrup back to softness.
