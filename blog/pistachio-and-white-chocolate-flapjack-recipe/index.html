Pistachio and White Chocolate Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

A pistachio and white chocolate flapjack splits its chocolate in two: half the white chocolate chips are folded through the oat mixture with chopped pistachios, and the other half is melted and drizzled over the cooled bake. White chocolate scorches easily, which is exactly why half of it never goes in the oven — and the green nuts scattered through the drizzle are the finish.

The short answer: Melt 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado sugar in a pan, stir in 300g porridge oats, 75g chopped pistachios, 50g white chocolate chips and a pinch of salt, press into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes. Cool completely, melt the remaining 50g of white chocolate, drizzle it over the top, scatter a few extra chopped pistachios if you like, leave to set and cut into 12 bars.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Pistachio and white chocolate flapjacks: sweet chocolate, salted nuts

White chocolate divides people. It is the sweetest of the chocolates — cocoa butter, sugar and milk solids, with none of the cocoa bitterness that keeps dark chocolate in check — and in a bake that is already built on syrup and sugar it can tip the whole thing into cloying. The answer in this recipe is the pistachio. Pistachios are savoury, faintly salty and properly nutty, and they cut against the white chocolate the way orange zest cuts against dark chocolate in our chocolate orange bars. Each flavour makes the other taste more like itself.

The base is our classic flapjack recipe with 50g of the oats swapped for the nuts and chips. The arrangement of the chocolate is the recipe's real idea, and it comes from a practical problem: white chocolate is fragile in heat. What goes through the mixture is there for pockets of sweetness in the crumb; what goes over the top is melted gently after the bake, where it cannot scorch, and drizzled rather than spread, so it stays a finish rather than becoming a second, sweeter bar sitting on the first. The chopped green nuts are not decoration, exactly — they are the clearest signal of what is inside the bar.

White chocolate scorches: why half of it never sees the oven

Dark chocolate tolerates a flapjack bake without complaint. White chocolate does not. Its milk solids and high sugar content make it brown and catch at temperatures dark chocolate shrugs off, and chips folded through a flapjack mixture come out of a 30-minute bake paler in flavour and sometimes faintly caramelised at the edges of the tin — pleasant enough, but not the clean sweetness you put them in for. The chips in the middle of the slab are insulated by the oats around them and survive well; it is the ones near the surface that suffer. Splitting the chocolate solves the problem neatly: 50g of chips through the mixture for pockets of sweetness, accepting that the topmost few will colour, and 50g melted afterwards for the drizzle, where the flavour stays pure.

Melting white chocolate needs the same gentleness. It seizes and turns grainy if it gets too hot or meets a splash of water. Melt it in a heatproof bowl over barely simmering water, off the boil, and stir it as little as you can get away with; or use a microwave in very short bursts, stirring between each. You are working with only 50g, so it melts fast and burns faster. The table below compares the three ways white chocolate can be used in a flapjack, so you can see why the recipe splits it.

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper, a large pan for the oat mixture, and a heatproof bowl for melting the drizzle chocolate. Buy the pistachios shelled: shelling 75g of pistachios yourself is slow, fiddly work, and the shelled weight is what the recipe means. Lightly salted shelled pistachios are genuinely fine here — the salt is part of the point — but avoid heavily salted or flavoured ones, which overwhelm the chocolate.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven, line the tin and chop the pistachios. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides. Chop 75g of shelled pistachios roughly — you want pieces, not dust — and set a teaspoonful aside if you want some for the top.
  2. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado in a large pan over a low heat. Stir until everything has melted into one smooth liquid. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil — boiled syrup sets like toffee and makes hard bars.
  3. Add the oats, pistachios, chips and salt. Take the pan off the heat and tip in 300g porridge oats, the chopped pistachios, 50g of the white chocolate chips and a pinch of salt. Stir until every oat is coated and shiny and the nuts and chips are spread through the mixture.
  4. Press into the tin. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working well into the corners. Firm pressing is what holds the bars together once cut.
  5. Bake for 30–35 minutes. The top should be golden brown with the edges a shade darker. Do not be alarmed if any white chocolate chips showing at the surface have coloured more than the oats — that is the scorching this recipe expects, and it is why only half the chocolate went in. The centre should look set but feel slightly soft when pressed; it firms up as it cools.
  6. Cool completely in the tin. Leave the slab in the tin until it is cold. The drizzle goes onto a cold bake: white chocolate drizzled over a warm slab melts into it and disappears instead of sitting on top.
  7. Melt the remaining chocolate and drizzle. Melt the remaining 50g of white chocolate in a heatproof bowl over a pan of barely simmering water, keeping the bowl clear of the water and the heat low — white chocolate turns grainy if it gets too hot. Drizzle it over the cold slab from a spoon in rough lines, scatter over the reserved pistachios if you kept any, and leave it to set at room temperature, about half an hour.
  8. Lift out and cut into 12. Use the paper overhang to lift the set slab onto a board and cut with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down. The drizzle is thin, so it slices without the cracking a thick white chocolate layer would give you; the notes on our chocolate flapjack recipe page cover thicker toppings if you ever want one.

Buying pistachios: shelled, lightly salted and properly green

Pistachios are the expensive ingredient in this recipe, so it is worth buying them with your eyes open. The colour is the first clue to quality: the kernels should be clearly green, ideally with purple-brown skins, not beige all through. Beige pistachios are older or lower-grade, and their flavour is flatter — in a recipe where the nut has a real job to do against white chocolate, that shows. Shelled nuts in a sealed bag keep their flavour far better than nuts from an open scoop bin, which stale quickly.

The salt question comes up every time this recipe is made, so to be plain about it: lightly salted pistachios are not a compromise here, they are arguably the better choice. The salt on the nuts sharpens the contrast with the white chocolate, and the pinch of salt in the ingredients list stays, because it seasons the oat crumb itself. What to avoid is the flavoured end of the shelf — chilli, lime or smoked pistachios will fight the chocolate rather than balance it. And if pistachios are simply too dear that week, the recipe works with chopped almonds and the same split of white chocolate; it is a different bar, milder and less striking, but a good one.

Storing pistachio and white chocolate flapjacks: about 5 days in an airtight tin

Keep the cut bars in an airtight tin at room temperature, with baking paper between the layers so the drizzled tops do not mark each other. They keep about 5 days. Keep the tin somewhere cool and out of sunlight: white chocolate shows heat damage sooner than dark, softening and blooming pale in a warm kitchen, and the green of the pistachios fades in bright light. The bars freeze well for up to 3 months, wrapped individually and sealed; defrost in the wrapping at room temperature. The general rules are in our guide to how to store flapjacks and how long they last.
