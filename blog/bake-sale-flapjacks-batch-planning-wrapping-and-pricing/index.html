Bake Sale Flapjacks: Batch Planning, Wrapping and Pricing

By Michael

Flapjacks are the bake sale's best friend: cheap to make in quantity, quick to bake, sturdy on the stall, and still good on day three if the stall runs over a weekend. What decides whether a bake sale batch is a pleasure or a panic is not the baking — it is the planning. How many bars, in how many batches, wrapped how, priced at what, and labelled with what: this page works through each one.

The short answer: Work backwards from how many bars you want to sell — one batch of our classic recipe makes 12 bars, so four batches make 48 — and bake them one or two days ahead, since flapjacks keep about five days in an airtight tin. Wrap each bar so it can be picked up and paid for without bare hands on the food, and label every batch with its ingredients and allergens. Price from your costs using the worked examples below, then round to a price that makes giving change easy.

Start From the Stall: How Many Bars Do You Actually Need?

The commonest bake sale mistake is baking by feel. Too few bars and your corner of the stall is bare by eleven; too many and you are carrying trays home. Start instead from the event. A school fete stall with several bakers contributing might need 24 to 48 flapjacks from you; a stall that is mostly yours needs more; a charity coffee morning where slices of cake dominate might need only a couple of dozen, positioned as the take-home option.

Ask the organiser two questions: roughly how many people are expected, and what else is being sold. Flapjacks sell steadily rather than spectacularly — they are the thing people buy for the bag, for the children, for later. If big cakes and cream things dominate the stall, flapjacks are the durable second purchase, and a moderate number, well wrapped, does best. If the stall is mostly biscuits and traybakes, flapjacks are a main event and you should plan generously. Leftovers are not a disaster with this bake, as the last section explains, so when in doubt, round up one batch rather than down.

Batch Planning: Tins, Oven Loads and Time

Our classic flapjack recipe — 175g butter, 175g golden syrup, 175g light muscovado and 350g porridge oats in a 20cm tin — makes 12 bars and takes 30 to 35 minutes in the oven at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3), plus about 10 minutes of mixing and pressing per batch. Everything about a bake sale is that recipe multiplied, so plan in batches and be honest about two limits: how many tins you own, and how many fit in your oven at once. If you are baking in serious quantity, our post on flapjack tin sizes explains how the mixture scales to bigger trays, which cuts the number of oven loads sharply.

Two ways to make that table kinder. First, two tins in the oven at once roughly halves the clock time for an even number of batches, provided the tins fit side by side with air around them; swap their positions halfway for an even bake. Second, work in an assembly line: while batch one bakes, weigh out batch two; while batch two bakes, press batch three into the freed tin as soon as batch one is turned out. The oven, not you, should be the bottleneck. And schedule the baking one or two days before the sale, not the night before — the bars need to be completely cold before wrapping anyway, and a day in hand absorbs every delay.

Buy ingredients from that table, not from memory: eight batches need 2.8kg of oats and 1.4kg each of butter, syrup and sugar, and there is no sadder bake sale sight than a baker at midnight, one batch short, with no oats in the house.

What a Batch Costs: A Worked Example, Clearly Labelled

Prices for ingredients vary by shop and by month, so this site does not quote market prices as fact. What follows is a worked example with round example figures, to show the method: put your own receipt figures into the same sums. Worked example: say the ingredients for one batch of 12 classic flapjacks come to about £3.60 on your receipt — roughly £1.40 of butter, 60p of golden syrup, 50p of sugar and £1.10 of oats, as example figures. Divide by the bars: £3.60 across 12 bars is 30p a bar in ingredients. Add wrapping — say another 5p a bar for a cellophane bag or a square of foil, again as an example figure — and each wrapped bar stands you about 35p before your time, which for a bake sale is usually donated.

That cost figure does two jobs. It sets the floor — any price below 35p a bar loses money on ingredients even with your time free — and it lets you answer the organiser's real question, which is how much the stall can raise. Worked example, continued: if a batch of 12 costs about £3.60 in ingredients and you charge £1.50 a bar, the batch takes £18.00; if you have baked six batches (72 bars), the stall takes £108 if every bar sells, against about £21.60 of ingredient costs and roughly £3.60 of wrapping by the same example figures. Charity stalls usually price for goodwill and sell-through rather than maximum return, which is why the next section lands where it does.

Pricing: Fair, Round, and Easy to Pay

Three things make a price work on a stall. It should cover costs comfortably, using your own figures in the worked example above. It should feel fair for a homemade bar — buyers at a school or village stall are neighbours, and a price that raises eyebrows raises fewer pounds. And it should be a price that makes paying painless: £1, £1.50 or £2 a bar, "three for £4" style offers, and nothing ending in awkward pence that empties the change float by ten o'clock.

Size is your other pricing lever, and flapjacks give it freely. The same batch cut into 12 generous bars at one price, or into 16 smaller squares at a lower one, serves two crowds: the person who wants a proper bar and the person — often a child with a pound coin — who wants something of their own. Clearly label which is which, keep the two piles apart, and do not let the small squares be mistaken for the big bars at the big price. A smaller "taster" size also helps a first stall, where you would rather sell out and take notes than carry stock home.

One more worked example, on targets. Worked example: if the stall hopes to raise £100 from your flapjacks and you price at £1.50 a bar, you need to sell about 67 bars — so plan six batches (72 bars), not five, and expect a few bars of slack for breakages and the stall-keeper's own purchase. If that baking load looks too big, the honest levers are a higher price, a second baker, or a lower target — not thinner bars sold under the same description.

Wrapping for the Stall: Clean, Sealed and See-Through

Stall wrapping has a job beyond freshness: it tells a stranger that the food has been handled carefully. Wrap each bar individually, in a small cellophane bag, a square of foil, or a parchment wrap, so that buyers can pick a bar up, carry it home in a bag, and eat it later without it ever being touched by a queue of hands. Bars laid out naked on a plate sell to the first few customers and then sit there looking handled; wrapped bars still look new at three in the afternoon.

Wrap the day before the sale, once the bars are completely cold, and stack the wrapped bars in their transport tin in the order you will lay them out. Clear wrapping outsells opaque at a stall, because people buy flapjacks with their eyes first — the golden top is the advertisement. Foil is cheaper and tougher; use it for the children's pocket-money pile if costs matter, and clear bags for the main display. Keep a few unwrapped bars on a board at the front as the display sample if your organiser is happy with that, replaced as they dry, but sell from the wrapped stock behind them.

Labels: Ingredients and Allergens Are Not Optional Extras

Every batch on a public stall should carry a label with three things: what the item is, its full ingredient list, and its allergens made obvious. This matters more than the price ticket. A buyer with an allergy cannot tell from looking whether a bar contains nuts, and "flapjack" on its own does not answer them — the classic recipe contains milk, in the butter, and oats, which carry a gluten warning on most packets because of how they are milled and packed. Variations change the list: a seeded bar adds sesame for many recipes; a peanut butter bar adds peanuts; a chocolate bar may add soya. The label has to describe the bar in front of it, not flapjacks in general.

Write the labels when you wrap, batch by batch, and keep flavours physically separate on the stall so a label never drifts to the wrong pile. A simple card in front of each tray, repeated on a sticker on each wrap if you have them, covers it: "Classic flapjacks — ingredients: porridge oats, butter (milk), golden syrup, light muscovado sugar, salt." If anything in your kitchen might have cross-contact with nuts — a shared spoon, a tin used for a nutty batch — the honest course is to say so on the card, and to keep nut-containing bakes on a different tray entirely. Organisers increasingly expect this of home bakers, buyers rely on it, and it costs you ten minutes with a pen.

On the Day, and What to Do With Leftovers

Arrive with more than the bars: a float of small change and notes, a clear price card for each tray, tongs or a clean cloth for handling any unwrapped display bars, and a short list of your ingredients in case the label cards blow away — fete marquees are windy places. Lay the trays out with the label cards at the front edge, keep one pile of each type as the full, untouched display and sell from a second pile if the stall is busy, and keep the sun off chocolate-topped bars for the reasons our lunchbox and picnic guide covers.

Leftovers, if there are any, are where flapjacks beat every other bake on the stall. Properly wrapped bars keep about five days in an airtight tin at room temperature — our guide to how to store flapjacks has the details — so unsold bars come home, feed the household all week, or go into the freezer, where they keep for up to three months. Nothing is wasted, no money is lost beyond ingredients, and the stall's report can honestly separate "what sold" from "what was thrown away", which for flapjacks is usually none. Plan the batches, wrap and label them properly, price them from your own receipt — and the flapjack corner of the stall will look after itself.

One or two days ahead is the sweet spot. Baked bars keep about five days in an airtight tin at room temperature, so a Thursday bake is in perfect condition for a Saturday stall, and completely cold in good time for wrapping. Baking earlier in the week works too, but wrap them once cold and keep the tin shut until the day.

If you want a worked target, divide your fundraising goal by your price to get the number of bars you must sell, and plan batches from there.
