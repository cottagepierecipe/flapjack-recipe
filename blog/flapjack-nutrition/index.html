Flapjack Nutrition: The Classic Bar's Figures and How to Read Them

By Michael

Flapjack nutrition figures online swing wildly — one site calls a bar 200 kcal, another 450, and both may be describing honest flapjacks of different sizes. This post pins down the numbers for the flapjack this site actually makes. Every figure here comes from our own verified calculations for our own tested recipes: how they were worked out is explained below, along with the traffic-light ratings those figures produce, why per-bar and per-100g numbers tell different stories, and where the other nine tested recipes on the site land, from 211 to 349 kcal a bar.

The short answer: Our classic flapjack, cut into 12, works out at 320 kcal per bar of about 73g — 439 kcal per 100g — with 14g of fat, 8.0g of saturates, 43g of carbohydrates of which 26g are sugars, 3.6g of protein, 2.9g of fibre and 0.3g of salt per bar. Against the UK traffic-light thresholds that is red for fat, saturates and sugars, and amber for salt. Across our ten tested recipes the bars run from 211 to 349 kcal each. All figures are calculated estimates, worked out from the ingredients as this post explains.

The classic flapjack's figures, in full

These are the figures for our classic flapjack recipe — 175g butter, 175g golden syrup, 175g light muscovado sugar, 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt, baked in a 20cm tin and cut into 12 bars of about 73g each. They were calculated from the ingredients using published per-100g values, then checked and verified before publication, and they appear in the nutrition panel on the recipe page itself. The per-100g column is worked out from the same batch figures — it is the column that lets you compare this flapjack fairly against any other food, whatever size anyone cuts it.

Read the table the way a baker would and it is simply the recipe restated: the fat is the butter, the sugars are the syrup and muscovado plus the oats' own small contribution, the fibre and most of the protein are the oats. Nothing in the panel is a surprise once you have seen the ingredient list, which is exactly how nutrition figures should behave — and it is why a flapjack is a treat. At 439 kcal per 100g it sits in the same territory as other butter-and-sugar bakes, and this site has never claimed otherwise.

How the figures are worked out — and how to check your own

The method is the one food labels start from, and it is simple enough to repeat at home. Take the exact ingredients and weights in the recipe. Multiply each one by its published energy and nutrient values per 100g — the standard reference figures used across the food industry. Add the batch up, then divide by the number of bars. On this site, the resulting panel is labelled a calculated estimate wherever it appears, and nothing is published until Michael has checked the calculation — the same deliver, verify, then publish pattern as the kitchen testing. No laboratory has measured these bars, and natural variation — a riper banana, a heavier spoon of syrup, an uneven cut — moves the real figure a little either way. Read every number as a good estimate, honestly arrived at.

To work out your own flapjack — a family recipe, or one of ours with a swap — weigh everything that goes in, look up each ingredient's per-100g values from its packet, multiply, add and divide by your number of bars. Weigh the finished slab too if you want a true per-bar weight; our classic's 73g bar is simply the batch weight divided by 12. The arithmetic takes ten minutes and turns "about 300 calories, probably" into a figure you can defend.

The traffic lights: where the classic lands

UK front-of-pack labelling colours four nutrients against fixed per-100g thresholds. Red — the high band — starts above 17.5g of fat, above 5g of saturates, above 22.5g of sugars and above 1.5g of salt per 100g. Set the classic flapjack's per-100g figures against those lines and the result is honest and unsurprising: fat at about 19g is red, saturates at about 11g are red, sugars at about 36g are red, and salt at about 0.4g sits in the amber middle band (amber runs from 0.3g to 1.5g). Three reds and an amber: the profile of a butter-and-syrup bake, and the same picture the ingredient list already gave you.

Two things are worth holding onto about those colours. First, they describe the food, not the eater: a red band on a flapjack eaten as an occasional treat is a different thing from red bands across a whole day's eating, and the thresholds were designed for comparing similar products, which is the use this post makes of them. Second, the colours are per 100g, so they cannot be argued with by cutting a smaller bar — the smaller bar changes what you ate, not what the flapjack is. Both facts argue for the same approach: know the figures, choose the portion, enjoy the bar.

The range across our ten tested recipes

The classic is not the whole story. All ten recipe pages on this site carry verified figures worked out the same way, and the spread shows what recipe design can and cannot do. The lightest bar is the healthy recipe at 211 kcal — banana and dates doing much of the sweetening — and the heaviest is the chocolate at 349 kcal, where a full chocolate topping adds itself to the base. Everything else falls between, and the pattern is instructive: the swaps that move the figure are the ones that change the fat and sugar quantities, not the flavourings.

The portion on the plate

The panel describes the flapjack — but flapjack is often served as a pudding, warm, with custard or ice cream, like the portion in the photograph: an oat-crumble serving with a scoop on top. Everything added to the plate adds its own figures, and a bar eaten as a dessert is a different total from the same bar eaten from the tin. Neither accounting is cheating; they are just different, and the honest one counts what is actually in front of you. It is also why a per-bar figure should always be read alongside the question of how the bar is being served — the tin figure and the pudding figure are both real.

Per bar versus per 100g: two numbers, two jobs

Per 100g is the comparison measure. It strips out portion size, so it tells you what the food itself is like — how energy-dense, how fatty, how sweet — and it is the basis the traffic lights are calculated on. Per bar is the eating measure: it tells you what you actually had, and it depends entirely on how the slab was cut. Our classic batch holds about 3,840 kcal in total; cut into 12, that is 320 kcal a bar, but cut into 16 the same flapjack is about 240 kcal a piece, and cut into 8 it is about 480. The recipe did not change — only the knife did. This is why the wild range of flapjack figures online is mostly a portion story, and why any figure quoted without a bar weight is only half a figure.

The practical habit is to use both numbers for what they are good at. Comparing two recipes, or a homemade bar against a bought one? Per 100g. Deciding what your afternoon slice actually cost you? Per bar, with the bar weighed or at least honestly sized. Our lower-calorie flapjacks post makes the same point from the other side: its lighter bar saves about 23% per bar but about 15% per 100g, and the post gives both, because both are true. So the whole picture, stated once without decoration: a classic flapjack is an energy-dense, high-sugar treat with useful fibre from its oats, at 320 kcal for a 73g bar. The lighter recipes bring that down by redesigning the mixture, and cutting any flapjack smaller brings the per-portion figure down further still. What no honest version of the figures will do is turn the bar into a health food.
