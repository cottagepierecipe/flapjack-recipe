How to Line a Flapjack Tin and Lift the Bake Out

By Michael

Flapjack mixture is half glue: melted butter, sugar and golden syrup that sets hard as it cools. Line the tin properly and the whole slab lifts out in one clean piece; line it badly and you will be chiselling oat concrete off the metal. Here is the sling method that works every time, the corner trick for a square tin, and what to do when the paper sticks anyway.

The short answer: Cut one sheet of baking paper long enough to cover the base of the tin and hang over two opposite sides, grease the tin lightly first so the paper stays put, and fold neat pleats at the corners. Bake, cool the slab completely in the tin, then lift it out by the overhanging ends like handles and peel the paper away.

Why lining matters more for flapjacks than for cakes: the syrup sets like glue

A sponge cake is mostly trapped air and crumb; even a badly greased tin usually lets go with a shake and a knife round the edge. A flapjack is different. As the bake cools, the syrup in the mixture turns from a liquid into a firm, sticky cement, and wherever the mixture touches bare metal, it bonds. Corners are the worst, because the syrup pools there and the oats pack tightest where you pressed them in.

Lining does three jobs at once. It stops the slab welding itself to the tin. It gives you handles, so a heavy, fragile-while-warm slab comes out supported along its whole length instead of being levered and cracked. And it protects the cut: a slab lifted onto a board can be sliced straight with a long knife, which is much harder inside the walls of a tin — ragged squares are often cut-in-the-tin squares. More on that in how to cut flapjacks without breaking them. A lined tin is also far easier to wash.

The sling method, step by step: one sheet, two handles, no wrestling

This is the method used for the classic flapjack recipe on this site, baked in a lined 20cm square tin. It takes about a minute once you have done it twice.

Step 1: measure the sheet against the tin. Cut a sheet of baking paper (the non-stick kind sold as baking parchment works the same way) as wide as the tin and long enough to cover the base, climb both ends, and leave 3–4cm hanging over each rim. For a 20cm square tin, about 20cm × 34cm is right. Those hanging ends are your sling handles, so do not trim them flush.

Step 2: grease the tin first. Smear a thin film of butter or oil over the base and sides of the empty tin. This is not to stop the flapjack sticking — the paper does that — it is to glue the paper down so it does not spring up or slide about while you press in a heavy, sticky mixture.

Step 3: press the paper in. Lay the sheet across the tin and push it down into the base, running a finger along each inside edge so the paper takes the tin's shape. The middle fits neatly; at the ends, the paper has to go somewhere — which is where the corner folds come in.

Step 4: fold a pleat at each corner. Fold the excess paper at each corner into a flat triangular pleat against the side of the tin, the way you would wrap the end of a parcel. Held by the grease, the pleats stay put and leave the inside smooth. Scrunched corners trap mixture and tear the slab, so flat folds are worth the ten seconds they take.

Step 5: add a second strip if the bake is heavy. One sling lifts a standard batch easily. For a double batch, or a fruit flapjack loaded with extra weight, lay a second narrower strip across the tin the other way, so you have handles on all four sides and the slab is fully supported.

Step 6: fill, level and bake as the recipe says. Spoon the mixture in, level it, and press it down firmly with the back of a spoon, working into the corners. The grease underneath stops the paper travelling as you press.

Corners on a square tin: snip or fold, but never scrunch

Square tins are the standard for flapjacks — the classic recipe uses a 20cm square — and corners are where most lining goes wrong. There are two tidy ways to deal with them. The pleat fold above keeps the sheet in one piece. The alternative is to snip: lay the paper over the tin, mark where the corners fall, and cut a short slit from each corner of the sheet towards the middle, stopping at the corner of the base. The flaps overlap neatly inside the tin with no bunching.

What does not work is pushing a big sheet in and letting it crumple into the corners. The mixture flows into the folds, and the slab comes out with ridges baked into its corners and chunks missing where you pulled the paper free. A round tin is more forgiving, because crumpled paper can ease around a curve — as in the photo below, of a round pan lined with crumpled baking paper. For a square flapjack tin, fold or snip instead.

Whichever method you choose, check the paper sits flat against the base before the mixture goes in — any ridge in the paper becomes a ridge in the bottom of the slab.

A flat sheet laid over a tray, like the one in the next photograph, is a tray liner rather than a tin lining: fine for biscuits, where nothing needs lifting out of a deep shape. Flapjacks bake in a tin with sides, so the paper has to climb the walls and come back over the rim.

Grease alone is not enough for syrup bakes: what the release-paste photo shows

You will see cake-release pastes — flour, oil and shortening whisked together, like the coating in the photograph below — recommended as a complete alternative to paper. For a plain sponge they work well. For flapjacks they are a gamble: hot syrup is runny and seeks out every pinhole and thin patch in the coating, and where it finds bare metal it sets hard — the corner it has hold of usually stays in the tin.

If you like using a release paste, treat it as the glue layer, not the barrier: coat the tin, then lay the paper sling over it. The paste holds the paper flat, and the paper does the releasing. A loose-bottomed tin is the other halfway house, but syrup can seep into the join and glue that shut too, and it still leaves you nothing to lift with. Paper remains the reliable answer.

The lift out: cool first, lift level, peel last

How you remove the bake matters as much as how you lined it. Flapjacks are at their most fragile straight from the oven: the syrup is still liquid and a warm slab flexes and cracks if lifted too soon. It looks solid. It is not, yet.

Leave the tin on a wire rack until the slab is completely cool — at least the cooling time in the method, and longer does no harm. As it cools the syrup firms up, and the bake turns into one rigid piece that can take its own weight.

Then run a palette knife down the two unlined sides to free any spots where syrup crept over the paper's edge. Grip the two overhanging ends, one in each hand, and lift straight up in one smooth movement, keeping the slab level. Set it down, paper and all, on a chopping board — and if the slab is a heavy one, slide a flat baking tray under the middle as it clears the tin.

Only then peel the paper away, pulling it back flat against the slab rather than up at an angle, so it does not take crumbs with it. Stuck paper and torn corners: rescuing a bake that will not let go

If the slab refuses to lift, the syrup has crept somewhere the paper did not reach — over the top edge of the sling, or through a tear. Do not force it; a forced lift tears the slab, not the bond. Warm the base of the tin briefly instead — sit it in a bowl with a centimetre of hot water for a minute — just enough to soften the syrup at the join, then lift straight away.

If the paper itself has stuck to the bottom of the slab, the usual cause is greaseproof paper rather than non-stick baking paper — near-identical in the drawer, very different with syrup. For stubborn patches, dampen the paper with a wet cloth, leave it a minute, and peel again; it softens and releases rather than tearing the base. If the packet does not say non-stick or baking parchment, assume it is greaseproof.

And if the overhang turns out too short to grip, invert the tin onto a board, tap the base, and lift the tin away; the slab lands upside down and flips back with a second board. Not elegant, but the bake survives — and next time the sheet gets measured against the tin first. Choosing the right tin helps as well — see what size tin you need for flapjacks if yours is an odd shape.
