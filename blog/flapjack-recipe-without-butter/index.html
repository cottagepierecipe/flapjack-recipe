Flapjack Recipe Without Butter

By Michael

No butter in the house, a dairy-free eater to feed, or a jar of coconut oil to use up? A flapjack does not actually need butter — it needs fat, and coconut oil does the job very well. This version keeps everything else from our tested classic — the golden syrup, the muscovado, the oats — and changes only the fat in the pan.

The short answer: Replace the 175g butter in the classic recipe with 150g coconut oil and change nothing else. Coconut oil is pure fat, while butter is only about 82 per cent fat, so you need a little less of it. The bars set firm when they are cold, soften in a warm kitchen, and bake in the same 30 to 35 minutes (estimate).

Not yet kitchen-tested: this recipe adapts our tested classic flapjack and has not been cooked by us yet. Michael tests every new recipe himself, and this page will be updated once he has cooked it. Times marked (estimate) are estimates.

Coconut oil instead of butter: the one swap that matters

Butter does three jobs in a flapjack. It coats every oat so the syrup can stick, it carries the sweetness evenly through the mixture, and it sets again as the bars cool, giving them their firm, clean-cut bite. Any replacement has to do all three jobs, and coconut oil is the closest match in the cupboard — it is solid when cold, liquid when warm, and mild enough not to take over.

The one thing you cannot do is swap it weight for weight. Butter is about 82 per cent fat; the rest is water and a little milk solid. That means 175g of butter actually gives you roughly 145g of fat. Coconut oil is 100 per cent fat, so the full 175g would tip the bars towards greasy. Dropping to 150g lands you almost exactly on the same amount of fat the classic recipe was built around — which is why this recipe works with no other changes at all.

Flavour is the only other choice to make. Refined coconut oil is neutral and lets the syrup and muscovado do the talking, just as butter does. Unrefined (virgin) coconut oil adds a gentle coconut note of its own — pleasant here, and if you want to lean into it properly, our coconut flapjack recipe builds the whole bar around the flavour. Either type works in this recipe at the same 150g.

One practical difference: coconut oil melts at a lower temperature than butter, around 24°C rather than the mid-thirties. In the pan that means it liquefies almost as soon as it warms, so there is no need for anything above a low heat. In the finished bars it means the set is temperature-sensitive in a way butter bars are not — more on that below, because it decides where you store them and when you cut them.

Ingredients

Everything except the fat is the tested classic flapjack exactly as written — same tin, same oven, same twelve bars. If you have already made the classic, you already know this recipe; you are only changing what melts first.

IngredientAmountNotes
Coconut oil150gRefined for a neutral taste; unrefined adds a gentle coconut flavour
Golden syrup175gThe glue that holds the oats together — weigh it, do not guess
Light muscovado sugar175gFor the deep caramel flavour; light brown soft sugar is the closest swap
Porridge oats350gStandard rolled porridge oats, the same as the classic
Fine sea saltA pinchBalances the sweetness and lifts the flavour

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a little overhang on two sides so the slab lifts out cleanly later.
  2. Melt the coconut oil gently. Put the 150g coconut oil in a large saucepan over the lowest heat. It melts fast — much faster than butter — so stay with it and take the pan off the heat as soon as it is liquid. It should never get hot enough to shimmer or smoke.
  3. Add the syrup and sugar. Stir in the 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado. Keep the heat low and stir until the sugar has dissolved into a smooth, glossy liquid. Do not let it boil: boiled syrup sets like toffee and turns the finished bars hard.
  4. Take the pan off the heat. Once everything is liquid and combined, the cooking part of the fat-and-syrup stage is done. From here you are just mixing.
  5. Stir in the oats and salt. Tip in the 350g porridge oats and the pinch of salt. Stir until every oat is coated and shiny and there are no dry patches hiding at the bottom of the pan — dry oats are the main cause of crumbly bars.
  6. Press into the tin. Scrape the mixture into the lined tin and press it down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, right into the corners. A firm press now is what gives you bars that hold together later.
  7. Bake for 30 to 35 minutes (estimate). Bake until the edges are golden and the centre looks set but still slightly soft — it firms up as it cools. If the middle looks wet and glossy, give it another couple of minutes; if the edges are darkening fast, it is done.
  8. Mark the cutting lines while warm. About five minutes out of the oven, while the top is still soft enough not to crack, score twelve bars with a table knife. Do not cut through yet.
  9. Cool completely in the tin. This matters more here than with butter. Coconut oil only sets as it cools, so warm bars will crumble if you rush them. Leave the slab until it is completely cold and feels firm.
  10. Lift out and cut into 12. Lift the slab out on the paper and cut along your scored lines with a sharp knife. If your kitchen is warm and the bars feel soft, half an hour in the fridge (estimate) firms them up first.

Butter substitutes for flapjacks compared

Coconut oil is the main route here, but it is not the only fat that works. The two other honest options behave quite differently, and one popular idea — simply missing the fat out — does not work at all.

SubstituteAmountHow the bars turn out
Coconut oil150gThe main recipe above. Firm, clean-cutting bars when cool; softens in a warm kitchen
Dairy-free baking block175gThe closest set to butter — it is made to behave like butter, so use the full weight
Neutral vegetable oil (such as sunflower)140mlThe softest set. The oil stays liquid at room temperature, so the bars stay tender and can feel greasy; cut them very cold

Use a block if you choose the dairy-free baking block, not a soft tub spread. Tub spreads contain much more water, which steams off in the oven and leaves the mixture short of fat — the bars come out softer and less bound. Baking blocks are formulated to melt and set like butter, which is exactly what a flapjack needs.

Why leaving the fat out altogether fails: without fat there is nothing to coat the oats, so the syrup soaks into a few patches and leaves the rest dry. The mixture will not press into a slab that holds, and in the oven the exposed oats scorch while the sugary patches overcook. What comes out is not a lighter flapjack — it is sweet, crumbly baked oats that fall apart in the tin. If you want a lighter bar, the honest route is a smaller portion of a proper one, not a fat-free mixture.

The set: firm when cool, softer in a warm kitchen

The one real difference you will notice with coconut oil bars is how much the weather matters. Because coconut oil melts at around 24°C, the bars are firm and slice cleanly in a cool kitchen, then soften noticeably on a hot afternoon or in a room with the heating on. Butter bars change much less, because butter holds its set to a higher temperature.

None of this is a problem once you know it. Cut the bars when they are properly cold, store them somewhere cool rather than next to the oven or on a sunny worktop, and if a warm day makes them feel bendy, twenty minutes in the fridge puts the set back. If your first slice crumbles, the bars were almost certainly still warm in the middle — give the slab more time, not a shorter bake next time.

This page only swaps the butter — for fully vegan baking

This recipe changes one ingredient and keeps the rest of the classic intact, which makes it the right page if butter itself is the problem — an empty butter dish, or someone who cannot eat dairy. Fully vegan baking asks a few more questions than the fat alone, and those are answered properly on our vegan flapjack recipe page, which is built for the job from the start rather than adapted into it.

Storing flapjacks without butter

Keep the cut bars in an airtight tin in a cool cupboard and they should keep well for about 5 days (estimate), just as the classic bars do. Cool matters more than it does for butter bars: a tin stored in a warm kitchen will give you soft bars by day two, though the flavour is unaffected and a short spell in the fridge firms them up again. There is no need to refrigerate them as a rule — a cool cupboard is enough in most homes, most of the year.
