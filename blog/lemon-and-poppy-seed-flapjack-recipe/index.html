Lemon and Poppy Seed Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

A lemon and poppy seed flapjack is the classic oat bar flavoured the way a lemon drizzle cake is: the finely grated zest of two lemons and two tablespoons of poppy seeds stirred through the mixture, then a sharp lemon drizzle spooned over the warm bake and left to set. The zest scents the crumb, the seeds speckle it, and the drizzle does the sharp work on top. Three small additions, and the bar tastes like a different recipe.

The short answer: Melt 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado sugar in a pan, stir in 350g porridge oats, a pinch of salt, the finely grated zest of 2 lemons and 2 tablespoons of poppy seeds, press into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes. Mix 75g icing sugar with the juice of 1 lemon, spoon it over the warm bake, leave it to set, and cut into 12 bars.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Lemon and poppy seed: the drizzle cake, rebuilt as a flapjack

Lemon and poppy seed is one of British baking's settled pairings — it fills loaf cakes, muffins and the cake in the photograph above — and it works for a plain reason. Lemon on its own in a bake can taste thin and sharp; the poppy seeds give it body, a faint nuttiness and something to look at. Here the pairing is applied to our classic flapjack recipe in its full, unadjusted form: 175g each of butter, golden syrup and light muscovado to the full 350g of porridge oats. The oats stay at the full measure because zest and seeds add flavour without adding bulk — there is nothing to make room for.

This is the second lemon flapjack on the site, and it is worth saying plainly how it differs from the first. Our lemon flapjack recipe puts lemon through the mixture and stops there — a lemon bar, evenly flavoured, with no topping. This one adds the poppy seeds and finishes the bake with a set lemon drizzle, so the sharpness sits on top as well as through the crumb, the way it does on a drizzle cake. If you want the plainer bar, make that one. If you want the cake-shop version, make this one. The comparison table further down sets the two side by side.

Zest in the mixture, juice in the drizzle — never the other way round

The one rule that makes this recipe work is the division of the lemon. The flavour of a lemon lives in the oils in its skin. The juice is mostly water and acid — sharp, but thin-tasting once it has been baked. So the zest goes into the mixture, where its oils perfume the whole crumb and survive the oven, and the juice goes into the drizzle, which is never cooked at all and keeps its raw sharpness. Swap them round and both halves fail: juice in the mixture wets it and loosens the set for very little flavour, and zest in an icing is gritty and bitter-flecked.

Zest the lemons properly. Use a fine grater, take only the yellow skin, and stop on each patch as soon as white shows through — the white pith is bitter, and in a lemon recipe there is nowhere for bitterness to hide. Unwaxed lemons are the better buy when you can get them, since the zest is going into the food; if yours are waxed, give them a good scrub under hot water first. Zest them before you start melting anything, while your hands are dry, and stir the zest through the oats thoroughly: it clumps, and a clump means one sharp bar and eleven plain ones.

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides, a large pan, a fine grater for the zest, and a small bowl for the drizzle. Line the tin carefully — our guide to how to line a flapjack tin shows the paper arrangement that lifts a drizzled slab out without cracking the top.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven, line the tin and zest the lemons. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides. Finely grate the zest of 2 lemons, taking only the yellow skin and none of the white pith, and set it aside.
  2. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado in a large pan over a low heat. Stir until everything has melted into one smooth liquid. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil — boiled syrup sets like toffee and makes hard bars.
  3. Add the oats, salt, zest and poppy seeds. Take the pan off the heat and tip in 350g porridge oats, a pinch of salt, all the lemon zest and 2 tablespoons of poppy seeds. Stir until every oat is coated and the zest and seeds are spread through the mixture rather than sitting in clumps.
  4. Press into the tin. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working well into the corners. Firm pressing is what holds the bars together once cut.
  5. Bake for 30–35 minutes. The top should be golden with the edges a shade darker, and the centre should look set but still feel slightly soft when pressed — it firms up as it cools.
  6. Make the drizzle. While the bake is in the oven, sift 75g icing sugar into a small bowl and stir in the juice of 1 lemon, a little at a time, until you have a thick, pourable icing — it should fall off the spoon in a slow ribbon, not run like water. You may not need every drop of the juice; stop when the consistency is right.
  7. Drizzle over the warm bake. As soon as the tin comes out of the oven, spoon the drizzle over the hot surface, letting it run into the texture of the oats. Warm — not left to go cold first — is the moment: the heat helps the icing spread and then set into the surface.
  8. Leave to cool and set, then cut into 12. Leave the slab in the tin until it is completely cold and the drizzle has set firm and opaque — about an hour. Lift it out by the paper overhang and cut into 12 bars with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down so the set drizzle is sliced rather than dragged.

Poppy seeds: speckle and a faint nuttiness, not crunch

Two tablespoons of poppy seeds looks like a lot in the bowl and is exactly right in the bar. Be clear about what they do, though, because people expect the wrong thing from them. Poppy seeds do not crunch — they are too small, and once they have sat in a warm, buttery mixture and baked for half an hour they are softer still. What they give is a faint, pleasant nuttiness in the background and the dark speckle through the pale crumb that makes a lemon bake look like a lemon-and-poppy-seed bake. The flavour leads; the seeds support. If you leave them out, you have a good lemon flapjack with a drizzle — which is a fine thing, but it is not this recipe.

The seeds need no preparation at all. They do not need soaking, toasting or grinding — they go into the pan dry, with the oats, and the warmth of the mixture does the rest. Buy them from the baking aisle rather than the spice rack if you can; they are the same seeds, but baking-aisle packets tend to be fresher, and poppy seeds go stale and taste dusty if they have sat in a cupboard for years. If yours smell of nothing and taste of nothing on the tip of your tongue, they will do the same in the bar — replace them.

The drizzle: thick enough to sit, thin enough to run

The drizzle is the same icing that finishes a lemon drizzle cake, and it obeys the same rules. Consistency is everything. Too thin and it vanishes into the oats, leaving a sticky sheen and no flavour. Too thick and it sits in white lumps that never spread. The test is the ribbon: lift the spoon and the icing should fall back into the bowl in a slow, thick ribbon that holds its shape for a second before it levels. Add the juice a spoonful at a time and stop the moment you get there — lemons vary in size and juiciness, which is why the recipe gives a juice quantity as a guide rather than a promise.

Storing lemon and poppy seed flapjacks: about 5 days in an airtight tin

Wait until the drizzle has set completely before the bars go anywhere near a tin — warm or unset icing will stick to everything it touches. Once set, keep the bars in an airtight tin at room temperature with baking paper between the layers, and they keep about 5 days. The drizzle stays crisp for the first two or three days and softens slightly after that, though the flavour holds. The bars freeze well for up to 3 months, wrapped individually and sealed; be aware the drizzle comes back a little duller after freezing, so if the bars are for guests, freeze them before the drizzle goes on and ice them once defrosted. The general rules are in our guide to how to store flapjacks and how long they last.
