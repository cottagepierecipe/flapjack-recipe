Chocolate Peanut Butter Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

These flapjacks melt smooth peanut butter into the base itself, so the peanut flavour runs through every bite rather than sitting in a layer, and finish with a swirled topping of dark chocolate and more peanut butter. One thing to say plainly at the start: these bars contain peanuts, in the base and in the topping. They are not suitable for anyone with a peanut allergy, and they should never go into a lunchbox or a bake sale tin unlabelled.

The short answer: Melt 150g butter with 150g smooth peanut butter, 150g light muscovado sugar and 3 tablespoons of golden syrup, stir in 325g porridge oats and a pinch of salt, press into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 25–30 minutes. Cool, then melt 150g dark chocolate with 2 tablespoons of peanut butter, spread and swirl it over the top, leave to set, and cut into 12 bars.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Chocolate peanut butter flapjacks: peanut in the base, chocolate on top

Most chocolate-and-peanut-butter bakes put the peanut in a filling or a frosting and keep the base plain. A flapjack invites the opposite arrangement, because the base is made in a pan: peanut butter melts straight into the butter, syrup and sugar, coats the oats with everything else, and bakes into a bar that tastes of peanut all the way through. It also works for its living. Peanut butter brings fat, which keeps the bars tender, and a savoury depth that plain butter lacks, so the finished bar eats less sweet than the ingredients list suggests — which is just as well, because there is chocolate still to come.

The base adapts our classic flapjack recipe: the butter comes down to 150g because the peanut butter brings its own fat, the syrup becomes 3 tablespoons working alongside the peanut butter as a binder, and the oats come down to 325g to keep the mixture pressable rather than stiff. The topping follows the topping route on our chocolate flapjack recipe page — dark chocolate melted and spread — with peanut butter stirred into the chocolate so the swirl is flavour as well as pattern. If peanuts are a problem in your house, stop here and make our nut-free flapjack recipe instead; this page is peanuts from top to bottom.

Smooth peanut butter binds best — and stir the jar first

The recipe uses smooth peanut butter, and the reason is mechanical as much as anything: smooth melts evenly into the hot butter and syrup, coats every oat, and gives a bar with a consistent set. Crunchy peanut butter works in the same quantity and plenty of people prefer it — the bars come out nubbly, with pockets of peanut pieces — but the pieces are unevenly spread by nature, and the occasional bar is more peanut than oat. Either way, stir the jar thoroughly before you weigh anything out. Peanut butter separates in the jar, oil rising to the top, and a spoon taken from an unstirred jar can be mostly oil or mostly solids depending on where it lands. Both will throw the recipe off: too much oil makes greasy bars, too little makes dry ones.

Sweetened and unsweetened peanut butters both work. The sweetened kind brings a little extra sugar and salt; the unsweetened kind — often just peanuts, sometimes peanuts and salt — gives a more grown-up bar and is the better match for the dark chocolate on top. Check the salt either way. Peanut butters vary a lot in saltiness, and the pinch of salt in the ingredients assumes an average jar; if yours is a very salty one, leave the extra pinch out. Taste is the guide: the melted mixture, before the oats go in, should taste like the bar you want to eat, only warmer.

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper and a large pan. Allergen note: this recipe contains peanuts in both the base and the topping. Keep that in mind for everything that follows — who you serve them to, where you store them, and how you label the tin.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides for lifting the finished slab out later.
  2. Stir the peanut butter jar, then melt everything. Stir the peanut butter thoroughly in its jar so the oil is mixed back in. Put 150g butter, 150g of the stirred peanut butter, 150g light muscovado and 3 tablespoons of golden syrup in a large pan over a low heat. Stir until it all melts into one smooth, glossy liquid. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil — boiled syrup sets like toffee and makes hard bars.
  3. Add the oats and salt. Take the pan off the heat and tip in 325g porridge oats and a pinch of salt, unless your peanut butter is very salty. Stir until every oat is coated and shiny. The mixture will look a little softer and glossier than the classic — that is the peanut butter's oil, and it firms up as it cools.
  4. Press into the tin. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working well into the corners. Firm pressing is what holds the bars together once cut.
  5. Bake for 25–30 minutes. The top should be golden brown with the edges a shade darker, and the centre should look set but still feel slightly soft when pressed — it firms up as it cools. Leave the slab to cool in the tin for at least 20 minutes before the topping goes on; cool to room temperature is fine too.
  6. Melt the chocolate with the peanut butter. Break 150g dark chocolate into a heatproof bowl set over a pan of barely simmering water. When it is nearly melted, add 2 tablespoons of smooth peanut butter and stir until the two are one smooth, pourable mixture.
  7. Spread and swirl. Pour the chocolate mixture over the cooled base, still in its tin, and spread it to the corners. While it is still wet, drag the tip of a knife or a skewer through it in a few loose S-shapes to pull the paler peanut swirls through the dark chocolate. Two or three passes is a swirl; ten passes is a uniform mid-brown.
  8. Leave it to set. Leave the tin at room temperature until the topping is firm and matte rather than glossy — about an hour. If you want extra crunch, scatter a handful of chopped roasted peanuts over the topping while it is still wet, before the swirling step.
  9. Lift out and cut into 12. Use the paper overhang to lift the cold slab onto a board. Cut with a large sharp knife at room temperature, pressing straight down, and wipe the blade between cuts so the swirl stays clean.

Chocolate peanut butter problems: greasy bars, lost swirls, bland middles

The failures here are mostly about oil and salt — the two things peanut butter brings that a plain flapjack does not have to think about. The table below covers the usual ones. The summary is short: stir the jar, mind the salt, and swirl once, briefly, while the topping is wet.

The swirled top: pattern that is also flavour

The swirl on these bars is not decoration for its own sake. Because the peanut butter is stirred into the chocolate before spreading, the paler trails the knife pulls through are genuinely more peanut-flavoured than the dark parts — every bite is a slightly different ratio of the two. That only works if the mixture goes on evenly and is swirled briefly. Spread first, with the back of a spoon, until the corners are covered; then swirl. Doing both at once leaves bare patches at the edges and a thick pool in the middle.

If you would rather skip the swirl, the topping also works plain: melt the chocolate on its own, spread it, and scatter a spoonful of chopped roasted peanuts and a pinch of flaky salt over it while it is wet. And if you want no topping at all, the base stands on its own perfectly well — it is, after all, a peanut butter flapjack — though the bars keep a day or so less well without the chocolate sealing the top. Our main chocolate flapjack recipe page covers the chips and cocoa routes if you want chocolate through the bar instead of on it.

Storing chocolate peanut butter flapjacks: about 5 days, labelled

Keep the cut bars in an airtight tin at room temperature with baking paper between the layers, and they keep about 5 days. Because these bars contain peanuts, label the tin — actually write it on a piece of tape — so nobody in the house mistakes them for a nut-free bake, and keep them away from anything being made for someone with a peanut allergy. They freeze well for up to 3 months, wrapped individually and sealed, and defrost at room temperature in their wrapping. The general rules are in our guide to how to store flapjacks and how long they last.

Over-swirling. The pattern is made by dragging a knife through the wet topping two or three times; each extra pass mixes the pale peanut trails further into the dark chocolate until the two become a single mid-brown. The flavour is identical — the swirl is only lost as a pattern. Spread the topping evenly first, then make a few loose S-shapes and stop while the contrast still looks strong; it softens slightly as it sets anyway.

Other smooth nut butters — almond, cashew, hazelnut — work in the same quantities and by the same method, though each changes the flavour and all of them are still tree-nut allergens, so the bars remain unsuitable for anyone with a nut allergy. Seed butters such as sunflower seed butter also work and are the route to a nut-free version of the idea; expect a slightly different, milder flavour and a mixture that can look a touch green-tinged after baking, which is harmless.
