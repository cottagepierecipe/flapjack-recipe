Hot Cross Bun-Spiced Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

A hot cross bun's flavour is not really about the bun — it is the mixed spice, the extra cinnamon, the raisins and sultanas, the candied peel and the orange that runs through all of it. This recipe borrows that whole combination and puts it in a flapjack: the classic oat mixture, spiced and fruited like the inside of a bun, with an optional icing cross piped on each bar once it has cooled.

The short answer: Make the classic flapjack — 175g butter, 175g golden syrup, 175g light muscovado sugar and 350g porridge oats — and stir in 2 tsp mixed spice, 1 tsp ground cinnamon, 100g raisins and sultanas, 50g mixed peel and the zest of 1 orange. Press into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes. Makes 12 bars. An icing cross piped on each cooled bar is decoration only.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

The bun's flavour, in a bar

Strip a hot cross bun down to what you actually taste and very little of it is bread. It is warm spice first — mixed spice with cinnamon pushed forward — then dried fruit, then the bitter-sweet chew of candied peel, and orange underneath everything. All of those travel well, because none of them depends on the dough. Put them into our classic flapjack recipe mixture instead and you get a bar that tastes unmistakably of the bun while staying entirely a flapjack: chewy oats, caramel from the muscovado, and the spice and fruit folded through. It is a spring bake in the same way the bun is, and like the bun it is honestly just as good in any week you fancy it. If you are interested in the fruit side of the mixture on its own, our fruit flapjack recipe is the classic base carrying dried fruit without the spice.

Two proportions matter. The spice is heavier than you might expect — 2 teaspoons of mixed spice plus a further teaspoon of cinnamon across one tray — because oats and syrup are both strong flavours that swallow seasoning, and a bun's spice sits in a bland dough that lets it through. And the fruit is kept at 150g in total, peel included: more than that and the mixture stops binding as a flapjack and starts behaving like a fruit slice that crumbles. What follows takes each element in turn.

The spices: mixed spice with the cinnamon pushed forward

Mixed spice is already a cinnamon-led blend — cinnamon, allspice and nutmeg are its usual backbone, with cloves or ginger in some makers' versions. So why the extra teaspoon of cinnamon on top? Because that is how a bun tastes. In a bun, cinnamon is not one note among equals; it is the note you name. Adding the extra teaspoon to the blend restores that balance in a mixture where the syrup's caramel would otherwise round everything into a general warmth. Stir the spices into the melted butter, syrup and sugar rather than sprinkling them over the oats — spice bloomed briefly in warm fat coats the oats evenly, and you avoid the dry, dusty pockets of spice that a late stir-in can leave. If your mixed spice has been open a long time, this is the recipe that will tell you: old spice gives heat without aroma, and there is nowhere for it to hide here.

The fruit: raisins, sultanas and a short soak

The 100g of raisins and sultanas can be all raisins, all sultanas or any mixture of the two — sultanas are a little softer and sweeter, raisins a little darker in flavour, and a bun usually carries both. What matters more than the ratio is their condition. Dried fruit that goes into a bake dry will pull moisture out of the mixture around it, and fruit sitting on the surface can catch and scorch in the oven's heat. If your raisins are hard and dry, soak them for 10 minutes in hot water, drain them well and pat them dry before they go in; plump fruit folds through the mixture evenly and stays soft in the finished bar. Fruit that is already soft and sticky needs no soak — soaking it anyway just makes it shed water into the mixture. Fold the fruit through the oats before pressing, and press a little plain mixture over any pieces sitting proud on the surface if you can; buried fruit bakes, exposed fruit toasts.

Mixed peel and orange zest

Mixed peel is chopped candied orange and lemon peel, and its bitter edge is doing quiet work in this recipe: against the syrup and muscovado it is the one ingredient that pulls the sweetness back, and a bar without it tastes noticeably flatter and sweeter. The 50g in the recipe is the amount a bun would carry, and it is enough — peel is strong, and more makes the bars taste of marmalade rather than of the bun. The zest of 1 orange, grated fresh into the mixture, backs the peel up with the fruit's own aroma; use the zest only, none of the white pith, which is bitter in the wrong way. If you only have one of the two, use the peel and add a second orange's zest, or use the zest alone and accept a gentler bar.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven, line the tin, soak the fruit if needed. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving overhang at two sides. If your raisins and sultanas are hard and dry, cover them with hot water for 10 minutes, then drain well and pat dry.
  2. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado in a large pan over a low heat and stir until melted into one smooth liquid. Never let it boil — boiled syrup sets like toffee and makes hard bars.
  3. Bloom the spices in the melt. Take the pan off the heat and stir in 2 tsp mixed spice and 1 tsp ground cinnamon until the melt is evenly spiced and smells warm.
  4. Add the oats, fruit, peel and zest. Tip in 350g porridge oats, the 100g raisins and sultanas, 50g mixed peel, the zest of 1 orange and a pinch of salt. Stir until everything is evenly distributed and every oat is coated — the fruit and peel should run through the whole mixture, not sit in one corner of the pan.
  5. Press into the tin and bake. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working into the corners and covering any fruit sitting proud on the surface. Bake for 30–35 minutes, until golden with the edges a shade darker.
  6. Cool and cut into 12. Leave the slab to cool in the tin until firm, then lift it out on its paper and cut into 12 bars with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down. Spiced flapjack cuts more cleanly once it is properly cool.
  7. Pipe the crosses, if you are using them. Stir about 75g icing sugar with a teaspoon or two of water to a thick icing, spoon it into a small piping bag or a food bag with the corner snipped, and pipe a cross on each cooled bar — one line along, one line across. Leave the icing to set for about 30 minutes before stacking or tinning the bars.

The icing cross: decoration only

Say it plainly, as the recipe does: the cross adds nothing to the flavour worth mentioning and nothing at all to the keeping or the structure. It is icing sugar and water. What it adds is recognition — a bar with a white cross reads instantly as the bun's cousin, which at Easter is half the pleasure — and a small extra sweetness on the first bite. Pipe it once the bars are cut and completely cool; on a warm bar the icing melts, spreads and disappears into the surface. If you skip it, nothing about the recipe changes, and for most of the year the plain spiced bar is the one to make. If you do pipe it, pipe after cutting, not before: a cross piped on the whole slab is cut in half by the knife and looks like a mistake.

Serving: warm, with tea, like the bun

A hot cross bun is eaten warm, split and buttered, and while a flapjack needs no butter — it is built on it — the warmth is worth copying. Ten minutes in a low oven brings a day-old bar back to something close to fresh-baked, the spice opening up again as it warms, though leave iced bars unheated or heat them only briefly, as the cross softens fast. These are tea bars: the spice and peel want a strong cup beside them. Baked for Easter they sit happily on the same table as the buns themselves, and the comparison in the photograph below is the honest one — the buns are the original; these bars are the borrow, and they do not pretend otherwise.

Storing the spiced bars

The bars keep about 5 days in an airtight tin at room temperature, with baking paper between the layers — if they carry icing crosses, the paper matters, as the crosses will stick to the bar above them. The spice, if anything, settles and improves over the first day or two. They freeze well for up to 3 months, wrapped individually and sealed; freeze them un-iced if you can, and pipe the crosses after defrosting, since icing goes dull and sticky in the freezer. Defrost at room temperature in the wrapping. The general rules are in our guide to how to store flapjacks and how long they last.
