Best Honey for Flapjacks: Clear, Set, Mild and Strong

By Michael

Honey in a flapjack does two jobs at once: it is a liquid sweetener that binds the oats, and it is a flavouring strong enough to define the whole bar. That second job is why the jar you choose matters more than it does in most baking. A mild blossom honey gives you a gentle, golden flapjack; a dark heather honey will take the bar over completely. This guide works through the types — clear and set, mild and strong, single-flower and blended — and what each one does in the tin.

The short answer: For a classic honey flapjack, use a mild, clear (runny) honey — an everyday blossom honey or a supermarket blend. It pours and weighs easily, melts straight into the butter, and flavours the bars without overwhelming the oats. Keep strong honeys like heather for small proportions or for bakers who want the honey to dominate. Set honey works just as well as runny, because it melts in the pan; comb honey is wasted in a bake — save it for toast. One safety rule stands above all of this: honey is not suitable for babies under 12 months.

What the honey has to do in the tin

Our honey flapjack recipe uses 200g of runny honey with 75g of light brown sugar — the honey is the main sweetener, not a spoonful of flavouring, so its character sets the character of the bake. In the pan it has to melt into the butter smoothly and coat 325g of oats evenly; in the oven it has to bind without scorching; in the finished bar it has to taste like honey rather than generic sweetness. Any honey does the first two jobs. The third is where the jars part company, and it is why "best honey" for flapjacks really means "best honey flavour for a bar that is mostly oats and butter".

Two practical truths before the types. First, runny honey measures like syrup: it pours, it weighs accurately, and a spoon dipped in hot water slides through it cleanly. Second, honey costs more than golden syrup — noticeably more per bake, and several times more for the interesting single-flower jars — so the choice of jar is a cost decision as well as a flavour one. The last section of this guide is honest about that.

Clear (runny) honey: the easy measure and the recipe standard

Clear honey — the runny, pourable kind — is what our recipe assumes, and for good reason. It goes into the pan without a fight, melts into the butter in moments, and coats the oats evenly, which is what an even set depends on. Flavour-wise, most clear honeys on an ordinary shelf are blossom honeys or blends: gently floral, sweet without an edge, and happy to sit behind butter and toasted oats while still tasting unmistakably of honey. For a first honey flapjack, or for baking for a mixed audience including children, this is the jar to buy.

Runny honey also keeps the baker honest on quantities. Honey is denser than water and heavier than it looks; a recipe written in grams, like ours, only works if the honey can actually be weighed, and runny honey pours onto the scale instead of arriving in unmeasurable spoonfuls. If your honey has started to crystallise in the jar — a natural process, not spoilage — stand the jar in warm water for ten minutes and it will pour again.

Strong and dark honeys: heather, chestnut and the jars that take over

At the other end of the shelf sit the strong honeys: heather honey, chestnut honey, and the dark, almost treacly jars from woodland flowers. These are wonderful things on toast and in dressings, but in a flapjack they do not share the stage. Heather honey is intensely aromatic, slightly bitter at the edges, and jelly-like in the jar; chestnut honey is dark and assertively bitter-sweet. Baked into 325g of oats, a full 200g of either will produce a bar that tastes of the honey first, the oats second and the butter hardly at all — and the bitterness, pleasant in a spoonful, grows in a whole bar.

The way to use them is as a proportion, not a replacement: swap 50g of the recipe's runny honey for 50g of heather or chestnut, and you get a bar with a distinct honey character and its balance intact. Bakers who love those flavours will enjoy the experiment; everyone else should treat the strong jars as flavourings and the mild jar as the workhorse. A jar of dark honey also colours the bake more deeply, so check the edges a few minutes early the first time you use one — dark mixtures look done before they are, and over-baked honey turns bitter.

Raw and unfiltered honey: what the words mean in a bake

"Raw" honey generally means honey that has not been heated much or finely filtered, so it keeps more of its pollen, wax flecks and local character; unfiltered jars can be cloudy and may crystallise sooner. In a flapjack, the honest position is that most of what makes raw honey distinctive does not survive the oven: the delicate aromatics that justify its price are exactly the ones a 25-minute bake at 160°C drives off, and the pollen and fine particles make no difference you could taste blind against a good ordinary honey in a bar this sweet and buttery.

That is not an argument against raw honey — it is an argument for eating it as it comes, on toast or over yoghurt, where you get what you paid for. For the baking tin, a sound everyday honey does the same work for less money. If a jar of raw honey is what you have, of course use it; the bars will be good. Just do not buy a premium raw jar specially for flapjacks expecting the premium to show.

Set honey and comb honey: both fine, one wasted

Set (crystallised) honey worries bakers unnecessarily. The crystals are glucose separating out — it happens fastest in honeys with a high glucose share, like rapeseed — and they melt away completely in the pan with the butter. A set honey makes exactly the same flapjack as its runny twin, with one practical difference: it is harder to weigh, because it spoons rather than pours. Spoon it into the pan on the scale, in lumps, and let the pan do the rest. Soft-set (creamed) honey is the easiest of the set kinds to handle and often mild in flavour, which suits flapjacks well.

Comb honey is the exception — the one form we would keep out of the tin. It is honey still in its wax comb, sold for eating as it is, and the wax is the problem: it melts in the pan into the mixture rather than vanishing, and nobody wants wax through their flapjack. It is also the most expensive way to buy honey, priced as a delicacy. Eat the comb on toast, where it belongs, and bake with the jar.

Cost honesty: honey is the expensive way to sweeten a flapjack

A flapjack sweetened with golden syrup costs pence in sweetener per batch. The same batch made with 200g of honey costs noticeably more, and with a single-flower or raw honey, more again — honey is simply a dearer ingredient, jar for jar, than syrup. That does not make the honey flapjack a bad bake; it makes it a bake to choose for its flavour, on purpose, rather than a default. The sensible pattern for most kitchens is a mild everyday honey — often a blend, which is blended precisely to be consistent and affordable — for regular baking, and the interesting jars kept for the occasions and the toast rack. Flavoured honeys (with added ginger, cinnamon and the like) sit in between: they bake fine and can be pleasant, but you are paying for flavouring you could add yourself for less, and the added flavours are dosed for spreading, not for a 325g oat mixture. Our post on honey versus golden syrup covers the wider comparison — sweetness, set and flavour — between the two sweeteners.

One more thing, and it is a safety point rather than a baking one: honey is not suitable for babies under 12 months, because it can contain bacteria that a baby's gut cannot handle. That applies to honey flapjacks exactly as it does to honey from the jar — baking does not remove the risk — so bars from our honey recipe are for older children and adults only.
