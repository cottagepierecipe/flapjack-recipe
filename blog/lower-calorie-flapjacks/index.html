Lower-Calorie Flapjacks: A Lighter Recipe and Portion Comparison

By Michael

These lower-calorie flapjacks keep the full 350g of oats and cut the butter and sugar instead, with mashed banana making up the difference so the bars stay moist. Every figure in this post is a calculated estimate, worked out from the ingredients — how is explained below — and the post makes the honest comparison too, including the one nobody selling a lighter recipe likes to make: what simply cutting the classic smaller achieves.

The short answer: Melt 125g butter, 100g golden syrup and 75g light muscovado sugar in a pan, stir in 150g mashed ripe banana, 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt, press into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 25–30 minutes. Makes 12 bars of about 67g, at about 248 kcal a bar — a calculated estimate, against the classic bar's 320 kcal.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

A lighter flapjack, stated plainly: what this recipe is and is not

Flapjack is a treat. It is oats bound with butter, syrup and sugar, and no rearrangement of those ingredients turns it into a health food. What this recipe does is narrower and more honest: it makes a recognisably proper flapjack — same tin, same method, same chew — that carries fewer calories per bar than our classic flapjack recipe, for people who would like the bar without quite so much of it. There are no sweeteners standing in for sugar, no strange substitutes, and no promises attached. It is a lighter version of a treat, and it tastes like one: slightly softer than the classic, with the banana there in the background rather than in charge.

The strategy is to cut where the calories are densest. Butter is by far the most calorie-dense ingredient in a flapjack, so the butter comes down from 175g to 125g; the syrup and sugar come down too, and 150g of mashed banana — mostly water and fruit sugar, at a fraction of the density — replaces the missing bulk and moisture. The oats stay at the full 350g, because the oats are the bar. Cut the oats and you have not made a lighter flapjack; you have made a smaller, sweeter one.

How the figures in this post were worked out

Every calorie figure here is a calculated estimate, and the method is simple enough to check. We took the exact ingredients and weights in the recipe below, multiplied each one by its published energy value per 100g — the standard reference figures used on food labels — added the batch up, and divided by 12 bars. Nothing was sent to a laboratory, and no figure should be read as exact: bananas vary, syrup spoons vary, and your oven does not change the calories but your cutting might. Read every number in this post as a good estimate, honest to within the natural variation of the ingredients. The classic's figures used for comparison are the site's verified figures for our classic recipe, worked out the same way.

Two comparisons follow from those figures, and both are given because they say different things. Per bar, the lighter flapjack is about 248 kcal against the classic's 320 kcal — about 72 kcal less, roughly 23% less. But the lighter bar is also slightly smaller, about 67g against the classic's larger cut, so the fair like-for-like comparison is per 100g: 371 kcal per 100g against 439 kcal per 100g, which is about 15% less. Both statements are true; the per-100g one is the honest measure of the recipe itself, and the per-bar one is what you actually eat.

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper, leaving overhang at two sides, and a large pan. Weigh the banana after peeling and mashing — 150g is the amount the figures are based on, and a couple of spoonfuls either way will not change them noticeably, but half a banana missing will change the texture: the banana is doing structural work here, replacing moisture the cut butter and syrup would have provided.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven, line the tin and mash the banana. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving overhang at two sides. Mash 150g of ripe banana thoroughly with a fork in a bowl — the smoother the mash, the more evenly it spreads through the mixture.
  2. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put 125g butter, 100g golden syrup and 75g light muscovado in a large pan over a low heat. Stir until everything has melted into one smooth liquid. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil — boiled syrup sets like toffee and makes hard bars.
  3. Stir in the banana. Take the pan off the heat and stir the mashed banana into the melt until it is evenly mixed through. The mixture will look paler and thicker than the classic melt — that is right.
  4. Add the oats and salt. Tip in 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt, and stir until every oat is coated. The mixture will be softer and wetter-looking than the classic mixture, because banana brings water with it; it sets as it bakes and cools.
  5. Press into the tin. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working well into the corners. With less syrup in the recipe, the press does more of the binding work than usual — do not skimp on it.
  6. Bake for 25–30 minutes. The top should be golden with the edges a shade darker. The centre will feel softer than a classic flapjack's when pressed — banana keeps it that way — but it should look set and dry on the surface, not wet.
  7. Cool completely and cut into 12. Leave the slab to cool fully in the tin; this softer mixture needs the full cooling time to set before it will cut cleanly. Lift the cold slab out using the paper overhang and cut into 12 bars with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down. Each bar is about 67g.

So which route should you take? If you want a full-size bar that eats like a flapjack and happens to carry about 23% fewer calories per bar — about 15% fewer like-for-like by weight — the lighter recipe is the better tool, because nobody has to feel short-changed. If you are happy with a smaller piece, the classic cut into 16 gives you the original flavour at about 240 kcal a piece, and the per-100g figure honestly admits nothing about the recipe changed. Plenty of people will do both: the lighter recipe, cut into 12, kept for everyday, and the classic made properly for occasions. That seems to us the right relationship with a treat.

Where the calories in a flapjack actually come from

The recipe above was designed from this table, and it is worth seeing, because it explains why the cuts fall where they do. These are the published energy values per 100g of the five ingredients — the same figures the calculations in this post are built from.

IngredientEnergy per 100gWhat it means for the recipe
Butter744 kcalThe dense one by a long way — every 25g you cut saves more than any other change
Light muscovado sugar392 kcalDense, but it also binds and flavours; cut with the syrup, not instead of it
Porridge oats370 kcalMiddle of the table — and they are the bar itself, so they stay
Golden syrup317 kcalLess dense than sugar, and it is the glue; 100g is about as low as binding allows
Banana89 kcalMostly water and fruit — which is why it can replace bulk at a quarter of butter's density

Read down that table and the recipe writes itself: the butter is where the calories concentrate, the banana is the only ingredient light enough to replace bulk cheaply, and the syrup has a floor below which the bars stop holding together. It also shows why there is no dramatic version of this recipe. A flapjack with the butter halved again is not a lighter flapjack — it is a dry oat slab that falls apart, because the ingredients that make it a flapjack are the ingredients in the table. About 15% per 100g is what honest adjustment buys, and this post would rather tell you that than pretend otherwise. If you are comparing lighter bakes generally, our healthy flapjack recipe takes a different route — dates and banana in place of most of the sugar — and our banana flapjack recipe is the one to read if the banana here is what caught your eye.

Storing the lighter bars: about 5 days in an airtight tin

The lighter bars keep about 5 days in an airtight tin at room temperature, with baking paper between the layers — the banana keeps them moist, and they stay softer than the classic throughout their life. Because they are a softer, moister bar, do not push them past the week, and in a warm kitchen keep the tin somewhere cool. They freeze well for up to 3 months, wrapped individually and sealed, and defrost at room temperature in their wrapping; a frozen bar taken out in the morning is ready by lunchtime, which makes the portioning easy to stick to. The general rules are in our guide to how to store flapjacks and how long they last.
