Double Chocolate Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

A double chocolate flapjack puts chocolate in the bar twice and on it once: cocoa powder in the mixture, so the base itself is chocolate rather than merely carrying chocolate, dark chocolate chips folded through, and a layer of melted dark chocolate spread over the top. It is the fullest chocolate version of the classic we make — rich, dark and firmly in pudding territory.

The short answer: Melt 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 150g light muscovado sugar, stir in 325g porridge oats, 25g cocoa powder, a pinch of salt and 100g dark chocolate chips, press into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes. Cool completely, spread 150g melted dark chocolate over the top, leave to set, and cut into 12 bars.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Double chocolate flapjacks: cocoa in the base, chips through it, chocolate on top

Most chocolate flapjacks are really oat flapjacks with chocolate involved somewhere — chips stirred in, or a topping poured over. This one starts from the other end. The 25g of cocoa powder in the mixture turns the base itself dark and chocolate-flavoured, so there is no plain mouthful anywhere in the bar. The chips then do what chips do — small pockets of solid chocolate in a baked matrix — and the topping finishes it with a clean, snappable layer. Three forms of chocolate, three different jobs.

The base is our classic flapjack recipe adjusted in one careful place: the cocoa replaces 25g of the oats, so the dry side of the recipe still totals 350g and the ratio of dry ingredients to butter, syrup and sugar — the ratio that decides whether a flapjack sets — stays exactly where the classic puts it. The sugar drops slightly, to 150g, because the chips and topping bring chocolate sweetness of their own. Our main chocolate flapjack recipe page covers each of the three routes separately; this recipe is what happens when you take all three at once.

The cocoa swap: 25g of cocoa in place of 25g of oats

Replacing oats with cocoa, rather than just adding cocoa on top of the full oat quantity, is the detail that keeps these bars setting properly. Cocoa powder is a dry, thirsty ingredient: it absorbs moisture from the melted butter and syrup as it mixes. Add 25g of it to the full 350g of oats and the mixture comes out stiffer and drier than intended, and the bars bake crumbly. Swap it in — 325g of oats plus 25g of cocoa — and the balance holds. You will still notice the mixture is a shade stiffer than the classic's; that is the cocoa drinking, and it is normal.

Sift the cocoa, or at least break up its lumps with a spoon before it goes in. Cocoa clumps stubbornly, and a clump stirred into the mixture stays a clump — a dry, bitter pocket in one bar and none in the next. Tipped in with the oats and stirred well, it coats everything evenly and the whole mixture turns a uniform dark brown, which is also how you know it has mixed through. As for which cocoa: natural and Dutch-processed both work. Dutch-processed is darker and smoother, natural is a touch sharper and lighter in colour; in a bar this rich the difference is small, so use whichever is in the cupboard.

The chips: folded in off the heat, so they stay chips

The chocolate chips go in with the oats, off the heat, and that timing is deliberate. The melted butter mixture is warm enough to soften the edges of the chips and help them grip the oats, but not hot enough to melt them away into the base — you want them to survive the mixing as distinct pieces and re-set in the baked bar as pockets. Stir them through in a few turns of the spoon rather than beating at the pan: the longer you stir, the more the residual heat works on them.

Dark chocolate chips around 70% cocoa give the clearest contrast against the cocoa base. Chopped bar chocolate works in the same 100g quantity and melts a little further, giving streaks rather than pockets — a different texture, not a worse one. Milk chocolate chips are fine too but push the whole bar sweeter and softer; if you use them, the topping becomes the place to restore the balance, so keep that dark. What matters most is that the chips are chocolate you would happily eat on their own, because in a bar this simple their flavour is fully on show.

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper, a large pan, and a heatproof bowl that sits over a pan of barely simmering water for melting the topping chocolate. A sieve for the cocoa earns its place too — two minutes of sifting saves a bar full of bitter lumps.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides for lifting the finished slab out later.
  2. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 150g light muscovado in a large pan over a low heat. Stir until everything has melted into one smooth liquid. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil — boiled syrup sets like toffee and makes hard bars.
  3. Add the oats, cocoa, salt and chips. Take the pan off the heat. Tip in 325g porridge oats, 25g sifted cocoa powder and a pinch of salt and stir until the mixture is a uniform dark brown with no pale streaks. Then add 100g dark chocolate chips and fold them through in a few turns, so they soften at the edges but stay whole.
  4. Press into the tin. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working well into the corners. This mixture is stiffer than the classic's because of the cocoa, so firm pressing matters even more than usual.
  5. Bake for 30–35 minutes. A dark bake gives you no colour cues, so judge by the edges and the surface: the edges should look set and very slightly pulled from the sides, and the centre should feel firm at the edges and just soft in the middle when pressed. It firms up a great deal as it cools. Leave the slab to cool completely in the tin.
  6. Melt the topping chocolate. Break 150g dark chocolate into a heatproof bowl set over a pan of barely simmering water, the bowl not touching the water. Stir now and then until smooth and fully melted, then take the bowl off the pan.
  7. Spread the chocolate over the cold bake. Pour the melted chocolate over the cooled slab, still in its tin, and spread it to the corners in an even layer with the back of a spoon or a palette knife. The bake must be cold — chocolate spread on a warm base sweats, slides and sets streaky.
  8. Leave it to set. Leave the tin at room temperature until the chocolate is firm and matte rather than glossy — about an hour. Fridge-set chocolate is more likely to crack when cut.
  9. Lift out and cut into 12. Use the paper overhang to lift the cold slab onto a board. Cut with a large sharp knife at room temperature, pressing straight down; warm the blade under the hot tap and dry it between cuts if the topping threatens to crack.

Double chocolate problems: dry bars, bitter pockets, cracked tops

A dark bake hides its own evidence — you cannot read colour the way you can on a plain flapjack — so this recipe's problems tend to be about judgement and about the cocoa. The table below covers the usual ones. The short version: swap the cocoa in rather than adding it, sift it, and judge the bake by touch and edges rather than by looking for golden.

What happenedLikely causeThe fix next time
Bars are dry and crumblyCocoa added on top of the full oats, or the bake ran longKeep the swap — 325g oats plus 25g cocoa — and judge the bake by the edges, not the clock alone
Bitter pockets in some barsUnsifted cocoa clumpsSift the cocoa or break up every lump, and stir until the mixture is one even colour
Chocolate cracked as the bars were cutTopping set too cold, or cut straight from the fridgeSet and cut at room temperature; warm the knife blade between cuts
Whole bar tastes flat despite all the chocolateSalt missed out, or low-cocoa chocolate throughoutKeep the pinch of salt and use chocolate around 70% cocoa for chips and topping
Chips vanished into the baseMixture stirred too long while hotFold chips in off the heat, in a few turns, and stop as soon as they are spread through

Judging a bake you cannot see brown

The hardest part of this recipe is knowing when it is done, because every visual cue bakers rely on — golden top, darker edges — is hidden under cocoa. Work by the other senses instead. The surface should look matte and set rather than wet and shiny. The edges should feel firm and have pulled very slightly away from the paper. Pressed gently in the centre, the slab should spring a little rather than denting and staying dented. And the whole tin should smell of warm chocolate rather than of raw cocoa. Any three of those four and the bake is done; the centre finishes setting as it cools, which is why the 30–35 minutes comes with cues and not just a number.

If you want a finish beyond the plain topping, a drift of grated chocolate over the still-wet layer melts in at the edges and sets as a rough, matte coat — pretty, and it hides any spreading imperfections. A pinch of flaky sea salt scattered over the wet chocolate is the other worthwhile addition: against this much chocolate, salt reads as more flavour, not as saltiness. Both go on within the first minute after spreading, before the surface starts to skin over.

Storing double chocolate flapjacks: about 5 days in an airtight tin

Keep the cut bars in an airtight tin at room temperature, with baking paper between the layers so the chocolate tops do not mark each other, and they keep about 5 days. Keep the tin somewhere cool and dark — a warm kitchen or a sunny shelf can bloom the chocolate, a pale grey film that looks alarming and tastes of nothing much, but a cool cupboard avoids the question entirely. The bars freeze well for up to 3 months, wrapped individually and sealed; defrost in the wrapping at room temperature so the topping does not sweat. The general rules are in our guide to how to store flapjacks and how long they last.

Both work, and in a bar this rich the difference is modest. Dutch-processed cocoa is treated to neutralise its acidity, so it tastes smoother and bakes darker — nearly black against the oats. Natural cocoa is lighter in colour and a little sharper. Neither needs the recipe adjusting; there is no raising agent in a flapjack for the acidity to react with. Use whichever you have, and spend the effort on sifting it instead.

It is not a like-for-like swap. Drinking chocolate is mostly sugar with some cocoa in it, so 25g of it adds a spoonful of extra sugar and only a fraction of the cocoa's flavour and colour — the base comes out paler, sweeter and barely chocolate-tasting. If it is all you have, the bars will still set and still eat well, but they will be a light chocolate version of this recipe rather than the double chocolate one. Proper cocoa powder is worth the cupboard space.
