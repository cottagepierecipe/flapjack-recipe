Lower-Fat Flapjacks: Half the Fat, Honestly Worked Out

By Michael

Most lighter flapjacks cut everything a little. This one cuts one thing a lot: the butter drops from the classic's 175g to 75g, with mashed banana replacing the lost moisture, while the syrup and sugar stay near their classic levels so the bar still binds, chews and tastes like a flapjack. Every figure in this post is a calculated estimate, worked out ingredient by ingredient below — and the post is plain about the limits of the result, starting with this one: at about half the classic's fat, these are lower-fat flapjacks, not low-fat ones, and the difference between those phrases matters.

The short answer: Melt 75g butter, 150g golden syrup and 150g light muscovado sugar in a pan, stir in 200g mashed ripe banana, 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt, press into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 25–30 minutes (estimate). Makes 12 bars of about 77g. As a calculated estimate, each bar carries about 258 kcal and about 7.3g of fat — roughly half the classic bar's 14g of fat.

Not yet kitchen-tested: this recipe adapts our tested classic flapjack and has not been cooked by us yet. Michael tests every new recipe himself, and this page will be updated once he has cooked it. Times marked (estimate) are estimates.

Lower-fat, not low-fat: the honest starting point

Words first, because this corner of baking is full of loose ones. In the UK, a food can only be called "low fat" if it contains 3g of fat or less per 100g. These flapjacks, on the calculated estimate set out below, contain about 9.5g of fat per 100g — a long way from that threshold, because oats themselves carry fat and a flapjack with almost no fat in it is not a flapjack at all. What this recipe honestly is: a flapjack with about half the fat of our classic flapjack recipe, like for like — about 7.3g of fat in a bar against the classic's 14g, and about 9.5g per 100g against about 19.4g. That is a large, real reduction, achieved without sweeteners, bulking agents or anything that does not belong in a baking tin. It is also the reduction that is actually available, and this post would rather show you its working than dress the result in a claim it has not earned.

The strategy is the mirror image of our lower-calorie flapjacks. That recipe cut the butter, the syrup and the sugar together, aiming at calories, and landed at about 248 kcal a bar with the fat still around 11g. This recipe aims at the fat specifically: the butter alone takes the cut — 100g of it gone — while the syrup and sugar stay at 150g each, close to the classic's 175g, so the binding and the chew survive almost intact. The calories fall less, to about 258 kcal a bar, because sugar carries calories without carrying fat. Two different levers, two different bars; the comparison table further down puts them side by side.

The butter cut: why 75g, and what replaces the rest

Butter is where a flapjack's fat lives. On the published figures used throughout this site, butter is about 82% fat, so the classic's 175g brings roughly 144g of fat into the batch before anything else is counted. Cut the butter to 75g and you remove about 82g of fat from the batch at a stroke — more than the oats, syrup and sugar could ever contribute between them. No other single change comes close, which is why this recipe makes it and leaves the rest of the formula nearly alone.

But butter is not only fat. It is also moisture and coating: it melts, wraps every oat, and carries the syrup through the mixture. Take 100g of it away and something has to do that work, or the bars come out dry and loose. Here, 200g of mashed ripe banana does it — banana is mostly water with a little sugar and starch, it coats oats readily, and at about 0.3g of fat per 100g it replaces butter's bulk at almost none of butter's fat. The flavour cost is real and stated now rather than discovered later: the bars taste faintly of banana, and they are softer than the classic, because banana sets softly where butter sets firm. Section by section, that trade is the recipe.

How the figures in this post were worked out

Every figure here is a calculated estimate, produced the same way as the figures in our lower-calorie post, and the method is simple enough to check at home. We took the exact ingredients and weights in the recipe below, multiplied each by its published value per 100g — the standard reference figures used on food labels — added the batch up and divided by 12 bars. The batch comes to about 3,095 kcal in total: butter 558, golden syrup about 476, light muscovado 588, banana 178 and oats 1,295. Divided by 12, that is about 258 kcal a bar. The fat adds up the same way: about 88g in the whole batch, or about 7.3g a bar. Nothing was sent to a laboratory. Bananas vary, hands with syrup vary, and your cutting will not be perfectly even, so read every number as a good estimate — honest to within the natural variation of the ingredients — and the classic's figures used for comparison are the site's verified panel for our classic recipe, worked out the same way.

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper, leaving overhang at two sides, and a large pan. Use properly ripe bananas — yellow and well freckled — because their job here is moisture and mild sweetness, and an under-ripe banana is starchy, dry and short on both. Weigh the banana after peeling and mashing; 200g is the amount the figures above are based on.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven, line the tin and mash the banana. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving overhang at two sides. Mash 200g of ripe banana thoroughly with a fork until smooth.
  2. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put 75g butter, 150g golden syrup and 150g light muscovado in a large pan over a low heat. Stir until everything has melted into one smooth liquid, and never let it boil — boiled syrup sets like toffee.
  3. Stir in the banana. Take the pan off the heat and stir the mashed banana through the melt until it is evenly mixed. The mixture will look paler and thicker than the classic melt; that is right.
  4. Add the oats and salt. Tip in 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt, and stir until every oat is coated. The mixture will be softer and moister-looking than the classic's, because the banana brings water with it.
  5. Press into the tin. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working well into the corners. With less butter in the recipe, the press carries more of the binding work than usual.
  6. Bake for 25–30 minutes (estimate). The top should be golden with the edges a shade darker. The centre will feel softer than a classic flapjack's when pressed — the banana keeps it that way — but the surface should look set and dry, not wet.
  7. Cool completely and cut into 12. Leave the slab to cool fully in the tin; this softer mixture needs the whole cooling time to firm up. Lift out using the paper overhang and cut into 12 bars with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down. Each bar is about 77g.

Read the fat rows and the recipes' characters are plain. Like for like, per 100g, the lower-fat bar carries about half the classic's fat — 9.5g against about 19.4g — and less than two-thirds of the lower-calorie bar's, because that recipe spread its cuts across everything and kept most of its butter's share of the mixture. Read the sugars row and the price shows just as plainly: this recipe barely touches the sugar, so its bars stay sweet and its calories fall by only about a fifth per bar, where the lower-calorie recipe cut deeper. Neither is the "better" recipe. If fat is the figure you are watching, this is the bar; if calories or sugars are, the lower-calorie flapjack is. And if what you want is simply a smaller piece of the real thing, the classic cut into 16 lands at about 240 kcal a piece with its fat per piece down near 10g — portioning is a lever too, and an honest post should say so.

Texture and keeping: softer bars, and they will not crisp the same way

Set expectations before the first bite. Butter is what makes a flapjack's edges crisp and its chew dense; with 100g of it gone and banana in its place, these bars are softer, moister and more yielding, with a gentle banana note behind the syrup rather than the classic's clean butter flavour. They do not crisp the same way, and longer in the oven is not the fix — extra baking dries the oats while the centre stays banana-soft, and the sugars at the edges scorch. Take them out when the surface looks set and dry, and let cooling do the firming. They keep about 5 days in an airtight tin at room temperature, staying softer than the classic throughout, and they freeze well for up to 3 months wrapped individually — a frozen bar defrosts by lunchtime, which suits a portion-planned bake. The general rules are in our guide to how to store flapjacks, and if a lighter bake with no banana at all is what you are after, our healthy flapjack recipe takes yet another route, through dates.
