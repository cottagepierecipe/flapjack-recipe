British Flapjacks vs American Flapjacks: What's the Difference?

By Michael

Ask for a flapjack in Britain and you will be handed a baked oat bar. Ask for one in the United States or Canada and you will be handed a pancake. Same word, two different foods — here is how each one is made, where the word came from, and what to ask for on the other side of the Atlantic.

The short answer: A British flapjack is a baked bar of oats, butter, sugar and golden syrup, cooked in a tin and cut into squares. An American "flapjack" is a griddle pancake — a batter cooked in a pan, served in a stack, usually at breakfast. The British bar is the one this site is about; in North America the word is simply a regional synonym for "pancake" or "hotcake". Neither side is using the word wrongly: they kept two different descendants of the same old word for a flat cooked food.

The British flapjack: a baked oat bar from a tin

The British flapjack is a tray bake. Porridge oats are stirred into melted butter, golden syrup and sugar, pressed firmly into a lined tin and baked until the edges are golden — our classic flapjack recipe uses 175g butter, 175g golden syrup, 175g light brown sugar and 350g oats in a 20cm square tin at 160°C. The slab cools in the tin, where the syrup mixture sets and binds the oats, and is then cut into bars or squares.

What you end up with is dense, chewy and portable: a bar that keeps for days in a tin, travels in a lunchbox without complaint and is eaten cold, by hand, usually with tea or coffee rather than as part of a cooked meal. It belongs to the same family of British baking as the traybake, the oat slice and the school-dinner bake. Variations run from the plain classic through our fruit flapjack to chocolate-topped and chewy versions, but the defining facts never change: oats, a syrup-bound mixture, an oven, a tin, a knife.

Two of those facts are worth underlining, because they are where the confusion with the American food starts. A British flapjack contains no flour and no batter — there is nothing pourable about it at any stage — and it is never cooked on a hob. If a recipe involves a frying pan, it is not describing the British bar.

The American flapjack: a pancake by another name

In the United States and Canada, "flapjack" means a pancake. It is not a different dish from the pancake — it is another name for the same dish, used more in some regions and generations than others, alongside "hotcake" and "griddle cake". An American flapjack starts as a batter of flour, eggs, milk and raising agent, is ladled onto a hot griddle or frying pan, and is flipped partway through cooking — the "flap" is thought to be part of the word's history, a reference to the toss or turn of the pan.

American flapjacks are eaten hot, in a stack, at breakfast, typically with butter and maple syrup. They are soft, light and perishable — food for the next ten minutes, not the next five days. Nothing about them involves oats as a defining ingredient, a baking tin, or a knife-and-slab moment at the end. Put a British flapjack and an American one side by side and the only things they share are a name and a roughly flat outline.

One word, two foods: where "flapjack" came from

The split is not a modern mistake; both uses are old. The word "flapjack" is recorded in the early 16th century for a flat tart or pancake-like food — something thin, flat and cooked on both sides or in a pan. Shakespeare uses "flap-jacks" in Pericles, and in that world the word belongs to the pan, not the baking tin. North American English kept that original meaning and applied it to the griddle pancake.

The British oat bar arrived much later. Golden syrup, the ingredient that defines the modern flapjack, dates from the 1880s, and the first recorded use of "flapjack" for the oat-based food is in 1935. So the British bar is the newcomer that borrowed the old flat food's name — plausibly because the slab is flat and is turned out of its tin — while the American pancake kept the meaning the word had carried for centuries. Neither usage is a corruption of the other; they are two branches of the same family tree, which is precisely why the confusion never dies: both sides can point to history and be right.

Ordering and searching across the Atlantic: what to ask for

The practical trouble starts when recipes, menus and shops cross the ocean. A British cook searching an American recipe site for "flapjack" will be offered pancake recipes all day; an American cook searching a British site for the same word will be handed oat bars and wonder where the batter is. The fixes are simple once you know which side of the word you are standing on.

In Britain. "Flapjack" on a café board, a bakery shelf or a recipe site means the oat bar, always. If you want the American dish, search and ask for "American pancakes" or "Scotch pancakes" for the smaller, thicker style — the word "flapjack" will not get you there. British shops sell flapjacks ready-made in the cake and cereal-bar aisles, individually wrapped or in multipacks.

In the United States and Canada. "Flapjack" on a diner menu means pancakes; order them expecting a stack, not a bar. If you want the British bar, the word will not find it for you — search for British recipes by the full phrase "British flapjack recipe" and check the ingredients for oats and golden syrup before you start, or look in shops for the nearest shelf items, which are sold as "oat bars" or "granola bars". Those are cousins, not twins: granola bars are usually built from toasted oats with different binders and a crunchier, drier bite, and many are closer to a biscuit bar than to the chewy, syrup-set British flapjack. The only reliable route to the real thing in North America is to bake it, which is also the easy route — the ingredients are ordinary, golden syrup is the only item that may take a British-foods shelf or an online order to find.

One more trap for readers of older books: because the oat-bar meaning only dates from 1935, a flapjack in a British novel or cookbook from before then is the flat pan-cooked food, not the bar. And an American recipe titled "flapjacks" from any era is pancakes. The date of the book and the side of the Atlantic it was printed on will tell you which food is meant faster than the title will.

If the bar is what you are after, our guide to storing flapjacks covers keeping them once baked — about 5 days in an airtight tin — and the classic recipe linked at the top of this page is the place to start baking.

Telling the two apart in a recipe: oats and a tin, or flour and a pan

If you find a recipe online simply titled "flapjacks", the title alone will not tell you which food it makes — but the recipe itself gives the answer in seconds, before you have bought a single ingredient. Run through three checks. First, the dry ingredients: oats by the hundreds of grams means the British bar; flour, with eggs and milk, means the American pancake. Second, the equipment: a lined square tin and an oven means the bar; a griddle, frying pan or hotplate means the pancake. Third, the ending: a British flapjack recipe finishes with cooling and cutting — the words "cool in the tin" or "cut into bars" are a certain sign — while an American one finishes at the stove, with stacking and serving while hot.

The same test works on menus. A British café listing flapjack among the cakes and traybakes, priced by the slice, is selling the bar; an American diner listing flapjacks among the breakfast plates, with eggs and bacon alongside, is selling pancakes. Context is the dictionary here: the word travels, but the food it names is always the local one.

There is one more modern wrinkle worth knowing. Wrapped snack bars sold in North America under British-style branding, and British coffee-shop chains trading in the US, have begun carrying the oat bar across the Atlantic under its British name. So the word is slowly gaining its second meaning in North America — but slowly is the operative word, and for now the safe assumption stands: in Britain, flapjack means the bar; in the US and Canada, unless the context clearly says otherwise, it means the pancake.
