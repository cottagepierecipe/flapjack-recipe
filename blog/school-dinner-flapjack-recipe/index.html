School Dinner Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

A school dinner flapjack is the plainest bake on this site: a big tray of oats, butter, sugar and syrup, cut thick and served warm with custard. No fruit, no chocolate, no extras — that plainness is the whole point. This is the school spec scaled for a home kitchen: a 20×30cm tin, fifteen thick squares, and the custard very much part of the recipe.

The short answer: Melt 200g butter, 200g light brown sugar and 4 tablespoons of golden syrup in a pan, stir in 400g porridge oats and a pinch of salt, press into a lined 20×30cm tin and bake at 180°C (160°C fan, Gas Mark 4) for 25–30 minutes. Cut into 15 thick squares while warm, and serve with custard.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

The school dinner flapjack: a big tray, a thick cut, no extras

Ask anyone who ate British school dinners and the flapjack comes up early, usually alongside the words "and custard". It was the reliable pudding: cheap to make in huge trays, sturdy enough to portion with a fish slice at speed, and sweet enough to be looked forward to. The recipe school kitchens worked from was a caterer's formula, not a home one — a shallow catering tray, a mixture of oats bound with fat, sugar and syrup, baked until set, and cut in the tray into a grid of generous squares. What this post does is keep that spec exactly, at a size a home oven and a home tin can manage.

The proportions differ slightly from our classic flapjack recipe, and deliberately. The classic is baked in a 20cm square tin and cut into 12 bars of a snacking size. The school version spreads a larger batch across a 20×30cm tin, which makes the slab a little thinner per square centimetre but the squares themselves bigger — the tin's area divides into 15 portions, each one a pudding portion rather than a snack. The oven temperature is a touch higher too, 180°C rather than 160°C, because the bigger, shallower slab needs the edges to set and colour in the time the middle takes to cook through. If you are scaling flapjacks up or down for other tins, our guide to adjusting a flapjack recipe for a different tin size gives the working for any tray you own.

Butter or margarine: what school kitchens actually used

The honest answer is margarine — block margarine, bought in catering packs, because it was cheap and it kept. That is the period-accurate choice, and plenty of people chasing the exact taste of their school dinners swear the margarine version is the right one: slightly less rich, slightly more savoury, with a firmer set when cold. Butter tastes better. There is no getting round it — the butter version has a flavour the margarine one does not, and it is the version we bake and the version written above.

Both work, and the method does not change whichever you choose, because the fat's job here is structural as much as anything: it carries the sugar and syrup through the oats and sets as the slab cools. What does not work is a soft tub spread. Tub spreads contain more water and less fat than block margarine, and the extra water steams in the bake and leaves the slab soft and greasy. If you use margarine, use the hard block kind, wrapped in foil. The comparison is set out in full in our post on butter, margarine or spread for flapjacks.

Golden syrup4 tbspAbout 80g. Weigh it if your spooning is generous
Porridge oats400gStandard porridge oats — the cheap ones are the authentic ones here
Fine sea saltA pinchKeeps the sweetness from tasting flat
CustardTo serveA jug of it, hot. Packet, tinned or homemade all count

A word on the sugar. Many school recipes used demerara, whose coarse crystals do not fully dissolve in the melt, so the finished square has a slight crunch at the edges and a more caramelised top. Light brown sugar dissolves completely and gives the smoother, chewier bar. Both are correct; they are just two schools' worth of difference. If you use demerara, give the melt an extra minute of stirring over the heat so the crystals start to go, and expect the slab to need the full 30 minutes. You will also need a 20×30cm tin lined with baking paper — line the base and two long sides, because this slab is wide and wants support when it comes out.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Set the oven to 180°C (160°C fan, Gas Mark 4). Line a 20×30cm tin with baking paper, leaving overhang along the two long sides.
  2. Melt the butter, sugar and syrup. Put 200g butter, 200g light brown sugar and 4 tablespoons of golden syrup in a large pan over a low heat. Stir until everything has melted into one smooth liquid. Keep it gentle and do not let it boil — boiled syrup sets like toffee and makes hard squares.
  3. Add the oats and salt. Take the pan off the heat, tip in 400g porridge oats and a pinch of salt, and stir until every oat is coated and shiny. The mixture will be stiff and heavy — that is right; there is a lot of oat in this batch.
  4. Press into the tin. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working into the corners and keeping the surface level. An even thickness matters in a big tray: thin patches overcook while thick ones are still soft.
  5. Bake for 25–30 minutes. The top should be golden all over with the edges a shade darker and just beginning to pull away from the paper. The centre should look set and feel firm-ish when pressed — it firms further as it cools.
  6. Cut while warm, into 15. Leave the slab in the tin for 10 minutes, then cut it in the tin with a large knife: three strips along the length, five across, making 15 squares. Cutting in the tin, in a grid, is how traybakes are portioned — it keeps every square the same size. Leave the squares in the tin until cold if you are not serving them warm.
  7. Serve warm with custard. If the squares have cooled, warm them through in a low oven for 5 minutes or in the microwave for a few seconds each, and serve in bowls with hot custard poured over. Warm flapjack soaks up custard at the edges and stays chewy in the middle — that contrast is the pudding.

Why custard? The logic of the school pudding

The custard was not an afterthought, and it was not just generosity. A plain flapjack square is dense, sweet and dry in the mouth — on its own it is a biscuit-tin bake. Poured over with hot custard it becomes a pudding: the custard brings the moisture the square lacks, softens its edges, and its mild vanilla blandness is exactly the foil a very sweet square needs. School kitchens also worked to a budget and a timetable, and custard from powder is quick, cheap and forgiving — it can be held hot and ladled at speed, which matters when you are serving two hundred children. The pairing survived because it works, not just because it is remembered fondly.

At home, use whatever custard you like. A tinned custard, warmed, is closest to the dinner-hall version; packet custard made slightly thicker than the instructions say coats the square better; homemade custard is better than either and takes ten minutes. What matters is that it is hot and there is plenty of it. A square served without custard is a fine flapjack; with custard, it is the school dinner.

Two practical notes on scaling. A double batch in one deep tray bakes thicker and needs an extra 5 minutes, and the middle squares will be softer — acceptable, but two standard trays side by side give the more even bake. And if you are baking tray after tray, the mixture holds well in the pan off the heat while the first tray bakes; just stir it before spooning it out, because the syrup settles.

Storing school dinner flapjacks: about 5 days in an airtight tin

The squares keep about 5 days in an airtight tin at room temperature, with baking paper between layers — school flapjack was built to keep, and this one does. It is, if anything, better on day two, when the slab has settled into its chew. Keep back a few squares for the full treatment later in the week: warmed through and served with custard, a four-day-old square is indistinguishable from a fresh one. For longer keeping, freeze the squares wrapped and sealed for up to 3 months, and defrost at room temperature in the wrapping. Custard itself does not freeze with the square — make or warm that fresh. The general rules are in our guide to how to store flapjacks and how long they last.

Questions people ask

At 180°C the edges set and colour in the time the middle takes to cook through. At the classic's 160°C the bigger tray comes out pale and soft in the centre while the corners dry. The higher heat is a tray-size adjustment, not a different kind of bake.

Usually one of three things: the mixture was pressed too loosely into a big tin, so it never compacted; the slab was cut fridge-cold, when the set butter makes it brittle; or the oats were jumbo oats, which bind less well than porridge oats. Press firmly and evenly, cut the slab while it is still warm (10 minutes out of the oven) in the tin, and use standard porridge oats, and the grid cuts cleanly.

School custard was made from custard powder — cornflour, colouring and flavouring — mixed with milk and sugar, and held hot in a bain-marie. Packet custard powder made up at home is the closest match; tinned custard is a little richer and sweeter. Make it slightly thicker than the packet says: a school-dinner pour should coat the square, not run off it like milk.

If you want a fruit version, our fruit flapjack and raisin versions keep the same base logic with the extras folded through. For a school tray with something in it, the most period-plausible addition is 100g of raisins stirred through the oats; dinner ladies did occasionally ring that change.

Warm, not hot, is the aim — you want the fat in the square to soften, not melt out. Five minutes in an oven at 150°C, or 15–20 seconds in a microwave, is enough for one square. Serve it in a bowl straight away with the custard poured over while both are warm; a reheated square left standing goes back to being a cold flapjack within minutes.
