Condensed Milk Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

One tin of sweetened condensed milk does two jobs in this flapjack: it takes the place of the golden syrup and it carries most of the sweetness too, so the sugar drops to a quarter of the classic's amount. The milk solids in it change the bake as well — the bars come out softer and fudgier, pale gold and milky-tasting, and they brown faster than the classic ever does.

The short answer: Melt 175g butter, one 397g tin of sweetened condensed milk and 75g light muscovado sugar. Stir in 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt. Press into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 25–30 minutes (estimate) — shorter than the classic, because condensed milk browns quickly. Cool completely in the tin and cut into 12 bars.

Not yet kitchen-tested: this recipe adapts our tested classic flapjack and has not been cooked by us yet. Michael tests every new recipe himself, and this page will be updated once he has cooked it. Times marked (estimate) are estimates.

Condensed milk flapjacks: one tin doing two jobs

The base here is our classic flapjack recipe, the kitchen-tested version: butter, golden syrup and light muscovado melted together, 350g of oats stirred in, pressed into a 20cm tin. In this version the tin of condensed milk steps into two of those roles at once. It is liquid and sticky, so it coats the oats and binds the bars the way syrup does. It is also heavily sweetened — sweetened condensed milk is milk with much of the water removed and a large amount of sugar added — so it brings most of the sweetness with it, and the muscovado falls from 175g to 75g. What stays is the small amount of sugar that gives depth and colour; what goes is the rest.

If you have met sweetened condensed milk before, it may well have been outside baking altogether — it is the thick, pale, glossy milk that some people spread straight onto toast, where it sits like a sweet glaze. That gives you the right picture of what goes into the pan: pourable but heavy, sweet enough to be a topping on its own, and rich with milk. In the flapjack it melts into the butter, coats the oats and then sets them into bars that are softer and more fudge-like than the classic, with a milky, almost caramel flavour that is quite different from the syrup version. The closest comparison in spirit is a millionaire-style topping softened into a bar — sweet, pale and rich rather than dark and treacly.

Condensed milk is not evaporated milk: the swap that will not work

Here is the trap this recipe exists partly to warn about. Condensed milk and evaporated milk sit side by side in shops, come in similar tins, and are made by a similar process — milk with much of its water removed. The difference is the one thing that matters: sweetened condensed milk has a large amount of sugar added; evaporated milk has none. Evaporated milk is essentially concentrated unsweetened milk, made for pouring into coffee or cooking down into sauces.

Swap the tins and the recipe fails in a specific way. Without the sugar, there is not enough sweetness, but the bigger problem is the set: the sugar in condensed milk is part of what firms the bars as they cool, and an evaporated-milk batch comes out pale, bland and loose, more like baked porridge than a flapjack — it will not slice into clean bars. If evaporated milk is what you have, you do not have a version of this recipe; you have the makings of the classic instead, where the syrup and sugar do the work properly. Our guide to the best sugar for flapjacks explains what sugar is actually doing in the mixture, which is the same job the tin is doing here.

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper and a large pan. Lining matters a little more than usual with this recipe: condensed milk mixtures are keen to catch and stick at the edges, and paper all the way up the sides gives you a clean release. Scrape the tin out thoroughly with a flexible spatula — condensed milk clings, and what stays in the tin is a noticeable share of your binder.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, bringing the paper up the sides and leaving a little overhang at two sides for lifting the slab out later.
  2. Melt the butter, condensed milk and sugar. Put 175g butter, the whole 397g tin of sweetened condensed milk and 75g light muscovado in a large pan over a low heat. Stir steadily until the butter has melted and everything is one smooth, pale, glossy liquid. Keep the heat low and keep stirring — condensed milk scorches on the base of a pan more readily than syrup does, and never let it boil.
  3. Add the oats and salt. Take the pan off the heat and tip in 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt. Stir until every oat is coated and there are no dry patches. The mixture will look looser and paler than the classic's — that is right.
  4. Press into the tin. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working well into the corners. A firm, even press now is what gives you clean-cutting bars later.
  5. Bake for 25–30 minutes (estimate). Shorter than the classic's 30–35, because the milk solids and sugar in condensed milk brown fast. The top should be pale gold with the edges deepening first, and the centre should look set. If your oven browns the top quickly, put the tin on a lower shelf instead and give it closer to the classic's time (estimate). Check from 20 minutes — the gap between golden and caught is short with this mixture.
  6. Score while warm. About 5 minutes after the tin comes out, mark the portion lines with a table knife — 12 bars — while the top is soft enough not to crack.
  7. Cool completely in the tin. Leave the slab until it is cold before cutting. Condensed milk flapjacks set soft and fudgy, and they firm up a great deal as they cool; cut warm, they will squash and smear rather than slice.
  8. Lift out and cut. Use the paper overhang to lift the cold slab onto a board and cut through along the scored lines with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down. A knife wiped between cuts keeps the fudgy edges tidy.

Softer, fudgier, quicker to brown: what the milk solids do

Condensed milk brings milk solids into a flapjack, and milk solids change how a bake behaves in ways worth knowing before you troubleshoot. Sugars and proteins together brown fast in heat — it is the same browning that gives baked milk puddings and flans their golden tops — which is why this version can catch at the edges while the classic, at the same temperature, is still deciding. It is also why the bars are softer: the set here comes from sugar and butter as usual, but the milk keeps the texture tender and slightly chewy rather than crisp, a fudge-like bite instead of a biscuity one.

That softness is the character of the recipe, not a fault — but it does mean the failure mode to guard against is a batch cut too soon and judged underdone. Give the slab its full cooling time, and if the first batch still feels too soft for your taste, the honest fixes are a couple of extra minutes in the oven (estimate) or a slightly firmer press, not extra oats, which would dry the bars out. If a batch does crumble, our guide to why flapjacks fall apart works through the causes in order.

Storing condensed milk flapjacks

Keep the cut bars in an airtight tin at room temperature with baking paper between the layers. They keep about 5 days; being softer and richer, they are at their best in the first three. They freeze well — wrap the bars individually, seal them in a bag or box and use within 3 months, defrosting at room temperature in the wrapping; the fudgy texture survives freezing better than most. There is more detail in our guide to how to store flapjacks and how long they last.
