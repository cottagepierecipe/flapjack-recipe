Seeded Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

A seeded flapjack keeps everything that makes the classic good — the butter, golden syrup and muscovado mixture, the chewy set, the golden edges — and swaps some of the oats for a mix of seeds. Sunflower, pumpkin, sesame and linseed bring a nuttier flavour, a firmer bite and a bar that cuts into neat, speckled slices.

The short answer: Take the classic flapjack and replace 50g of the oats with 100g of mixed seeds: 300g porridge oats, 100g mixed seeds (sunflower, pumpkin, sesame and linseed), 175g each of butter, golden syrup and light muscovado sugar, and a pinch of salt. Bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes and cut when completely cold.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Seeded flapjacks: the classic recipe with seeds in place of some oats

This recipe starts from the tested classic flapjack on our homepage and changes only the dry ingredients. The classic is 350g of porridge oats bound with equal weights of butter, golden syrup and sugar. Here, the oats drop to 300g and 100g of mixed seeds take their place. The binder stays exactly the same, which is why the bars still set properly: it is the syrup mixture, not the oats, that holds a flapjack together.

The swap changes the character of the bar more than the method. Oats soften into the syrup as they bake and give the classic its chew. Seeds stay distinct. Each bite has the soft chew of the classic plus small, firm pops of seed, and the flavour turns nuttier and less sweet-tasting, because seeds bring their own savoury edge and natural oils. The cut face of the bar looks different too — pale oats freckled with green pumpkin seeds, dark linseed and golden sunflower kernels.

One thing the seeds do not do is absorb syrup the way oats do. Oats drink up some of the butter and syrup; seeds mostly just get coated. That is why the seeds replace the oats weight for weight and a little more: 100g of seeds for 50g of oats keeps the mixture coated but not swimming. If your pressed mixture looks wet and pools syrup at the edges of the tin, scatter over another tablespoon of oats before it goes in the oven.

If you are after a bar without nuts rather than one with seeds for their own sake, our nut-free flapjack recipe takes a similar route with a smaller amount of seeds and a lot of label-checking advice. This post is the fuller seeded version, written for people who simply like seeds.

Ingredients

IngredientAmountNotes
Butter175gSalted or unsalted; reduce the pinch of salt if using well-salted butter
Golden syrup175gWeigh it into the pan — a warmed spoon helps it slide off
Light muscovado sugar175gLight brown sugar works in its place
Porridge oats300gStandard porridge oats, not jumbo — they bind better
Sunflower seeds40gShelled kernels, not seeds in their shells
Pumpkin seeds30gThe flat green kind, sold shelled
Sesame seeds15gA small amount — sesame is strong-flavoured
Linseeds15gAlso called flaxseeds; brown or golden
SaltA pinchBrings out the flavour of the seeds

The seed amounts add up to 100g and are a guide, not a law. A ready-mixed bag of baking seeds can go in as it is — just check it is seeds only, without dried fruit or added sugar, and note that many mixes are heavy on sunflower, which is fine. What matters is the total: 100g of seeds against 300g of oats.

The four seeds in the mix: what each one brings

Sunflower kernels are the backbone. They are mild, plump and soft enough to bite through without a fight, so they carry the mix. Pumpkin seeds are firmer and greener, with a savoury taste that stands up to the syrup; they also look good in the cut bar. Buy both shelled — the papery shells on the seeds in the photographs are not something you want in a flapjack.

Sesame seeds are the flavouring of the group. Fifteen grams is enough: they toast as the bars bake and give the whole slice a nutty edge that tastes more deliberate than sweet. A word of care — sesame is one of the allergens that must be labelled on food in the UK, and some people are allergic to it. If you are baking for others, tell them the bars contain sesame.

Linseeds are the smallest and the hardest-working. They slip into the gaps between the oats, and because their coats turn slightly sticky with heat and moisture, they help the bar hold its edges when cut. Whole linseeds pass through the mixture without dominating it; there is no need to grind them for a flapjack.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Heat the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a little paper overhanging two sides so you can lift the slab out later.
  2. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put the butter, golden syrup and muscovado in a large pan on the hob over a low heat. Stir now and then until everything has melted into one glossy liquid. Do not let it boil — boiled syrup sets like toffee and makes hard bars.
  3. Stir in the oats, seeds and salt. Take the pan off the heat. Tip in the oats, all four seeds and the pinch of salt, and stir until every oat and seed is coated and shiny, with no dry patches hiding at the bottom of the pan.
  4. Press the mixture into the tin. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press it down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working it into the corners. Firm pressing is what stops flapjacks crumbling later.
  5. Bake until the edges are golden. Bake for 30–35 minutes. The edges should be golden brown and the middle still look a little soft — it firms up as it cools. The seeds on top will look toasted; that is right.
  6. Cool in the tin. Leave the slab in the tin on a wire rack. Warm flapjack is fragile flapjack, and cutting it now is the surest way to end up with seeded granola instead of bars.
  7. Score while warm, cut when cold. After about 10 minutes, mark 12 bars with a table knife. When the slab is completely cold, lift it out on the paper and cut through along the marks with a large sharp knife.

What the finished bars look like

A good seeded flapjack cuts into a tidy bar with the seeds spread right through it, not sunk to the bottom or sitting on top. The edges are a shade darker than the middle, the top is glossy, and you can see the seeds — green, gold, cream and dark brown — in every slice. It should feel firm in the hand but give when you bite, with the seeds providing small pockets of crunch inside the chew.

Swapping the seeds: how to change the mix

Keep the total at 100g and most swaps look after themselves. This table covers the common changes.

SwapAmountWhat changes
All sunflower100g kernelsThe mildest bar; good for children and seed-sceptics
Sunflower and pumpkin only60g / 40gFirmer bite, no sesame — the simplest two-seed version
Chia seeds for the linseed15gWorks, but chia swells more; the set is slightly softer
Chopped nuts for half the seeds50gRicher and crunchier — but the bars are no longer nut-free
Extra sesameUp to 25gStrong, toasty flavour; reduce the sunflower to keep 100g total

Two swaps to avoid: seeds in their shells (unpleasant to eat and they spoil the set) and roasted, salted snacking seeds, which make the bars taste of a pub snack and throw the salt balance off. Plain, raw baking seeds are what you want.

Halving, doubling and changing the tin

The recipe doubles cleanly: multiply everything by two and use a tin of roughly twice the area — around 23cm by 33cm — so the pressed layer stays the same depth. Same depth means roughly the same baking time; a deeper layer needs longer and stays softer in the middle, and a thinner one crisps up and needs less time. For half the recipe, use a loaf tin of about 20cm by 10cm and expect the bake to be a few minutes shorter. Whichever way you scale, keep the pressed layer around 2cm deep — that depth is what gives the bars their balance of chewy middle and firm edge. The seed ratio does not change with scale: whatever the batch size, seeds stay at 100g for every 300g of oats.

Do the seeds need toasting first?

No. The seeds toast in the oven as the flapjack bakes, and 30 minutes at 160°C is plenty to bring out their flavour. Toasting them beforehand in a dry pan is a small refinement some bakers like — it deepens the taste — but it carries a risk: pre-toasted seeds can scorch around the edges of the tin on the second cooking. If you do toast them, keep them pale. Our view: skip it. The recipe has enough steps already, and the difference is modest.

How to store seeded flapjacks

Keep the cut bars in an airtight tin at room temperature. They keep well for about 5 days — the seeds hold their texture better than the oats in the classic do, and the bars are often at their best on the second day, once the flavours have settled. Do not keep them in the fridge; the cold dries the syrup mixture and dulls the seeds. For longer keeping, the bars freeze in the same way as the classic — our guide to freezing flapjacks covers the wrapping and defrosting. If the tin lives somewhere warm, check the bars after a few days: seeds contain natural oils, and a warm cupboard shortens the life of any bake made with them.
