Pear and Ginger Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

A pear and ginger flapjack folds diced ripe pear through the classic oat mixture and uses ginger twice: ground ginger stirred through the crumb for warmth in every bite, and crystallised ginger chopped small for chewy, fiery pieces. The fresh fruit makes it a longer bake than a plain flapjack — 35 to 40 minutes — and the bars are at their best within about three days.

The short answer: Melt 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado sugar in a pan, stir in 325g porridge oats, 2 teaspoons of ground ginger, 50g crystallised ginger chopped small, a pinch of salt and 2 ripe pears (about 300g) peeled, cored and diced about 1cm, press into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 35–40 minutes. Cool completely in the tin and cut into 12 bars.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Pear and ginger flapjacks: fresh fruit, and ginger used twice

Pear and ginger is a quieter pairing than most on this site. Pears are not sharp like apples or fragrant like oranges; ripe, they are soft, grainy and gently sweet, and on their own in a flapjack they would almost disappear behind the butter and syrup. Ginger is what gives them definition. This recipe uses it in two forms because the two forms do different jobs, and between them they run through the whole bar: ground ginger in the mixture, warming every bite evenly, and crystallised ginger in pieces, so that now and then a bite lands hotter and chewier than the rest.

The base is our classic flapjack recipe with 25g of the oats held back to make room for the fruit. Fresh fruit changes a flapjack in one unavoidable way: it brings water with it. Pears give up juice as they bake, and that juice has to go somewhere — into the oats, and out as steam — which is why this version bakes for 35–40 minutes instead of the usual 30–35, and why the set is softer and more cake-like than the classic. Managed properly, that softness is the pleasure of the recipe. Managed badly, it is a soggy middle, and our guide to why fruit flapjacks turn soggy covers the causes in full.

Two gingers, two jobs: ground for warmth, crystallised for heat

Ground ginger is dried and powdered, and its heat is broad and warm rather than sharp. Stirred through a flapjack mixture it disappears completely as an ingredient you can see, and reappears as a background warmth that makes the whole bar taste spiced. It is the right form for flavouring a crumb, because it distributes perfectly evenly — no bite is without it. Two teaspoons sounds modest; in a bake this sweet, it is enough to be unmistakable without tipping into a gingerbread.

Crystallised ginger is fresh ginger cooked in syrup and rolled in sugar, sold in soft, chewy pieces. Chopped small and folded through, it survives the bake as distinct pieces with a concentrated, fiery-sweet heat that ground ginger cannot imitate. The contrast is the point: the ground ginger sets the temperature of the whole bar, and the crystallised pieces spike it, bite by bite. Chop them small — about the size of a pea — because a big piece of crystallised ginger in a soft bar is a shock rather than a pleasure. The pieces are sticky and cling to the knife; a light dusting of the mixture's own oats on the board stops the worst of it. The table below sets the ginger options side by side.

Ginger formWhat it does in a flapjackVerdict for this recipe
Ground gingerEven warmth through the whole crumb; invisible in the finished barUsed — 2 tsp, the base layer of ginger flavour
Crystallised gingerChewy pieces with concentrated, fiery-sweet heatUsed — 50g chopped small, the spikes of heat
Fresh ginger, gratedBright and sharp, but wet — it adds moisture to a mixture that already has fresh fruit in itNot used here; it suits a drier bake

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper and a large pan for the oat mixture. The pears matter more than any other choice in this recipe: ripe but firm is the brief. A pear that yields slightly at the stalk end but still feels solid is right. Rock-hard pears stay crunchy and raw-tasting in the bake; soft, overripe pears collapse into juice and wet the mixture before it even reaches the oven. Conference pears are the reliable British choice; Williams pears work too, and go softer.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven, line the tin and prepare the fruit and ginger. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides. Peel, core and dice 2 ripe pears into pieces of about 1cm. Chop 50g of crystallised ginger into small pieces and set both aside.
  2. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado in a large pan over a low heat. Stir until everything has melted into one smooth liquid. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil — boiled syrup sets like toffee and makes hard bars.
  3. Add the oats, ground ginger and salt. Take the pan off the heat and tip in 325g porridge oats, 2 teaspoons of ground ginger and a pinch of salt. Stir until every oat is coated and shiny and the ginger is spread evenly through the mixture.
  4. Fold in the pear and crystallised ginger. Add the diced pear and the chopped crystallised ginger and fold them through gently but thoroughly. Fold the pear in last and handle it kindly — the dice should stay as pieces, not be stirred into a purée against the side of the pan.
  5. Press into the tin. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working well into the corners. Press a little more gently than for a plain flapjack: you are compacting the mixture, not crushing the pear dice flat.
  6. Bake for 35–40 minutes. The extra 5 minutes over the classic bake is the fresh fruit's moisture cooking off. The top should be golden brown with the edges a shade darker, and the centre should look set — with fruit in the mixture it will still feel softer than a plain flapjack when pressed, and that is right. Underbaking is the one real failure here, so give it the full time and trust the colour.
  7. Cool completely in the tin. Leave the slab in the tin until it is cold. A fruit flapjack firms up a great deal as it cools, and cutting it warm — when the pear is soft and the set is at its weakest — tears the bars and squeezes the fruit out of the cut faces.
  8. Lift out and cut into 12. Use the paper overhang to lift the cold slab onto a board and cut with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down. You will see the pear pieces in the cut face, pale against the oats — that is exactly how it should look.

The pears: ripe but firm, diced small, and a longer bake

Almost everything that can go wrong with this recipe is a pear decision. Size first: dice of about 1cm cooks through in the time the oats need, softens into the crumb and still reads as a piece of fruit when you bite it. Bigger chunks stay firm in the middle and unbalance the bar — a mouthful of raw-tasting pear in a baked bar is a genuine disappointment. Smaller dice, or grated pear, dissolves into the mixture and simply wets it: all of the moisture problem with none of the fruit.

Ripeness is the second decision, and it cuts both ways. Underripe pears are hard and bland, and 40 minutes in a flapjack does not rescue them. Overripe pears are the sogginess risk — they hold so much juice that the mixture goes into the oven wet, and no amount of baking fully recovers the set. The pear you want gives a little at the stalk end and smells of pear, but still feels firm in the hand. If your pears are a day or two short of that, make our ginger flapjack today and this one when they are ready; the ginger-only version is the same warmth without the fruit to manage.

Storing pear and ginger flapjacks: best within about 3 days

Fresh fruit shortens a flapjack's life, and it is better to be plain about that than to borrow the classic's storage times. These bars are at their best within about 3 days. Keep them in an airtight tin at room temperature on the first day; after that, keep the tin in the fridge, where the fruit stays fresh and the bars stay safe, and let a bar come back towards room temperature before eating it if you can — the ginger warmth reads better warm than fridge-cold. They do not freeze as successfully as a plain flapjack: the pear pieces go soft and weepy on defrosting, so this is a bake to make for the next few days rather than for the freezer. The general rules are in our guide to how to store flapjacks and how long they last.

The usual causes are pears that were too ripe and juicy, dice cut too small so they broke down into the mixture, or an underbaked slab.
