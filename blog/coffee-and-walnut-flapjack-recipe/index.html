Coffee and Walnut Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

A coffee and walnut flapjack takes the pairing from the classic British coffee cake and puts it in an oat bar: instant coffee dissolved and stirred into the melted butter and syrup, and chopped walnuts folded through the oats. The coffee sits in the background, deepening the buttery base rather than shouting, and the walnuts toast as they bake.

The short answer: Dissolve 2 tablespoons of instant coffee granules in 1 tablespoon of hot water. Melt 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado sugar in a pan, stir the coffee in, then stir in 300g porridge oats, 100g chopped walnuts and a pinch of salt, press into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes. Cool in the tin and cut into 12 bars.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Coffee and walnut flapjacks: the cake pairing in an oat bar

Coffee and walnut is one of the set pairings of British baking, up there with lemon and poppy seed or chocolate and orange, and it earns its place the same way: two flavours that are both slightly bitter, both warm, and better together than either is alone. In a cake the coffee usually goes in the sponge and the icing. In a flapjack there is no icing and no sponge, so the arrangement is simpler — the coffee dissolves into the melted base, and the walnuts go through the oats where they toast in the oven and stay distinct in every bite.

The base is our classic flapjack recipe with two adjustments: 50g of the oats are swapped for 100g of chopped walnuts, so the bars are a little less dense and a little richer, and the coffee goes into the melt. The result is a grown-up flapjack — the one people reach for with a cup of coffee rather than in a lunchbox — and it is noticeably less sweet-tasting than the classic, even though the sugar has not changed. Bitterness does that: it balances sweetness without removing it.

Instant coffee, dissolved: why grounds do not work here

The coffee in this recipe is instant coffee granules, dissolved in a tablespoon of hot water before they go anywhere near the pan. That choice is deliberate, and it is worth understanding. Coffee grounds — from a cafetière, a filter or an espresso puck — are ground roasted beans, and they do not dissolve. Stirred into a flapjack mixture they stay as grit: tiny hard particles that get between your teeth and taste raw and harsh, because a 30-minute bake in a syrup mixture does not extract them the way hot water under pressure does. Instant coffee is coffee that has already been brewed and dried, so it melts straight back into liquid and spreads evenly through the whole batch.

Dissolving it first, rather than tipping the granules into the pan, matters for the same reason: granules dropped into melted butter and syrup can sit in damp clumps and leave one bar bitter and the next bland. A tablespoon of hot water turns them into a dark syrup that stirs through the melt in seconds. Two tablespoons of granules is the right amount for a background flavour — present in every bite, clearly coffee, but not a coffee hit. If you want the bars stronger, three tablespoons is the ceiling before the bitterness starts to bully the walnuts. The table below compares the coffee options honestly.

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper, a large pan for the oat mixture, and a small cup or jug for dissolving the coffee. Chop the walnuts on a board with a large knife rather than in a food processor: a processor takes them to dust at the edges before the middle pieces are cut, and dust disappears into the crumb. You want pieces big enough to bite on.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven, line the tin and make the coffee. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides. Put 2 tablespoons of instant coffee granules in a small cup, add 1 tablespoon of hot water and stir until completely dissolved and smooth.
  2. Chop the walnuts. Chop 100g of walnuts roughly on a board — halves and quarters, with some smaller pieces, is about right. Set them aside.
  3. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado in a large pan over a low heat. Stir until everything has melted into one smooth liquid. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil — boiled syrup sets like toffee and makes hard bars.
  4. Stir in the coffee. Pour the dissolved coffee into the melted mixture and stir it through. The liquid will turn a shade darker and smell distinctly of coffee; that is the flavour base of the whole batch, evenly spread before the oats go in.
  5. Add the oats, walnuts and salt. Take the pan off the heat and tip in 300g porridge oats, the chopped walnuts and a pinch of salt. Stir until every oat is coated and shiny and the walnut pieces are spread through the mixture.
  6. Press into the tin. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working well into the corners. Firm pressing is what holds the bars together once cut.
  7. Bake for 30–35 minutes. The top should be golden brown with the edges a shade darker and the walnut pieces at the surface toasted and fragrant. The centre should look set but still feel slightly soft when pressed — it firms up as it cools.
  8. Cool in the tin, then cut into 12. Leave the slab to cool completely in the tin. Use the paper overhang to lift it onto a board and cut with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down so the walnut pieces slice cleanly instead of tearing out.

The walnuts toast in the bake: chop them roughly, not fine

Raw walnuts are soft and slightly bland. Baked walnuts are a different ingredient — firmer, darker, with a toasted depth that stands up to coffee. That change happens in the oven, in the bars, which is why this recipe folds the nuts through the mixture raw and lets the bake do the work rather than asking you to toast them first. It is also why the chop matters. Pieces around half a walnut down to a quarter toast through and stay distinct; a fine chop toasts too, but vanishes into the crumb, and you end up with a coffee flapjack that merely hints at nuts.

One storage point belongs to the walnuts rather than the bars: walnuts go stale and then rancid faster than most nuts, because of their oils. If your walnuts have been open in a cupboard for months, taste one before it goes in. A stale walnut will flavour the whole batch. Fresh bags keep best in the fridge or freezer once opened — for the next batch as much as for this one. Pecans are the nearest swap if walnuts are not what you have, and they make a sweeter, softer bar; our recipes index lists the nut variations on the site together.

Storing coffee and walnut flapjacks: about 5 days in an airtight tin

Keep the cut bars in an airtight tin at room temperature. They keep about 5 days; the coffee flavour holds well and the walnuts are at their best in the first three days, after which they slowly lose their toasted edge. There is no need to refrigerate the bars — the fridge dulls the coffee and firms the butter more than is pleasant. For longer keeping, freeze the bars wrapped individually and sealed for up to 3 months, and defrost in the wrapping at room temperature. Serve them the way the cake is served: with coffee, ideally, where the flavour in the bar and the flavour in the cup meet. The general rules are in our guide to how to store flapjacks and how long they last.

Questions people ask

At 2 tablespoons of instant coffee to this size of batch, the coffee is a clear background flavour: you taste it in every bite, it deepens the butter and syrup, and it balances the sweetness, but the bar does not taste like eating a coffee bean. For a stronger coffee presence, go to 3 tablespoons dissolved the same way. Beyond that the bitterness starts to cover the walnuts rather than partner them.

There is some caffeine in the bars, but a modest amount: 2 tablespoons of instant coffee spread across 12 bars works out at a small fraction of a cup of coffee per bar. Most people would not notice it. If caffeine in the evening bothers you, make the batch with decaffeinated instant coffee — the method is identical and the flavour only a little milder.

A single strong shot of espresso, about 30ml, can replace the dissolved instant — stir it into the melt the same way. Expect a very slightly softer set, because you are adding more liquid, and press the mixture extra firmly. What does not work is ground coffee of any kind: the grounds never dissolve, and they stay gritty and raw-tasting in the finished bar.

You do not need to. The walnuts toast in the bake itself — 30–35 minutes in the oven, surrounded by hot syrup and butter, is plenty for pieces of this size. Toasting them first as well risks taking them too far, because they continue to darken in the oven and can catch and turn bitter at the edges of the tin. Raw into the mixture is the tested route.

Pecans are the closest swap — sweeter and softer than walnuts, and they toast just as well in the bake. Hazelnuts work too, chopped roughly, and give a bar that leans towards chocolate territory even without chocolate in it. Keep the amount at 100g and the chop rough, whichever nut you use, so the pieces stay distinct in the crumb.
