Caramelised Biscuit Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

This flapjack takes the warm, spiced, caramel flavour of caramelised biscuits and builds it into the bar twice over: a smooth caramelised biscuit spread melted into the syrup mixture, and crushed caramelised biscuits folded through the oats, with more pressed over the top. It is sweeter and richer than our classic, deliberately — this is the flapjack for people who eat the biscuits straight from the packet and want that flavour in a bake.

The short answer: Melt 150g butter, 125g golden syrup, 125g light muscovado sugar and 100g smooth caramelised biscuit spread in a pan. Stir in 300g porridge oats and 50g crushed caramelised biscuits, press into a lined 20cm square tin, scatter another 50g of crushed biscuits over the top and press again. Bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes (estimate). Cool completely and cut into 12 bars.

Not yet kitchen-tested: this recipe adapts our tested classic flapjack and has not been cooked by us yet. Michael tests every new recipe himself, and this page will be updated once he has cooked it. Times marked (estimate) are estimates.

Two lots of biscuit: one melted in, one crushed through

A caramelised biscuit flapjack can go wrong in a dull way: a plain flapjack with a biscuit-flavoured hint that disappears behind the oats. This recipe avoids that by using the biscuit flavour in two forms that do different jobs. The smooth spread melts into the butter and syrup, so every oat is coated in the spiced caramel taste and the flavour runs through the whole bar rather than sitting in pockets. The crushed biscuits do the textural work: half folded through the mixture, where they soften slightly into chewy, caramel-rich seams, and half pressed over the top before baking, where they stay crisper and give the bar a rubbly, biscuity crust.

The quantities are adjusted from our classic flapjack recipe, and the adjustments are the recipe, so they are worth explaining. The oats come down from 350g to 300g because the crushed biscuits add dry bulk of their own — keep the full 350g and the mixture would be too dry to bind. The butter, syrup and sugar all come down too, because the spread and the biscuits bring their own fat and sugar with them. Nothing here is a straight addition on top of the classic; it is a rebalance, and that is what keeps the bars flapjacks rather than a sugar slab with oats in it.

The spread: what it is and what it does in the pan

Caramelised biscuit spread is the biscuits themselves, ground with oil and sugar until they turn into a smooth, spoonable paste — the same spiced caramel flavour, in a form that melts. In this recipe it goes into the pan with the butter, syrup and sugar, and it behaves well there: it loosens into the melt within a minute or two of gentle stirring and coats the oats evenly. Use the smooth version, not the crunchy one. Crunchy spread has biscuit pieces suspended in it, and those pieces are sized for spreading on toast, not for a flapjack mixture — they make the melt uneven and the folding-in ragged. The crunch in this recipe comes from the biscuits you crush yourself, at a size you choose.

One practical point: the spread makes the melt thicker than the classic butter-and-syrup liquid, and it can catch on the base of the pan if the heat is too high. Keep the heat low, stir steadily, and take the pan off the heat the moment everything has melted together. As with every flapjack on this site, the syrup mixture must never boil — boiled syrup sets hard, and no amount of biscuit flavour will rescue a bar you cannot bite through.

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper, leaving overhang at two sides, and a large pan. Crush the biscuits in a sealed bag with a rolling pin, or pulse them briefly in a food processor: you want rubble, not dust — pieces roughly the size of a pea, with some smaller crumbs among them. Dust disappears into the mixture and takes the texture with it; big chunks stay hard and fall off the cut bars. Weigh the crushed biscuits after crushing, because a surprising amount of any packet ends up as dust on the worktop.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving overhang at two sides so the slab lifts out cleanly.
  2. Crush the biscuits. Crush 100g of caramelised biscuits to pea-sized rubble as described above, and set half (50g) aside for the top.
  3. Melt the butter, syrup, sugar and spread. Put 150g butter, 125g golden syrup, 125g light muscovado and 100g smooth caramelised biscuit spread in a large pan over a low heat. Stir steadily until everything has melted into one thick, smooth liquid. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil.
  4. Add the oats and the first biscuits. Take the pan off the heat. Tip in 300g porridge oats and 50g of the crushed biscuits, and stir until every oat is coated and the biscuit rubble is spread evenly through the mixture.
  5. Press into the tin and top with biscuit. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working into the corners. Scatter the remaining 50g of crushed biscuits over the surface and press again, more lightly, so the pieces sit in the top of the slab rather than on it.
  6. Bake for 30–35 minutes (estimate). The top should be golden and the biscuit pieces a shade darker at the edges. The centre will feel soft when pressed — it firms up as it cools, and the spread in the mixture makes this slab slower to set than the classic.
  7. Cool completely and cut into 12. Leave the slab to cool fully in the tin; cutting it warm will drag the biscuit topping and crumble the edges. Lift the cold slab out using the paper overhang and cut into 12 bars with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down.

The topping: why half the biscuits go on top

It would be simpler to fold all 100g of crushed biscuit through the mixture, and the bars would still taste right — but they would eat differently. Biscuit folded through a flapjack softens as it bakes, because it sits surrounded by syrup and steam; it comes out as tender, caramel-flavoured seams in the bar. Biscuit pressed onto the surface bakes in the open air of the oven and keeps a dry crunch, closer to the biscuit as it comes from the packet. Putting half in each place gives you both textures in the same bite, and the topped bars look like what they are before you ever taste one.

Press the topping in properly. Loose rubble scattered over a baked slab sheds everywhere — into the tin, onto the plate, down your front. Pressed into the warm, sticky surface of the unbaked mixture, the pieces set into the top as the syrup cools and stay where they were put. If a few pieces do work loose when you cut, press them back onto the cut bars while the slab is still just warm, and they will stick.

How this bake compares with the classic and the caramel flapjack

Three recipes on this site live in the same sweet, caramel corner, and they are not interchangeable. The classic is the plain reference point. Our caramel flapjack recipe builds its flavour from a cooked caramel poured over the base. This one gets its flavour from the biscuits themselves — spice as well as caramel, which neither of the other two has. The table sets out what actually changes.

Sweetness deserves a plain word. Even with the syrup and sugar reduced, this is one of the sweeter bars on the site, because biscuits and spread are both sweetened products and there is no ingredient in the tin pulling the other way. Cut the bars into 12 as written, not 9 — the smaller bar suits the richness — and if you are baking for people who like the flavour but not the full sweetness, the spiced route in our gingerbread flapjack recipe gives warmth with less sugar in the tin.

Cutting and storing: the topping needs a cold slab

More than most flapjacks, this one punishes impatient cutting. The spread in the mixture keeps the slab soft for longer as it cools, and the biscuit topping is only held by the surface of the slab — cut while warm and the knife drags pieces off and the bars tear. Give it the full cooling time in the tin, ideally until the slab feels completely cold and firm, then cut once, firmly, straight down. The bars keep for about 5 days in an airtight tin at room temperature, layered between baking paper; the topping stays crisper on the first two days and softens gently after that, which many people prefer anyway. They freeze well for up to 3 months, wrapped individually — freeze them the day they are baked if the crisp top matters to you, because freezing pauses the softening. The general rules are in our guide to how to store flapjacks.

Questions people ask

The recipe is written for smooth, and smooth is what we would use. Crunchy spread carries biscuit pieces suspended in it, and they make the melted mixture uneven — some spoonfuls mostly spread, some mostly rubble — so the oats coat less evenly. If crunchy is what you have, it will still bake; stir the melt for longer to distribute the pieces, and expect a slightly rougher texture. You will still want the full 100g of biscuits you crush yourself, because that is where the topping comes from.

Gradually, yes — and the recipe expects it. Fresh from the oven, once cooled, the topping is crisp. Over the following days in the tin, the pieces draw moisture from the bar beneath and soften towards chewy. That is the same thing that happens to any biscuit-topped bake, and it is not a fault. If the crisp top is the point for you, eat them within two days, or freeze the bars on baking day and defrost them as needed; freezing holds the texture where it was.

Sweeter, noticeably. The syrup and sugar are each cut by 50g against the classic, but the spread and the crushed biscuits are both sweetened products, and they more than replace what was taken out — plus the biscuit spice makes the sweetness taste rounder and stronger. It is a rich bar, which is why the recipe cuts it into 12 rather than fewer, larger bars. If you want the spiced-caramel flavour at a plainer sweetness, reduce the sugar to 100g; the bars will set a little firmer and the biscuit flavour will still come through.

You can, but you get a different and milder bar: biscuits folded through a classic mixture give you caramel-flavoured seams in a plain flapjack, not a bar that tastes of the biscuits throughout. If you do it, go back to the classic quantities — the full 350g of oats and 175g each of butter, syrup and sugar — because the reductions in this recipe were made to balance the spread. Because the crushed biscuits are doing part of the oats' job. A flapjack mixture is a balance of dry bulk to melted binder: 350g of oats is what 175g each of butter, syrup and sugar can coat and hold. Here the binder is reduced and 100g of biscuit crumb joins the dry side, so the oats come down to 300g to keep the same balance. Keep the full 350g of oats as well as the biscuits and the mixture turns dry and crumbly, and the bars break instead of cutting.

Not with the biscuits and spread named here — caramelised biscuits and their spreads are usually made with butter or milk products, so check the packet of the ones you buy, because recipes vary between brands and countries.
