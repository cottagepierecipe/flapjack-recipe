Chocolate and Coconut Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

A chocolate and coconut flapjack takes the classic oat bar and folds two things through the mixture itself: desiccated coconut, which runs through every bite, and dark chocolate chips, which melt into pockets as the bars bake. Nothing sits on top. The coconut is not a garnish here — it replaces part of the oats, so it changes the texture of the bar as well as the flavour, making the crumb a little drier, a little more textured, and ready for a firmer press in the tin.

The short answer: Melt 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado sugar in a pan, stir in 275g porridge oats, 75g desiccated coconut and a pinch of salt, fold through 100g dark chocolate chips, press firmly into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes. Cool completely in the tin and cut into 12 bars.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Chocolate and coconut flapjacks: the coconut goes through the mixture, not on top

Most chocolate-and-coconut baking treats the coconut as a coating — something rolled around the outside or scattered over a topping. These bars do it the other way round. The desiccated coconut goes into the pan with the oats and is stirred through the melted butter, syrup and sugar, so it is distributed through the whole slab. Every bar has coconut in every bite, and the texture changes accordingly: desiccated coconut is dry and absorbent, and it drinks up some of the syrup as the mixture sits, so the crumb sets a touch firmer and less sticky than the plain classic. If you have made our coconut flapjack recipe, this is its chocolate counterpart — the same coconut-through-the-mixture idea, with dark chocolate folded in to stand up to it.

The base quantities stay close to our classic flapjack recipe: the full 175g each of butter, golden syrup and light muscovado sugar, because the coconut needs the same amount of syrup to bind it as the oats it replaces. What changes is the dry side of the pan — 275g of porridge oats plus 75g of desiccated coconut instead of 350g of oats. That ratio matters. More coconut than this and the bars turn dry and crumbly, because coconut does not soften and bind the way oats do in the bake. At 75g it flavours the whole batch and firms the crumb without taking over.

Dark chocolate chips folded through, or a melted topping instead

The chocolate in this recipe goes through the mixture as chips, and that is a deliberate choice. Chips stirred into the warm oat mixture soften and slump a little in the oven but keep their identity, so a cut bar shows dark pockets of chocolate running through a pale, coconut-flecked crumb. The chocolate is part of the bar rather than a layer on it, which suits a bar you might pack in a lunchbox or wrap for a bake sale — there is no topping to crack, stick to the wrapping or slide off in a warm bag.

There is a technique point worth knowing: let the oat mixture stand off the heat for a minute or two before the chips go in. Tipped into a pan that has just come off the hob, chips can melt completely and streak the whole mixture brown. A short rest keeps them as chips. Some melting at the edges is fine and even good — it marbles the crumb — but distinct pockets are the aim. If you prefer your chocolate as a proper layer instead, make the bars without chips and use the topping route from our chocolate flapjack recipe: cool the slab completely, spread over 150g of melted dark chocolate, and leave it to set before cutting. The two versions eat quite differently — the topped one richer and more of a dessert, this one the sturdier, more portable bar.

Fine sea saltA pinchLifts both the coconut and the chocolate

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides, and a large pan — the mixture is bulky once the oats and coconut go in, and a big pan makes the stirring and the pressing mixture easier to judge.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides so the cold slab lifts out cleanly.
  2. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado in a large pan over a low heat. Stir until everything has melted into one smooth liquid. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil — boiled syrup sets hard and makes hard bars.
  3. Add the oats, coconut and salt. Take the pan off the heat and tip in 275g porridge oats, 75g desiccated coconut and a pinch of salt. Stir until every oat is coated and shiny and the coconut is spread evenly through the mixture, with no dry white patches left.
  4. Rest the mixture, then fold in the chips. Leave the pan off the heat for a minute or two — just long enough that the mixture stops steaming fiercely — then tip in 100g dark chocolate chips and fold them through with a few turns of the spoon. You want them distributed, not melted away.
  5. Press into the tin, firmly. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down hard and evenly with the back of a spoon, working well into the corners. Coconut makes this mixture drier and looser than the plain classic, so the press matters more here, not less.
  6. Bake for 30–35 minutes. The top should be golden with the edges a shade darker, and the centre should look set but still feel slightly soft when pressed — it firms up as it cools.
  7. Cool completely in the tin. Leave the slab in the tin until it is fully cold. Warm flapjack crumbles when it is cut, and coconut flapjack crumbles more than most.
  8. Lift out and cut into 12. Use the paper overhang to lift the cold slab onto a board and cut it into 12 bars with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down through the chocolate pockets rather than sawing.

The press matters more with coconut: dry, crumbly and soft bars fixed

Coconut changes how this mixture behaves in two ways, and both of them point at the same two steps: the press and the cooling. Desiccated coconut absorbs liquid as it sits, so the mixture stiffens and dries slightly between the pan and the tin — work reasonably quickly once the oats are in, and do not leave the pan sitting around while you do something else. And because coconut grains do not knit together the way softened oats do, a loosely pressed slab has nothing to hold it once cut. Press harder than you would for the classic, especially into the corners, and give the slab its full cooling time. The table below covers the usual failures.

What happenedLikely causeThe fix next time
Bars crumbled when cutPressed too loosely, or cut while still warmPress firmly into the corners and cut only when the slab is completely cold
Bars came out dryToo much coconut, or baked too longKeep the coconut at 75g against 275g oats, and take the slab out while the centre still feels slightly soft

Serving chocolate and coconut flapjacks

These are sturdy, portable bars — the kind that travel in a lunchbox or a coat pocket without shedding a topping. The coconut makes them keep their shape at room temperature better than the plain classic, and the flavour pairing holds up well alongside a cup of tea or coffee, where the dark chocolate reads properly. For a pudding, a warm bar with a spoonful of yoghurt or a scoop of vanilla ice cream works well; warm them for a few minutes in a low oven rather than the microwave, which melts the chocolate pockets unevenly. If you want the same flavours pushed further towards dessert, our double chocolate flapjacks take the chocolate count higher still.

Storing chocolate and coconut flapjacks: about 5 days in an airtight tin

Keep the cut bars in an airtight tin at room temperature. They keep about 5 days; the coconut keeps the crumb textured and the chocolate pockets stay distinct. The bars also freeze well for up to 3 months, wrapped individually and sealed — freeze them on the day they are baked if you can, and defrost in the wrapping at room temperature. The general rules are in our guide to how to store flapjacks and how long they last.
