Chocolate Mini Egg Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

This is the Easter version of our classic flapjack: chocolate chips folded through the oat mixture, a layer of melted milk chocolate spread over the warm slab, and pastel chocolate mini eggs pressed into the top before it sets. Everything important in this recipe happens after the bake — the eggs never go into the oven, and this post explains why, along with how to get the topping to stay put and the eggs to stay in it.

The short answer: Make the classic flapjack — 175g butter, 175g golden syrup, 175g light muscovado sugar and 350g porridge oats — with 100g chocolate chips folded in, press into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes (estimate). While the slab is still warm, spread 150g melted milk chocolate over the top and press in about 150g chocolate mini eggs. Leave to set completely before cutting into 12 bars.

An Easter flapjack, built on the classic

Nothing about the base of this recipe is new, and that is deliberate. The slab underneath is our classic flapjack recipe — the tested one — with a handful of chocolate chips folded through the mixture so the chocolate runs through the bar as well as over it. What makes it an Easter bake is the finish: a proper chocolate top, spread over the slab while it is still warm from the oven, and a covering of small sugar-shelled chocolate eggs pressed into that top while the chocolate is still wet. The eggs keep their shells, their colours and their crunch, the top sets into a single layer with the eggs held in it, and the whole thing cuts into bars that look like spring. Our chocolate flapjack recipe covers the cocoa, chips and topping routes through the classic in more depth; this post is about the one variation where the decoration is the point.

It is worth being clear about what the eggs are doing. They are not an ingredient in the baking sense — they contribute no binding, no structure, nothing the recipe depends on. They are crunch, colour and chocolate, in that order, and they are there because at Easter a flapjack can carry a bit of theatre. The bar underneath has to stand up on its own, and it does: chips in the mixture, chocolate on top, and a tested oat base below both.

The eggs themselves: small, speckled and added last

Chocolate mini eggs are small solid chocolate eggs in thin, crisp sugar shells, usually pastel-coloured and often speckled. Any small sugar-shelled chocolate eggs will do for this recipe — you need about 150g of them, which is a generous single layer over a 20cm slab with a few left over for the cook. Buy them whole and keep them whole: halved eggs show their chocolate faces, which looks fine, but the shell is what gives the crunch and the colour, and a whole egg pressed shell-down into the chocolate top holds better than a half. Sizes vary a little between makers, so if yours are at the larger end, space them slightly wider apart and accept a slightly less crowded top rather than forcing them in and cracking the shells against each other.

The shells deserve a sentence of their own, because they explain the whole method. A sugar shell is there to protect the chocolate and to snap when you bite it. Heat undoes both jobs: in the oven the shell cracks and softens, the chocolate inside melts and scorches at the edges, and the colours bleed into the oats. Every part of what makes a mini egg a mini egg is undone by baking it. Pressed into a warm chocolate topping on a finished slab, the same egg stays exactly what it was in the packet. There is no version of this recipe where the eggs go in before the bake and come out well, and the method below is built around that fact.

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper, leaving overhang at two sides so the finished, decorated slab can be lifted out whole — with a chocolate top covered in eggs, this is not a slab to turn out or lever from the tin. Lifting it out on its paper and cutting it on a board is how the top survives.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving overhang at two sides.
  2. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado in a large pan over a low heat and stir until melted into one smooth liquid. Never let it boil — boiled syrup sets like toffee and makes hard bars.
  3. Add the oats, salt and chips. Take the pan off the heat, tip in 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt, and stir until every oat is coated. Fold in 100g chocolate chips last. The warm mixture will soften the chips slightly, which is fine — they hold their shape well enough and marble through the bars.
  4. Press into the tin and bake. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin, press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working into the corners, and bake for 30–35 minutes (estimate), until golden with the edges a shade darker.
  5. Melt the topping chocolate. While the slab bakes, break 150g milk chocolate into a heatproof bowl set over a pan of barely simmering water and let it melt, stirring now and then. Keep the water off the boil and the bowl dry — water in melted chocolate makes it seize and turn grainy.
  6. Spread the chocolate over the warm slab. As soon as the slab comes out of the oven, pour the melted chocolate over it and spread it to the edges with the back of a spoon or a palette knife. The warmth of the slab keeps the chocolate fluid while you work and helps it bond to the oats beneath as it sets.
  7. Press in the eggs straight away. While the topping is still wet, press about 150g chocolate mini eggs gently into it in rows, spacing them so the knife can pass between them when you cut. Press just enough for the chocolate to grip them — no harder, or the shells crack.
  8. Leave to set completely, then cut. Leave the decorated slab at room temperature until the topping is fully set — at least 2 hours, and longer on a warm day. Lift it out on its paper and cut into 12 bars with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down and aiming the cuts between the eggs.

Decorating the top: getting the topping to stay on

A chocolate topping lifts off a flapjack for a short list of reasons, and this recipe is arranged to dodge all of them. Spreading the chocolate onto a warm slab is the main one: the chocolate stays liquid a little longer, settles into the top of the oats rather than sitting on them as a separate sheet, and sets in contact with the bar. A topping spread onto a cold slab sets as its own layer and is the one that shears off in sheets when you cut. The eggs go in while the chocolate is still wet for the same reason — each egg is set into the topping, not balanced on it. Our post on why chocolate topping lifts off flapjacks goes through the causes in full, and the chocolate-topped flapjacks guide compares topping choices across the board. The practical defence is to work in rows from one side, eggs ready in a bowl beside the tin, and you will finish well before it sets.

Milk, dark or white chocolate on top?

The recipe is written with milk chocolate because it is the traditional partner for the eggs and the one children reach for first, but the topping is a genuine choice and the three behave differently. This table sets out what changes.

ToppingSetAgainst the sweet eggs and baseCutting
Milk chocolateFirm but not hardThe sweetest balanced option — eggs, topping and base all in the same registerCuts cleanly once fully set
Dark chocolate (about 70%)The firmest set of the threeBitter against the sweet eggs — the grown-up version, and many prefer itCan crack if cut fridge-cold; cut at room temperature
White chocolateThe softest set, and slowest in a warm kitchenVery sweet with the eggs on an already sweet base — a small bar goes a long wayStays slightly soft; a warm knife drags it, so cut cool and wipe the blade

Whichever you choose, the amount stays 150g and the method does not change. Dark chocolate is the one swap that changes the character of the bar rather than just its sweetness, and if the flapjacks are for adults it is worth trying first.

Spare eggs: ideas for the rest of the packet

Mini eggs are sold in packets sized by optimism rather than by recipe, so there will be some left. They keep well in their shells in a cool cupboard, and they are useful decorators far beyond this flapjack: pressed into the icing on cupcakes, scattered over a trifle, or chopped — shells and all — through vanilla ice cream. The nest idea in the photograph below is the classic Easter table treatment and takes minutes: green-tinted icing piped or forked into a rough nest on a cupcake, three eggs in the middle. Children can do the whole job themselves, which is rather the point of it.

Storing the bars and the leftover chocolate

The finished bars keep about 5 days in an airtight tin at room temperature, with baking paper between the layers so the eggs do not mark the bars beneath them. Keep the tin out of direct sun and away from the oven — the topping softens in warmth and the eggs will pick up a bloom if the tin heats and cools repeatedly. The bars freeze well for up to 3 months, wrapped individually and sealed; defrost them at room temperature in their wrapping, and expect the shells to look a little duller afterwards, though the crunch survives. Leftover Easter chocolate of any kind, shelled or foil-wrapped, keeps best in a cool, dry cupboard rather than the fridge, where it can pick up moisture and other flavours. The general rules are in our guide to how to store flapjacks and how long they last.
