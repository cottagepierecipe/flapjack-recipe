Caramel Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

A caramel flapjack is two things stacked together: a plain, buttery oat base, baked a little less sweet than the classic, and a thick layer of condensed milk caramel poured over the top and left to set until it slices like fudge. The base does the unglamorous work of holding everything together. The caramel is the part people remember.

The short answer: Bake the base from 175g butter, 150g golden syrup, 150g light muscovado sugar and 350g porridge oats in a lined 20cm square tin at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes. While it bakes, simmer a 397g tin of sweetened condensed milk with 100g butter, 100g light brown sugar and 1 tablespoon of golden syrup until thick and amber. Pour the caramel over the base, leave it to set, and cut into 12 bars.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Caramel flapjacks: a plain oat base under a set caramel layer

Most people meet this combination as millionaire's shortbread — biscuit at the bottom, caramel in the middle, chocolate on top. A caramel flapjack swaps the shortbread for a flapjack base and keeps the rest of the idea. The swap suits the caramel better than you might expect: oats have a toasted, almost nutty flavour that plain biscuit lacks, and a flapjack base is chewy where shortbread is crumbly, so the whole slice eats as one soft, dense bar rather than three layers arguing with each other.

The base is our classic flapjack recipe — the version we have kitchen-tested — with one deliberate change, explained in the next section: a little less syrup and a little less sugar, because the caramel layer brings plenty of both. The tin, the temperature and the bake are the classic's, unchanged. What is new here is everything that happens after the base comes out of the oven: a pan of simple caramel, made from a tin of condensed milk, poured over while both are warm and left alone until it sets firm enough to cut cleanly.

The base is baked with 25g less syrup and 25g less sugar

The classic base is 175g each of butter, golden syrup and light muscovado sugar to 350g of oats. For this version the butter and oats stay exactly as they are, but the syrup drops to 150g and the sugar to 150g. That is not dieting — the caramel layer adds a whole tin of condensed milk plus more butter and sugar on top — it is balance. A full-sweetness base under a full layer of caramel is the kind of sweet that coats your mouth and hides every other flavour. Taking a little sweetness out of the base lets the oats taste of oats and gives the caramel something plain to sit against.

The smaller amount of syrup has one practical consequence worth knowing before you start. Syrup is the glue in a flapjack, so a base made with slightly less of it needs to be pressed into the tin a little more firmly than usual — really work it into the corners and level it with the back of a spoon. On its own, this base is also a touch softer than the classic when it first cools. That is expected, and it is not a problem: once the caramel has set on top of it, the two layers firm up together and the finished bars slice and hold like any other flapjack.

The caramel: one tin of condensed milk, simmered gently and never boiled hard

The caramel here is the simplest reliable kind: sweetened condensed milk, butter, light brown sugar and a spoonful of golden syrup, cooked together in a pan. As the mixture heats, the sugars in the milk and the brown sugar deepen in flavour and colour, and the whole thing thickens from a pale, pourable liquid into something that coats a spoon heavily and holds a trail when you drag the spoon across the bottom of the pan. That change — not a timer — is what tells you it is ready, though the time it takes is given in the method as a guide.

Two rules govern the pan. First, keep the heat low and stir constantly: condensed milk is rich in milk solids and sugar, and it will catch and scorch on the base of the pan in the time it takes to answer the door, leaving brown flecks and a burnt taste that no amount of stirring afterwards removes. Second, never let it boil hard. A gentle simmer is plenty. Boiling drives the sugar towards toffee, and toffee is not a layer — it is a sheet of glass. Boiled syrup is also exactly what makes plain flapjacks set hard, as our guide to why flapjacks turn out hard explains, and the same chemistry applies in this pan. The spoonful of golden syrup in the mixture is there to help the caramel stay smooth rather than grainy, which makes it worth including even though the base already contains syrup.

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper, a large pan for the oat mixture and a second, heavy-based pan for the caramel — a thin pan has hot spots, and hot spots are how condensed milk scorches. A heatproof spatula or wooden spoon you are happy to keep moving for ten minutes is the other essential.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides for lifting the finished slab out later.
  2. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar for the base. Put 175g butter, 150g golden syrup and 150g light muscovado in a large pan over a low heat. Stir until everything has melted into one smooth liquid. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil — boiled syrup sets like toffee and makes hard bars.
  3. Add the oats and salt. Take the pan off the heat and tip in 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt. Stir until every oat is coated and shiny and there are no dry patches hiding at the bottom of the pan.
  4. Press into the tin. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working well into the corners. This base has slightly less syrup than the classic, so firm pressing matters even more than usual — it is what holds the bars together under the caramel.
  5. Bake for 30–35 minutes. The top should be golden brown with the edges a shade darker, and the centre should look set but still feel slightly soft when pressed — it firms up as it cools. Take the tin out and leave the base in it; the caramel goes on in the tin.
  6. Make the caramel while the base bakes. Put the 397g tin of sweetened condensed milk, 100g butter, 100g light brown sugar and 1 tablespoon of golden syrup into a heavy-based pan over a low heat. Stir until the butter has melted and the sugar has completely dissolved — rub a little between spoon and pan side; it should feel smooth, not gritty.
  7. Simmer the caramel until thick and amber. Keep the pan at the gentlest possible simmer, stirring constantly and scraping the bottom and corners, for about 10 to 15 minutes. The caramel will turn a deep amber and thicken until it coats the spoon heavily and a trail drawn across the pan bottom holds for a couple of seconds. Never let it boil hard, and do not leave it unstirred.
  8. Pour the caramel over the warm base. Pour it evenly over the baked base, still in its tin, then tilt the tin gently or spread with the back of a spoon so the caramel reaches the corners in an even layer. Warm base and warm caramel settle into each other and bond as they cool.
  9. Leave it to cool and set. Leave the tin at room temperature until the caramel is firm to a fingertip and no longer tacky — about 2 hours. In a hurry, about 45 minutes in the fridge does it, though the base stays a little softer for being chilled.
  10. Add the optional chocolate, or leave the caramel plain. If you are using it, melt 100g of chocolate in a bowl over a pan of barely simmering water, pour it over the set caramel and spread it thin. Leave to set at room temperature, about 30 minutes. A thick layer of chocolate cracks when cut; a thin one shatters neatly.
  11. Lift out and cut into 12. Use the paper overhang to lift the cold slab onto a board. Cut with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down through the layers, and wipe the blade clean between cuts — caramel drags on a dirty blade and smears the layers.

Caramel problems: runny, grainy, or set like toffee

Almost every caramel failure in this recipe comes back to one of two things: the heat was too fierce, or the pan came off the heat at the wrong moment. The table below is the short version. The longer version is simpler than it looks — stay at a low heat, keep stirring, and judge the caramel by how it behaves on the spoon rather than by the clock, because stoves vary.

What happenedLikely causeThe fix next time
Caramel still runny after coolingTaken off the heat too soonSimmer until it thickly coats the spoon and a trail across the pan bottom holds for a few seconds
Caramel feels grainy on the tongueSugar not fully dissolved before the simmer, or heat too high at the startDissolve the sugar completely over a low heat first; keep the spoonful of golden syrup in the mixture
Caramel set rock-hardBoiled too hard, or cooked well past the trail stageHold it at a gentle simmer, never a rolling boil, and stop when it turns amber and thick
Brown flecks and a burnt tasteThe mixture caught on the pan baseHeavy-based pan, low heat, constant stirring that reaches the corners
Bars crumble under the caramelBase pressed too loosely, or cut before everything setPress the base in firmly and cut only when the slab is fully cold and set

The optional chocolate top: 100g of chocolate, spread thin

The chocolate top is genuinely optional, and there are good reasons on both sides. With it, the bars are the full millionaire's-style arrangement and the slight bitterness of dark chocolate is a real help against two sweet layers. Without it, the caramel is the flavour and the bars are simpler and a fraction less sweet. If you do add it, keep it thin: 100g spread over a 20cm tin is a coating, not a layer, and that is deliberate. Thick chocolate sets hard, cracks away from the caramel when you cut, and takes over the bite. Our chocolate flapjack recipe covers the other approaches — chips stirred through and cocoa in the base — if chocolate all the way through is more your thing than chocolate on top.

Storing caramel flapjacks: about 5 days in an airtight tin

Keep the cut bars in an airtight tin at room temperature with baking paper between the layers — the caramel tops stick to each other, and to the lid, if they touch. They keep about 5 days; the caramel stays sliceable and the base stays chewy. In a warm kitchen the caramel softens, so if the tin lives somewhere hot, the fridge is the safer home — just let the bars come back to room temperature before eating, because both the base and the caramel eat hard straight from the fridge. They also freeze well for up to 3 months, wrapped individually and sealed; defrost in the wrapping at room temperature. The general rules are in our guide to how to store flapjacks and how long they last.

Questions people ask

Ready-made caramel from a tin — the thick, spoonable kind sold for filling cakes and pies — can be warmed very slightly, spread over the baked base and left to set, and it skips the simmering step entirely. Expect a softer set than the cooked version: it slices, but the layer stays a little more yielding, especially at room temperature on a warm day. The flavour is also a touch sweeter and less deep, because it has not cooked in the pan. It is a fair shortcut, not a failure.

Usually, yes. Runny caramel almost always means it came off the heat before it had thickened enough — it should coat a spoon heavily and hold a trail before it goes over the base. To rescue a made batch, keep the bars in the fridge, where the layer firms up considerably, and eat them within a few days. You cannot put the caramel back in the pan once it is on the base, so the rescue is cold and prompt eating rather than re-cooking — and next time, give the pan the extra few minutes.

Graininess is undissolved sugar that has crystallised as the mixture cooked. It happens when the heat is high at the start, so the mixture begins to simmer before the sugar has fully melted, or when the pan is left unstirred so crystals form on the sides and get stirred back in. The safeguards are all at the beginning: low heat, stir until you cannot feel any grit with the spoon, and keep the spoonful of golden syrup in the mixture, which helps the caramel stay smooth.

You can make a caramel that way, but it will not behave like this one. A cream caramel is closer to a sauce: rich and pourable when warm, and soft even when cold. Condensed milk is what gives this layer its firm, fudge-like set — the milk solids and the long, gentle cooking produce a caramel you can slice with a knife and pick up in a bar. If you swap to cream, expect to serve the result with a fork rather than in a lunchbox.

It is a matter of balance rather than rules, and you can make the base at the classic's full 175g of syrup and 175g of sugar if you prefer. What changes is the eating: the bars become sweet all the way through, with no plainer layer for the caramel to sit against, and most people find one bar more than enough. The reduced base is also slightly softer on its own, so if you do use the full classic base, you can press it a little less hard. Either way, the caramel method is unchanged.

They keep about 5 days in an airtight tin at room temperature, and the caramel layer arguably improves over the first day as it settles and firms. For anything longer, freeze them: wrapped individually and sealed in a bag or box, they keep for up to 3 months and defrost at room temperature in their wrapping in a couple of hours. If you are making them for an event, baking two days ahead and keeping the tin somewhere cool is the sweet spot between freshness and convenience.
