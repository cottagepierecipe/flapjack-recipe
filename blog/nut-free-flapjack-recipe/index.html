Nut-Free Flapjack Recipe with Ingredient-Checking Tips

By Michael

A nut-free flapjack is the classic oat bar with seeds folded through for crunch — no nuts and no nut butters anywhere in the recipe. The baking is the easy part. The part that needs care is the shopping: labels, warnings and a clean tin, all covered below before the method.

The short answer: Melt 175g butter with 175g golden syrup and 175g light brown sugar, stir in 350g porridge oats, 60g mixed sunflower and pumpkin seeds and a pinch of salt, press into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes. Before you bake, check the label of every single ingredient — oats and seeds in particular often carry "may contain nuts" warnings — and start with a clean tin and fresh baking paper. If you are baking for someone with a nut allergy, check your ingredient choices with them first; no recipe can make that decision for you.

Not yet kitchen-tested: this recipe adapts our tested classic flapjack and has not been cooked by us yet. Michael tests every new recipe himself, and this page will be updated once he has cooked it. Times marked (estimate) are estimates.

The recipe: classic flapjack with seeds instead of nuts

The base is our classic flapjack exactly as tested — the same butter, syrup, sugar and oats, the same tin, the same temperature. The only change is the addition of 60g of mixed seeds, sunflower and pumpkin, folded through the oats before the mixture is pressed into the tin. Seeds give the bars the crunch and richness that nuts would normally provide, without bringing any nut ingredient into the kitchen.

There are no nut butters in this recipe either. That matters more than it might look: several popular flapjack variations lean on peanut butter or almond butter as a binder, and those are firmly off the table here. If you have landed on this page from our peanut butter flapjack recipe, treat that post as the one to avoid for a nut-free bake — this page is its opposite number. The seeds need no preparation: no toasting, no soaking, no chopping. They go in dry, straight from the packet, once you have checked the packet (see the checking section below — seeds are one of the ingredients most likely to carry a warning).

Makes 12 bars. Equipment: a 20cm square tin, baking paper, a large saucepan and a spoon for pressing. If the tin last held a nut bake and has not been washed since, wash it first — and line it with fresh paper regardless, as the next section explains.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and prepare the tin. Heat the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Wash the tin if it has held nuts since its last wash, dry it, and line it with fresh baking paper, leaving paper overhanging two sides for lifting the slab out later.

  2. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Warm the butter, golden syrup and light brown sugar in a large saucepan over a low heat, stirring, until melted and smooth. Do not let the mixture boil, or the bars can set hard.

  3. Stir in the oats, seeds and salt. Take the pan off the heat. Add the porridge oats, the mixed seeds and the pinch of salt. Stir until everything is coated and glossy, checking the bottom of the pan for dry oats and making sure the seeds are spread through the mixture, not gathered in one place.

  4. Press into the tin. Tip the mixture into the lined tin, spread it into the corners and press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon. A firmly pressed slab cuts into bars that hold together; a loosely pressed one crumbles.

  5. Bake until golden at the edges. Bake for 30–35 minutes until the edges are golden and the centre looks set. Seeds on the surface will deepen in colour — that is fine, but take the tin out if they start to look dark and scorched rather than toasted.

  6. Cool in the tin, then cut. Leave the slab to cool in the tin on a wire rack until firm, then lift it out with the paper and cut into 12 bars with a large knife, pressing straight down. Store following the same routine as our guide to how to store flapjacks: an airtight tin, about 5 days.

Checking ingredients: the part of this recipe that matters most

A recipe can only be nut-free if the ingredients are, and the ingredient list on a packet is not the whole story. Manufacturers also print allergy advice — usually a box or a line near the ingredients — which may say an ingredient was made in a factory that handles nuts, or that the product "may contain nuts". Those warnings are voluntary and worded differently from brand to brand, which is exactly why every packet needs reading, every time. Formulations and factories change, so a packet that was fine last month deserves a fresh look this month.

The oats are the ingredient to check hardest. Plain porridge oats are just oats, but oats are harvested, transported and milled in systems that also handle other grains and, in many facilities, nuts and peanuts. A large share of ordinary oat brands carry a "may contain" warning for that reason. The same applies to the seed mix: seeds are commonly packed in facilities that also pack nuts, and a bag of sunflower seeds can carry a nut warning even though the seeds themselves are nut-free ingredients. Golden syrup, sugar and butter are less commonly flagged, but "less commonly" is not "never" — read them too. It takes two minutes at the shelf and it is the whole recipe, in a sense.

A clean tin and a fresh liner: keeping nuts out of the bake

Ingredients are half of it; the other half is what is already in your kitchen. If the last thing baked in your tin was a nut-topped traybake, residue can survive in the corners and in scratched baking paper. The routine is simple: wash the tin and any utensils that will touch the mixture in hot soapy water and dry them well, use a fresh sheet of baking paper from the roll rather than a saved piece, and use a clean spoon for pressing. If your kitchen stores open bags of nuts near the baking ingredients, close everything else up before you start, and wipe the worktop down.

One careful point about peanuts: a peanut is a legume, not a tree nut, but in food labelling and everyday allergy practice peanuts are treated alongside nuts, and many people allergic to one are told to avoid the other. This recipe contains no peanuts in any case. The practical rule stands above the botany: if you are baking for a specific person with a nut or peanut allergy, ask them — or whoever manages their allergy — exactly what they avoid and which brands they trust, and follow their guidance over anything written here. People manage allergies differently, and their own rules are the ones that count. This page can tell you how to read a label; it cannot promise that any bake is safe for any individual, and it does not try to.

Baking for schools and nurseries: their rules come first

Many schools and nurseries run a nut-free policy for packed lunches and bake sales, and the details differ from setting to setting: one school may ban nuts as an ingredient, another may also exclude anything carrying a "may contain nuts" warning, and policies change. A home baker cannot see those rules from the kitchen. So before baking these bars for a school or nursery, check the setting's own policy and keep to it exactly — including the label checks above, keeping the ingredient packets until the bake is handed over, and labelling what you send in. If a setting's policy is stricter than this recipe, the policy wins. Where there is any doubt, ask the setting directly rather than guessing: a two-line email is cheaper than a bake that has to be turned away at the door.

One last habit worth adopting: keep the ingredient packets until the bars have been eaten. If anyone has a reaction, or if a school queries what went into a bake sale batch, the actual labels — not your memory of them — are the evidence. Photographing the allergy advice panels while you bake takes seconds and settles questions that a recipe page never can. And if the person you are baking for has an allergy action plan or carries medication, that is theirs to manage; the baker's share is honest ingredients, honest labels and no surprises.
