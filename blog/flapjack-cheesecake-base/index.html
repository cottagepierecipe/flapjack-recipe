Flapjack Cheesecake Base: A Chewy Base That Holds a Slice

By Michael

A flapjack mixture pressed thin into a springform tin and baked on its own for a few minutes makes a cheesecake base with real chew — oats, butter, syrup and muscovado doing the job that crushed biscuits usually do, but holding together as one piece when you cut a slice. This post gives the base and a plain baked vanilla cheesecake to sit on it, and it is honest about the differences: this base is sweeter and more substantial than biscuit crumb, and it asks a little more of your knife.

The short answer: Make a half-size flapjack mixture — 90g butter, 90g golden syrup, 90g light muscovado sugar and 175g porridge oats — press it into a lined 20cm springform tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 15 minutes until firm. Cool, then pour over a filling of 600g full-fat cream cheese, 150g caster sugar, 2 eggs plus 1 yolk, 150ml double cream and 1 tsp vanilla extract, and bake at the same temperature for 45–50 minutes until just set with a slight wobble. Cool slowly, chill for at least 4 hours, and keep it in the fridge for up to 3 days. Serves 8.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Why put a flapjack under a cheesecake?

The standard cheesecake base is crushed biscuits stirred into melted butter — quick, reliable and, eaten blind, mostly a sweet crunch that shatters or slumps depending on how it was made. A flapjack base is a different thing. It is our classic flapjack recipe mixture at half quantity, pressed thinner than you would ever press a tray of bars and given a short bake of its own before the filling goes anywhere near it. As that mixture cools, the syrup and sugar set around the oats exactly as they do in a tray of flapjack, so the base behaves like one thin, chewy oat bar shaped to the tin. Under a slice it holds as a single layer instead of crumbling away from the filling, and it tastes of something — caramel from the muscovado, butter, toasted oats — rather than just sweetness.

There are trade-offs, and they are worth stating before you start. The base is sweeter than a biscuit base, so the filling above it is kept plain and only moderately sweet to balance it. It is chewier, too, which most people who have eaten flapjack will count as the point, but it does mean the fork meets a little resistance at the bottom of every bite. And because the base is baked twice — once on its own, once under the filling — it needs the first bake judged properly: too pale and it will be soft rather than chewy; too dark and the edges turn hard and bitter under an hour of further cooking. Fifteen minutes to firm and just golden is the target, and the cues in the method matter more than the clock.

One more honest point: there is no cream cheese photograph in this post. Every unbranded-looking shot of a cream cheese carton turns out to carry a readable brand, so the filling is shown by the finished slices further down instead of by a branded tub. The base, the bake and the slicing all get their photographs.

The tin, the lining and why a springform matters

A cheesecake cannot be tipped out of a tin the way a flapjack slab can, which is why this recipe is built around a 20cm springform tin — the ring unclips and lifts away, leaving the cheesecake standing on its base. Line the base disc with a circle of baking paper, and lightly butter the inside of the ring. The flapjack mixture is sticky with syrup and will grip bare metal, so do not skip the paper even if your tin claims to be non-stick. A deep loose-bottomed cake tin can stand in if it is all you have, lined the same way and with a wide strip of paper under the base to help you lift, but the springform is the right tool and the one the timings assume. Our guide to how to line a flapjack tin covers the paper-handling habits that apply here too — paper cut to fit, corners persuaded rather than forced.

Press the mixture in with the back of a spoon, working it flat and even and slightly into the edge where base meets ring. You are aiming for a layer roughly a centimetre thick, even across the middle — a thick patch in the centre will still be soft when the edges are done, and a thin patch will darken and harden. This pressing is the same job as pressing a tray of flapjack, only thinner, and it repays the same firmness.

Ingredients

For the flapjack base:

IngredientAmountNotes
Butter90gHalf the classic's amount — this is a half-size mixture for a thin base
Golden syrup90gThe binder that sets the base firm as it cools
Light muscovado sugar90gLight brown sugar is the nearest swap; muscovado gives the caramel note
Porridge oats175gHalf the classic's 350g. Porridge oats, not jumbo — they press thinner and hold better

For the filling:

IngredientAmountNotes
Full-fat cream cheese600gFull-fat is not optional — low-fat versions are too wet and set poorly
Caster sugar150gKept moderate because the base is sweet
Eggs2, plus 1 extra yolkAt room temperature if you can — they mix in without overbeating
Double cream150mlSoftens the set and rounds the filling out
Vanilla extract1 tspA plain vanilla filling is deliberate — the base brings the flavour

Everything for the filling mixes better at room temperature, so get the cream cheese, eggs and cream out of the fridge half an hour before you start if you remember. Cold cream cheese beaten hard to get it smooth is how air gets whipped into a cheesecake, and air is what puffs a filling up in the oven and then lets it sink and crack as it cools. Gentle mixing of soft ingredients is the whole secret, such as it is.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line the base of a 20cm springform tin with a circle of baking paper and lightly butter the ring.
  2. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put 90g butter, 90g golden syrup and 90g light muscovado in a pan over a low heat and stir until everything has melted into one smooth liquid. Keep it gentle and do not let it boil — boiled syrup sets like toffee and would make the base hard instead of chewy.
  3. Stir in the oats. Take the pan off the heat, tip in 175g porridge oats and stir until every oat is coated and there are no dry patches.
  4. Press the base into the tin. Spoon the mixture into the tin and press it down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working well into the edges. Aim for an even layer about a centimetre thick — no thick centre, no thin corners.
  5. Bake the base on its own. Bake for 15 minutes, until the surface looks set and dry and the edges are just turning golden. It should feel firm when pressed lightly. Leave it to cool in the tin for at least 15 minutes while you make the filling — a hot base would start melting the filling on contact.
  6. Beat the cream cheese and sugar. In a large bowl, beat 600g full-fat cream cheese with 150g caster sugar just until smooth and combined. Stop as soon as it is smooth; you are mixing, not whipping.
  7. Add the eggs, cream and vanilla. Beat in the 2 eggs and the extra yolk one at a time, then stir in 150ml double cream and 1 tsp vanilla extract until the filling is one even mixture. Scrape the bowl down once and give it a final few stirs by hand.
  8. Pour over the base and bake. Pour the filling over the cooled base, level the top, and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 45–50 minutes. The edges should look set and slightly puffed, and the centre should still wobble gently as one piece when you nudge the tin.
  9. Cool slowly, then chill. Switch the oven off and leave the cheesecake inside with the door ajar for about an hour — sudden cooling is what cracks the top. Then cool to room temperature, cover and chill for at least 4 hours, and overnight if you can. The base firms up fully in the fridge and the slices cut far better for the wait.
  10. Release and slice. Run a thin knife around the inside of the ring, unclip it and lift it away. Slice with a large knife dipped in hot water and wiped dry between cuts — the chewy base needs that firm, clean press rather than a sawing motion.

Baking and cooling: reading the wobble

A baked cheesecake is done before it looks done, and this is where most go wrong. The filling sets from the outside in, so at the end of the bake the centre — a circle about the size of a saucer — should still move when the tin is nudged, wobbling gently as one piece, like set jelly, rather than sloshing like liquid. That wobble is not rawness; the residual heat finishes the centre as the cheesecake cools, and it settles into the smooth, dense texture you want. Bake until the centre looks completely firm and you have baked the life out of it: dry edges, a domed top, and a crack on its way. The crack itself usually arrives during cooling, not baking — a cheesecake taken straight from a hot oven to a cool worktop contracts too fast and splits. The hour in the switched-off oven with the door ajar is the insurance, and it costs nothing but patience.

How the layers should look

Cut a slice and look at it from the side, because that is the view that tells you whether the base worked. You want two clear layers: a deep, pale filling, and beneath it a base of about a centimetre — clearly oats, clearly one piece, dark golden rather than pale. If the base has swollen up into the filling or the boundary is blurred, the base went in too hot or was pressed too thick in the middle. If the base crumbles away from the slice as you lift it, it was underbaked in its first bake or pressed too loosely; the syrup sets the base as it cools, and it needs both the full press and the full 15 minutes to do it. Eaten, the base should give a short chew at the end of each mouthful, after the filling has melted away — that contrast is the whole reason for making it.

Flapjack base or biscuit crumb? An honest comparison

Neither base is better in the abstract; they are different tools, and this table sets out what actually changes between them so you can choose with your eyes open.

Flapjack base (this recipe)Crushed biscuit base
Main ingredient175g porridge oatsAbout 200g crushed biscuits
BinderButter, golden syrup and muscovado, melted togetherMelted butter alone
BakingPar-baked 15 minutes before the fillingOften unbaked, or about 10 minutes
Texture under a sliceChewy and holds as one layerCrunchy or soft, and prone to crumbling away
FlavourCaramel and toasted oats — a flavour of its ownButtery biscuit, in the background
CuttingNeeds a hot knife and a firm pressCuts easily
KeepingStays firm in the fridge across the cheesecake's 3 daysCan soften against the filling by the second day

The short version: choose the flapjack base when you want the base noticed — with a plain filling, at a table where people like oats and chew. Choose biscuit crumb when the filling is the whole event, heavily flavoured or very rich, and the base should stay quietly underneath it. A strongly flavoured filling on a flapjack base is a lot going on at once, and the pairing in this recipe — plain vanilla above, caramel oats below — is deliberate.

Slicing and serving

Serve the cheesecake cold from the fridge, in slices cut with the hot, dry knife described in the method — dip, wipe, press down through the base in one motion, and wipe again before the next cut, or the chewy base will drag crumbs through the filling's clean face. Eight slices from a 20cm tin are generous; the base makes this a more filling cheesecake than the same filling on biscuit crumb, and nobody will feel short-changed by a slightly thinner slice. Fresh berries over the top, as in the photographs, are a serving idea rather than part of the recipe — the cheesecake needs nothing, but a handful of raspberries or blueberries suits the plain vanilla and the caramel base very well. Take it out of the fridge about 20 minutes before serving if you like the filling softer; eaten fridge-cold it is denser and the base chewier, which is how it is best the day it is cut.

Storing the cheesecake: the fridge, and three days

This is not a flapjack, whatever its base is made of, and it does not store like one. The filling is eggs, cream cheese and cream, so the cheesecake lives in the fridge from the moment it has cooled, covered loosely so the top does not pick up other flavours, and it should be eaten within 3 days. Do not leave it standing at room temperature for more than a couple of hours at a time when serving. The base softens slightly against the filling by the third day but still holds a slice — another small argument for the par-bake, which gives it the firmness to spare. The bars on the rest of this site keep in a tin for days, but anything with this filling follows dairy rules, not flapjack rules, and the 3-day limit is the one to keep.
