Mango and Coconut Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

A mango and coconut flapjack is the classic oat bar with dried mango chopped small and folded through, and desiccated coconut stirred into the oats in place of some of them. The flavours are tropical; the method is not. Dried mango behaves in a bake the way raisins and apricots do — chewy, sweet, and dry enough not to wreck the set — which is exactly why the recipe uses the dried fruit and not the fresh.

The short answer: Melt 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado sugar in a pan, stir in 275g porridge oats, 75g desiccated coconut and a pinch of salt, fold in 100g dried mango chopped into small pieces, press into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes. Cool completely in the tin and cut into 12 bars.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Mango and coconut: tropical flavours, dried-fruit rules

Mango and coconut is a pairing that turns up wherever both grow — in drinks, in rice puddings, in the barfi in the photograph above — because the mango's perfume and the coconut's mild, fatty sweetness sit comfortably together. In a flapjack the pairing has a practical shape. The base here is our classic flapjack recipe with two adjustments: part of the oats is swapped for desiccated coconut, so the coconut runs through every bite rather than sitting on top, and the mango goes in dried and chopped, distributed through the mixture like any other dried fruit. The result tastes clearly of both, and it sets as firmly as the classic — which a fresh-mango version would not.

That last point is the whole recipe, really. Almost everything in our fruit flapjack recipe collection comes down to one question: is the fruit dry enough to bake into a bar that has to set? Dried mango passes easily. It is mango with the water taken out — the flavour is concentrated, the texture is chewy, and it adds sweetness without adding juice. Fresh mango fails the same test for the opposite reason, as the next section explains.

The mango: dried, chopped small, soaked only if it needs it

Buy dried mango in slices or pieces and chop it down before it goes anywhere near the pan. The pieces want to be raisin-to-sultana size — big enough to find in the bar, small enough to spread through it. A sharp knife works, but mango is tough and leathery and fights the blade; a pair of kitchen scissors is honestly the better tool, snipping the slices into strips and then across into pieces. If the pieces gum up the blades, a very light dusting of the measured oats over them stops them sticking together in a clump.

One judgement call: how dry is your mango? Some dried mango is soft and pliable, almost like a fruit leather — that goes straight in. Some is hard and leathery, the kind that takes real chewing, and that sort benefits from 10 minutes in hot water before it is chopped, which softens it so it bakes up chewy rather than tough. If you soak it, drain it well and pat it dry on kitchen paper. Soaking and then adding the fruit dripping wet defeats the object — you have put back the water the recipe was avoiding.

And the fresh fruit itself: leave it for eating. A ripe fresh mango is mostly juice and soft flesh. Folded through a flapjack mixture it would leak as it baked, soften the set and give you damp patches with a mild flavour — the worst of both forms. The dried fruit gives more mango flavour per bite anyway, because drying concentrates it. This is the same rule that governs the other fruit posts on this site, and our guide to why fruit flapjacks turn soggy explains the moisture maths behind it.

Ingredients

IngredientAmountNotes
Butter175gSalted or unsalted both work
Golden syrup175gThe full classic amount — it is the glue
Light muscovado sugar175gLight brown sugar is the nearest swap
Porridge oats275gReduced from the classic 350g — the coconut takes their place
Desiccated coconut75gThrough the mixture, not just on top
Dried mango100gChopped into small pieces. Soak 10 minutes in hot water and drain if it is very dry and leathery
Fine sea saltA pinchKeeps the sweetness of two fruits in check

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper, a large pan, and a pair of kitchen scissors or a sharp knife for the mango. If you are soaking the mango, a small bowl and a sieve for draining it well.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven, line the tin and prepare the mango. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides. Chop the dried mango into small pieces with scissors or a knife. If it is very dry and leathery, soak it in hot water for 10 minutes, then drain it well and pat it dry.
  2. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado in a large pan over a low heat. Stir until everything has melted into one smooth liquid. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil — boiled syrup sets like toffee and makes hard bars.
  3. Add the oats, coconut and salt. Take the pan off the heat and tip in 275g porridge oats, 75g desiccated coconut and a pinch of salt. Stir until everything is coated and shiny and the coconut is spread evenly through the oats, with no dry white patches.
  4. Fold in the mango. Add the chopped mango and fold it through until the pieces are distributed. If the pieces have stuck together in a clump, tease them apart with the spoon as you go.
  5. Press into the tin. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working well into the corners. Firm pressing is what holds the bars together once cut, and it matters more with coconut in the mixture, which binds a little less readily than oats alone.
  6. Bake for 30–35 minutes. The top should be golden with the edges a shade darker — the coconut at the edges colours fastest, so judge by the centre as well as the rim. The centre should look set but still feel slightly soft when pressed; it firms up as it cools.
  7. Cool completely and cut into 12. Leave the slab to cool in the tin until it is completely cold. Lift it out by the paper overhang and cut into 12 bars with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down.

Coconut through the mixture: desiccated, even, and in place of oats

The coconut decision in this recipe is where it goes, not just how much. Scatter coconut over the top of a flapjack and it toasts, looks pretty and tastes of very little beyond the first bite. Stir desiccated coconut through the mixture and it flavours the whole bar — every bite carries it, and it slightly softens the chew of the oats in a way most people like. That is why the recipe uses the desiccated form: the fine shreds distribute evenly through 275g of oats, where the larger flakes in the photograph below would sit in patches. Flakes are the same ingredient in a bigger cut, and they work if they are what you have, but expect a less even spread.

Notice, too, that the coconut replaces oats rather than being added on top of the full measure. The classic ratio of fat and syrup to dry ingredients is what makes the bar set; pile an extra 75g of dry ingredient into the same amount of butter and syrup and the mixture runs dry and crumbly. Swapping keeps the balance. If coconut is the flavour you are really after, our coconut flapjack recipe gives it the whole bar, and the chocolate and coconut flapjack recipe pairs it with dark chocolate instead of mango.

Swapping the mango: other dried fruits that behave the same way

Dried mango is doing a defined job in this recipe — chewy, sweet, dry fruit in small pieces — and several other dried fruits do the same job without any other change to the method. Use the same 100g, chopped the same way.

SwapAmountWhat changes
Dried apricots100g, chopped smallSharper and tangier than mango; the bars taste less sweet overall
Dried pineapple100g, chopped smallSweeter and more strongly flavoured; the most tropical-tasting swap
Dried papaya100g, chopped smallMilder than mango and often sold ready-diced; the closest in texture
Golden raisins or sultanas100g, no choppingThe mildest option — the coconut takes the lead flavour instead
Dried mango plus lime zest100g mango plus the zest of 1 limeNot a swap but a sharpening: the lime lifts the mango the way lemon lifts lemon bakes

Storing mango and coconut flapjacks: about 5 days in an airtight tin

Because the fruit in these bars is dried rather than fresh, they keep like a standard flapjack: about 5 days in an airtight tin at room temperature, with baking paper between the layers. The mango stays chewy and the coconut flavour holds up well across those days — if anything the bars are slightly better on day two, once the flavours have settled into the crumb. They also freeze well for up to 3 months, wrapped individually and sealed; defrost at room temperature in the wrapping. The general rules are in our guide to how to store flapjacks and how long they last.
