Peanut Butter Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

Peanut butter changes a flapjack from the inside out: it melts into the butter and syrup, brings its own richness, and turns a plain oat bar into something that tastes like the middle of a peanut butter sandwich spread over toffee oats. This version keeps the classic method and tin, with less butter — because the peanut butter is doing part of butter's job.

The short answer: Melt 100g butter with 150g smooth peanut butter, 100g golden syrup and 100g light brown sugar. Stir in 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt, press into a lined 20cm square tin, scatter over 50g chopped roasted peanuts if you like, and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 25–30 minutes. Cool completely in the tin and cut into 12 bars. Peanuts are a major allergen — these bars are not suitable for nut-free settings; see the allergy note below.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Allergy first: peanuts are a major allergen

Before the recipe, the important paragraph. Peanuts are one of the major food allergens, and peanut allergy can be severe. These flapjacks contain peanuts in the mixture itself — not as a garnish you can leave off — so they are not suitable for anyone with a peanut allergy, nor for settings that are kept nut-free, which includes many school lunchboxes. Do not serve them to anyone without telling them exactly what is in the tin, and if you bake them in a kitchen where a peanut-allergic person cooks, clean pans and utensils thoroughly, because peanut traces transfer just as flour does. If you need the flapjack without the nuts, our nut-free flapjack recipe is built for exactly that, with ingredient-checking tips for nut-free baking.

Why the butter drops to 100g: peanut butter brings its own fat

In the classic flapjack, 175g of butter coats 350g of oats. Peanut butter is roughly half fat itself, so if you simply added 150g of it on top of the full butter weight, the mixture would carry far more fat than the oats could hold, and the bars would bake out greasy and soft. The balance in this recipe is therefore a swap, not an addition: butter down to 100g, peanut butter in at 150g, and the syrup and sugar each trimmed to 100g because the mixture is already rich and the peanut butter is lightly sweetened in most jars. The total liquid still coats 350g of oats properly — it just arrives from two jars instead of one.

The pinch of salt matters more here than in the classic. Peanut butter is sold both salted and unsalted depending on the brand, and oats and syrup are both mild; a pinch of fine salt in the mixture pulls the peanut flavour forward and stops the bars tasting flat. Taste your peanut butter first: if it is a well-salted one, keep the pinch small; if it is an unsweetened, unsalted natural jar, the full pinch earns its place.

Smooth or crunchy peanut butter: what each does to the bars

Either will bake, but they do not bake alike. Smooth peanut butter melts completely into the butter and syrup, so every oat gets coated and the finished bars slice cleanly with an even, dense chew. Crunchy peanut butter leaves its peanut pieces whole, which is pleasant to eat but means the fat spreads less evenly; the bars are a little more fragile at the edges and crumble slightly more when cut. If crunchy is what you have, use it and press the mixture down especially firmly — and the optional chopped-peanut topping below becomes unnecessary, because the crunch is already inside.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving overhang at two sides so the slab lifts out cleanly.
  2. Melt the butter and peanut butter together. Put 100g butter and 150g smooth peanut butter in a large pan over a low heat. Stir as they melt into each other — the peanut butter will loosen and swirl into the butter. Keep the heat gentle; peanut butter scorches faster than butter alone and will taste bitter if it catches.
  3. Add the syrup and sugar. Stir in 100g golden syrup and 100g light brown sugar and keep stirring over the low heat until everything is one smooth, dark, glossy mixture. Take it off the heat as soon as it comes together; do not let it boil.
  4. Stir in the oats and salt. Tip in all 350g of porridge oats and a pinch of salt. Stir thoroughly, turning the mixture over until every flake is coated and no dry oat remains. The mixture will be thick and heavy — that is right.
  5. Press into the tin. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press it down very firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working into the corners. Peanut butter mixtures are denser than the classic and reward a really firm press with clean-cutting bars.
  6. Add the peanut topping, if using. Scatter 50g chopped roasted peanuts evenly over the surface and press them in lightly with the spoon so they sit in the mixture rather than on it — loose nuts fall off when the bars are cut.
  7. Bake for 25–30 minutes. The bake is a little shorter than the classic, because peanut butter browns readily. Look for golden edges a shade darker than the middle, and a centre that looks set and feels firm with a slight give. If the top colours too quickly, the bars are done — take them out rather than pushing for more colour.
  8. Cool completely in the tin. Essential with this recipe: warm, the bars are soft and fragile, because the peanut butter only firms up as it cools. Leave them at least two hours, and do not be tempted to cut early.
  9. Lift out and cut into 12. Use the paper overhang to lift the cold slab onto a board. Cut with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down; the peanut topping, if you used it, cuts neater with a gentle sawing motion on the first pass through the nuts.

Troubleshooting: dry, crumbly or too soft

If a batch comes out dry and crumbly, the peanut butter is the first suspect. Natural jars vary: if the oil was poured off or not stirred back in, you have effectively removed part of the recipe's fat and the oats go short. Stir any natural jar completely smooth before weighing, so the oil is in the mixture where it belongs. Overbaking does the same damage more simply — peanut bars firm up a great deal as they cool, so a slab that looked barely set at 25 minutes can be exactly right cold; judge by the cues in step 7, not by prodding for hardness in the oven.

Too soft, and the cause is usually the reverse: a very oily jar, an underbake, or cutting while warm. Chilling a soft batch for an hour firms it enough to cut, though the honest fix for the next batch is two extra minutes in the oven. Greasy bars point the same way as the classic — too much free fat for the oats — which is why the butter here is 100g and not the classic 175g; if you doubled the peanut butter "for more peanut flavour", that is the batch that will weep oil. Keep the proportions and add flavour with the chopped peanut topping instead.

Variations: jam, chocolate and other nut butters

A thin layer of raspberry or strawberry jam spread over the pressed mixture before baking gives you the sandwich in bar form — use about 3 tablespoons, spread it shy of the edges, and know that the bars will be softer and keep a day less well. Dark chocolate chips, about 75g stirred in off the heat, melt into the mixture in streaks; let the pan sit for a minute first so they soften rather than vanish. Other nut butters — almond or cashew — swap in at the same 150g weight and behave similarly, though both are milder, so the bars taste less boldly of the nut; remember they are still nuts, and the allergy note at the top of this page applies in full. For storage, keep the cut bars in an airtight tin at room temperature with paper between the layers; they keep about 5 days, and freeze well for up to 3 months wrapped individually — see how to store flapjacks for the full keeping guide.
