Maple Syrup Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

Maple syrup is the swap people reach for when they want a flapjack that tastes a little less like a sweetshop and a little more like breakfast. It replaces the golden syrup in our tested classic weight for weight, the sugar comes down slightly to make room for it, and the bars come out with a softer set, a gentler sweetness and a warm, woody flavour that golden syrup never gives.

The short answer: Melt 175g butter, 175g pure maple syrup and 150g light muscovado sugar. Stir in 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt. Press into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes, allowing a few minutes longer if the centre still looks loose. Cool completely in the tin and cut into 12 bars.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Maple syrup in a flapjack: the classic with a gentler sweetener

The base here is our classic flapjack recipe, the version we have kitchen-tested: butter, syrup and sugar melted together, twice their weight of oats stirred in, pressed into a 20cm tin and baked. This version changes the syrup and trims the sugar, and nothing else — the same ratio of fat to oats, the same tin, the same oven temperature. That matters, because it means the structure you already trust is doing the binding; only the flavour and the set shift.

Two honest differences to expect. First, maple syrup is runnier than golden syrup — it pours like a thin oil where golden syrup crawls off the spoon — so the mixture going into the tin is slacker, and the finished bars set a touch softer. Second, it is a little less sweet, which is why the muscovado drops from 175g to 150g rather than staying level: with the full amount of sugar as well, the maple flavour would be buried under plain sweetness. As it stands, the bars taste of butter and oats first, with maple coming through as a warm, slightly woody note rather than a shout. If you want to understand the binder's job properly, our guide to how much golden syrup in flapjacks covers what syrup does in the mixture and why the weight matters more than the spoon count.

Maple grades: the darker syrup bakes better

Maple syrup is graded by colour, and colour tracks flavour. The syrup run early in the season is pale and delicate; as the season goes on the sap darkens and the flavour deepens. The grades you will see on bottles run from golden, delicate taste through amber, rich taste to dark, robust taste, with very dark, strong taste at the far end. For pouring over pancakes, the pale golden grade has its fans. For baking, it is the wrong end of the shelf: its flavour is so gentle that butter, oats and oven heat between them leave almost nothing behind.

Dark, robust syrup is the baker's grade. It costs no more — if anything it is often the cheaper bottle — and its stronger, almost caramelised flavour is the one that survives a flapjack. Amber, rich taste sits in the middle and works perfectly well if that is what you have. What follows is the simple version of the choice.

Golden, delicate tastePale gold, light and gentleWorks, but the maple note mostly disappears behind the butter and oats

One more thing the grade affects: colour. A dark syrup gives the bars a deeper, more bronzed top than golden syrup does, which can make them look done a few minutes before they are. Judge the bake by the set — set edges, a centre that no longer looks wet — rather than by colour alone.

Ingredients

IngredientAmountNotes

Light muscovado sugar150gTrimmed from the classic's 175g because the syrup brings its own sweetness and flavour

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper and a large pan. Weighing the maple syrup deserves a word: because it runs freely, it coats spoons and jugs and leaves a good spoonful behind each time, so the reliable method is to put the pan on the scales, add the butter, then pour the syrup in to weight. What clings to the bottle is a cook's perk, not a loss.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides for lifting the slab out later.
  2. Melt the butter, maple syrup and sugar. Put 175g butter, 175g maple syrup and 150g light muscovado in a large pan over a low heat. Stir until the butter has melted and the sugar has dissolved into one smooth, glossy liquid. Keep the heat gentle and do not let it boil — boiled syrup mixtures set hard.
  3. Add the oats and salt. Take the pan off the heat and tip in 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt. Stir until every oat is coated and there are no dry patches at the bottom of the pan. The mixture will look looser than the classic's — that is the runnier syrup, and it is expected.
  4. Press into the tin. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working well into the corners. With a softer-setting mixture, firm pressing matters even more than usual — it is what holds the bars together once cut.
  5. Bake for 30–35 minutes, checking the set. The edges should look set and a shade darker, and the centre should look set rather than wet and glossy. Because the maple mixture starts slacker, the centre may need a few minutes beyond the classic's time — up to about 40 minutes in total. It will still feel slightly soft in the middle; it firms as it cools.
  6. Score while warm. About 5 minutes after the tin comes out, mark the portion lines with a table knife — 12 bars — while the top is soft enough not to crack.
  7. Cool completely in the tin. Leave the slab until it is cold and fully set before cutting. This matters more here than with the classic: a maple flapjack cut warm will slump and crumble, while the same slab cut cold slices cleanly.
  8. Lift out and cut. Use the paper overhang to lift the cold slab onto a board and cut through along the scored lines with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down.

Maple sugar and maple extract are different things

Two other maple products sit near the syrup in shops, and neither is a swap for it. Maple sugar is maple syrup boiled down until it sets solid — it is a sugar, not a syrup. It brings a strong maple flavour but no liquid and none of the binding a syrup gives, so it cannot replace the maple syrup in this recipe. What it can do is replace part of the muscovado: swap 50g of the sugar for 50g of maple sugar and the maple note deepens noticeably, though it is an expensive way to flavour a tray of flapjacks and entirely optional.

Maple extract is different again: a flavouring, sold in small bottles like vanilla extract, meant to be used a few drops at a time. It adds maple scent but no sweetness, no liquid and no binding. Its one honest use here is rescue — if the only syrup you could get was the pale golden grade, a few drops of extract stirred into the melted mixture will push the flavour forward. It is a patch, not a plan; buying the darker syrup is simpler.

“Maple-flavoured” syrup is not maple syrup

The cheapest bottles on the shelf are often labelled maple-flavoured syrup, and the wording is doing real work. Those blends are mostly other syrups — sugar syrup or glucose-fructose syrup — with maple flavouring and colouring added, and sometimes only a small proportion of actual maple syrup or none at all. They are made to sit on pancakes at a low price, and there is nothing dishonest about them as long as you know what you are buying.

For this recipe, though, the distinction is the whole recipe. A blend will still melt, coat the oats and set into bars, but it will taste of generic sweetness rather than maple, and because the base syrups differ, the set can shift too — some blends are thicker, some thinner than pure maple syrup. The check takes two seconds: the bottle should say pure maple syrup, and the ingredients list should have one entry on it. Expect to pay noticeably more than for a blend; that is the cost of the tree, the season and the boiling, and it is why this is a bake for a bottle you mean to taste. Our honey flapjack recipe makes the same point from the other side — a named liquid sweetener only earns its place in the title if it is really in the tin.

Storing maple flapjacks

Keep the cut bars in an airtight tin at room temperature with baking paper between the layers — worth doing carefully here, because the softer set means the bars can stick to each other in a warm kitchen. They keep about 5 days, and the maple flavour is often clearer on the second day, once the bars have settled. They freeze well too: wrap individually, seal in a bag or box and use within 3 months, defrosting at room temperature in the wrapping. There is more detail in our guide to how to store flapjacks and how long they last.
