Ginger Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

The classic flapjack is a sweet, plain thing — butter, syrup, sugar and oats — and that plainness is exactly why it takes spice so well. This version keeps the tested classic exactly as it is and warms it through with ground ginger in the mixture and little chewy pieces of crystallised ginger folded through, so some bites are gentle and some land with a proper kick.

The short answer: Melt 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado sugar. Stir in 2 teaspoons of ground ginger, then 350g porridge oats, a pinch of salt and 75g crystallised or stem ginger chopped small. Press into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes. Cool completely in the tin and cut into 12 bars.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Ginger flapjacks: the classic recipe, warmed through

A plain flapjack is really a delivery system for butter and golden syrup, and its flavour is soft and caramel-like rather than spiced. Ginger changes that balance without changing the bake. Ground ginger stirred through the melted mixture spreads its warmth through every oat, and the heat survives the oven better than most people expect — what fades is the sharp, fresh top note, and what stays is a slow warmth at the back of the mouth. Little pieces of crystallised or stem ginger do a different job: because they go in as pieces, they stay as pockets of stronger, chewier heat scattered through the bar, so eating one is a small series of surprises.

The base here is our classic flapjack recipe, which is the version we have kitchen-tested: equal weights of butter, golden syrup and light muscovado sugar, twice that weight of porridge oats, baked in a 20cm tin. Nothing about the structure changes — the same ratio binds, presses and sets in the same way — so if the classic works in your oven, this one should behave the same way with a spiced flavour. The ginger is the only variable, which means any difference in the result is the spice, not a quietly altered ratio.

Ground, fresh or crystallised: three gingers, three kinds of heat

Ginger is not one ingredient but a small family, and the three forms you are most likely to meet behave differently in a flapjack. Ground ginger is the baker's spice: dried, powdered and consistent, it mixes evenly through a melted flapjack mixture and gives a warm, rounded heat with no fibrous bits. Fresh ginger is brighter and sharper, almost lemony, but it brings water and fibre with it, and its fiercest notes are the ones the oven softens most. Crystallised ginger — pieces of the root cooked in syrup and rolled in sugar — and its close relation stem ginger, which is sold in jars in syrup, keep their chew and deliver their heat in bursts.

Form of gingerKind of heatHow it behaves in a flapjack
Ground gingerWarm, rounded, evenThe backbone of this recipe. Mixes through the whole batch so every bite is spiced; nothing to chop, nothing fibrous
Fresh ginger, finely gratedBright, sharp, lemonyThe top notes fade most in the oven. Adds a little moisture and fine fibre; best used alongside ground ginger, not instead of it
Crystallised gingerSweet heat in chewy burstsSurvives baking as soft, sugary pieces. Chop small so the heat is scattered rather than landing in one mouthful
Stem ginger (in syrup)Deep, mellow, syrupyInterchangeable with crystallised in this recipe. Drain it, pat it dry and chop small; a spoonful of its syrup can replace a spoonful of the golden syrup

This recipe uses two of them together — ground ginger for the background warmth, crystallised or stem for the pieces — because neither quite does the other's job. A flapjack made with ground ginger alone is evenly warm and very good, but a little one-note. A flapjack made with pieces alone is plain for three bites and fiery on the fourth. The combination is the point.

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper and a large pan — the oats and the ginger pieces make this a bulky mixture, and a big pan makes the stirring far easier. If your crystallised ginger comes in large slabs, kitchen scissors or a heavy knife dipped in hot water will chop it without it gluing itself to the blade.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides for lifting the slab out later.
  2. Chop the crystallised ginger. Cut 75g of crystallised or drained stem ginger into small pieces, roughly pea-sized. Smaller pieces spread the heat evenly; a few slightly larger ones are fine and give the occasional stronger bite.
  3. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado in a large pan over a low heat. Stir until everything has melted into one smooth liquid. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil — boiled syrup sets like toffee and makes hard bars.
  4. Stir in the ground ginger. Take the pan off the heat and stir in 2 teaspoons of ground ginger until it disappears evenly into the mixture. Adding it now, while the fat is liquid and before the oats go in, means it coats everything rather than sitting in dry pockets.
  5. Add the oats, salt and ginger pieces. Tip in 350g porridge oats, a pinch of salt and the chopped crystallised ginger. Stir until every oat is coated and shiny and there are no dry patches hiding at the bottom of the pan.
  6. Press into the tin. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working well into the corners. Firm pressing is what stops flapjacks crumbling later.
  7. Bake for 30–35 minutes. The top should be golden brown with the edges a shade darker, and the centre should look set but still feel slightly soft when pressed — it firms up as it cools. A ginger flapjack looks much like the classic at this stage; the spice does not change the colour.
  8. Score while warm. About 5 minutes after the tin comes out, mark the portion lines with a table knife — 12 bars — while the top is soft enough not to crack.
  9. Cool completely in the tin. Leave the slab until it is cold and set before cutting; warm flapjack cuts raggedly and can fall apart. An hour at least, and longer does no harm.
  10. Lift out and cut. Use the paper overhang to lift the cold slab onto a board and cut through along the scored lines with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down.

Turning the heat up or down

Ginger heat is a matter of taste, and this recipe is written at the level most people would call properly warm without being challenging. The honest way to adjust it is one change at a time, because the three forms do different things. For a gentler bar — the version for children or for anyone who likes gingerbread more than ginger — drop the ground ginger to 1 teaspoon and keep the pieces at 75g; the bars will taste spiced and sweet with only mild warmth. For a hotter bar, raise the ground ginger to 3 teaspoons before you add more pieces: ground spice lifts the whole batch evenly, while piling in extra crystallised ginger just makes the fiery bites more frequent and leaves the rest of the bar unchanged.

Fresh ginger deserves a word of caution as a substitute. It is tempting to grate a thumb-sized piece (roughly 15g, finely grated) into the melted mixture, and it does work — but expect a brighter, sharper, slightly lemony flavour rather than more of the same warmth, and expect it to fade more in the oven than the ground spice does. If fresh is what you have, use it alongside 1 teaspoon of ground ginger rather than instead of the full amount. And if you swap stem ginger's syrup in for part of the golden syrup, keep it to a spoonful or two — the jar syrup is thinner than golden syrup, and a large swap softens the set.

The Christmas cousin: gingerbread flapjacks

If what you are really after is the full dark, treacle-heavy Christmas flavour, this gentle ginger version has a bigger sibling. Our gingerbread flapjack recipe swaps the light muscovado for dark, brings in black treacle alongside the syrup, and layers cinnamon and mixed spice behind the ginger. The two recipes share the same base and the same method — the difference is depth: this one is bright and warm, the other is dark and mellow. Making both in December and putting them side by side on the same plate is a very good use of an afternoon.

Storing ginger flapjacks

Keep the cut bars in an airtight tin at room temperature with baking paper between the layers. They keep about 5 days; the spice flavour settles and melds over the first day or two, so like many spiced bakes they are arguably better on day two than on day one. They also freeze well — wrap the bars individually, seal them in a bag or box and use within 3 months, defrosting at room temperature in the wrapping. There is more detail in our guide to how to store flapjacks and how long they last.
