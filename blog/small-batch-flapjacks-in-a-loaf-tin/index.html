Small-Batch Flapjacks in a Loaf Tin

By Michael

Sometimes twelve bars is too many. A half batch — flapjacks enough for one household for a couple of days, or a trial run of an idea before you commit a full tin to it — wants a smaller home, and the tin that suits it best is the one already in most cupboards: a standard 2lb loaf tin. Its long, narrow base takes half the classic recipe at close to the proper depth, and this page works through the quantities, the lining and the baking.

The short answer: Halve the classic recipe — 90g butter, 90g golden syrup, 90g light muscovado sugar and 175g porridge oats, with the same pinch of salt — and bake it in a lined 2lb loaf tin at the usual 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). The quantities are the classic's, halved and rounded to numbers a scale can actually weigh. Expect a slightly shorter bake than the full batch's 30 to 35 minutes — around 25 to 30 — and check early: golden edges, centre just set. The tin gives you about six thick bars.

Why a Loaf Tin, and Why the 2lb Size

A half batch spread across the usual 20cm square tin would lie far too thin — it would bake in no time, dry at the edges and cut into wafers. What a half batch needs is a base roughly half the area, so the mixture sits at something like its proper depth. A standard 2lb loaf tin has a base of about 21×11cm, around 231cm² against the square tin's 400cm². Half the mixture on a little over half the area lands at a touch under the full depth: close enough that the bake behaves like the one you know, just smaller.

The 1lb loaf tin is the trap here. It looks like the obvious small-batch tin and is far too small for a half batch — the mixture would sit deep, and a deep, narrow bake is slow to cook through and quick to darken at the ends. If a 1lb tin is what you have, the honest options are a still smaller batch, or a different small tin: anything sound with a base in the region of 200 to 250cm² will serve. Our guide to flapjack tin sizes covers the standard sizes; this page is about making one of them do a particular job.

The Quantities: The Classic Halved, Then Rounded

The rounding deserves a word, because it is deliberate. Half of 175g is 87.5g, and nobody's kitchen scale — or patience — works in half grams. Ninety grams is the weighable version of the same number, and at 2.5g in over 400g of mixture it changes nothing you can taste, provided all three of butter, syrup and sugar are rounded the same way so their proportions hold. The oats halve exactly, at 175g. The salt stays a pinch: seasoning does not scale down in straight lines, and a small pinch judged by the same fingers is the right amount for the smaller batch.

The classic flapjack recipe these quantities come from makes 12 bars in the square tin. Halved and baked in the loaf tin, the same mixture gives about six thick bars, cut across the loaf — or eight slimmer ones, if you cut narrower slices. Either way it is a two-or-three-day supply for a small household rather than a fortnight's commitment, which for many bakers is the entire point.

Lining a Long, Thin Tin

A loaf tin is lined with a single strip of baking parchment, cut to the width of the tin and laid along its length, so the paper covers the base and the two long sides and hangs over both short ends. Those overhangs are your handles: a flapjack slab in a narrow tin cannot be tipped out or prised at without damage, but it lifts out cleanly by its paper sling once it is cold. Grease the tin lightly first so the strip stays where you put it while the mixture goes in — narrow tins are where loose paper loves to skid and fold into corners.

The general method is the same as for the square tin, and our guide to how to line a flapjack tin covers it in full. The one loaf-tin habit worth forming is at the ends: press the mixture firmly into the two short ends of the tin, where a lazy press leaves the mixture thin and the end bars fragile. In a square tin a corner is a small fault; in a loaf tin, the ends are a third of your bars.

Making a Half Batch: Small Quantities, Same Method

The method is the classic's method, unaltered: melt the butter, syrup and sugar together gently until the sugar has dissolved, take the pan off the heat, stir through the oats and the pinch of salt until every oat is coated, press into the lined tin, bake. Nothing about the small scale changes the order of work or the care it needs — the melt should still not boil, the oats still want a thorough stir, the press still wants to be firm and level.

What small quantities do change is the margin for waste. Syrup clinging to the inside of a jug or left in the melting pan is a rounding error in a full batch; in a half batch it can be a noticeable share of the whole amount, and the mixture will be drier for it. Weigh the syrup into the pan if you can, or at least scrape the jug and the spoon properly. The same goes for oats left in the bowl: at half scale, everything left behind is twice the proportion it would have been.

Baking: Same Temperature, a Little Less Time, Checked Early

The temperature does not change: 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3), the classic's heat, because the mixture is the classic's mixture. The time shortens a little. The half batch sits at slightly less than the full depth, and time follows depth — so expect it to need a little less than the full batch's 30 to 35 minutes, around 25 to 30, and treat that as guidance to check against rather than a promise. Start looking a few minutes before you think it will be ready.

The signs are the familiar ones, unchanged by scale: the edges golden and set, the centre just set and no longer wet-looking, a slight firmness when the middle is pressed. A loaf tin hides its centre between two long walls, so judge by the middle of the surface, not the invitingly dark ends, which always look more done than they are. If the centre is still glossy and soft, give it a few minutes more and look again — small batches forgive patience and punish guessing. And as always, the slab finishes setting as it cools in the tin; a centre that seems barely set at the oven door is usually exactly right by the time it is cold.

Cutting and Keeping Six Bars

Let the slab cool in its tin until it is properly cold and firm, then lift it out by the parchment overhang and peel the paper away. Cut across the loaf, the short way, into even slices — about six thick bars from a half batch, each a generous square-ish portion, or eight if you slice thinner. A loaf shape flatters the cutting: every slice is the same width by construction, so a small batch portions more evenly than a square slab does.

Keeping is the same as for any flapjack. The bars store in an airtight tin at room temperature for about five days — a period a six-bar batch will rarely see out — and they freeze wrapped for up to three months, which is how a small batch becomes a standing supply: bake double small batches on two days and the freezer holds the difference. Our post on how to store flapjacks has the full detail. The practical charm of the small batch is freshness, though: six bars eaten within their best days, rather than twelve bars declining politely into a second week.

When a Small Batch Earns Its Keep

The best use of a half batch is the experiment. A new variation — a different sugar, a handful of something stirred through, a chocolate top — risks 90g of butter in a loaf tin rather than a full tin's worth if it disappoints, and a six-bar trial tells you everything a twelve-bar one would. It is also the right size for using up the end of a bag of oats or a jar of syrup that will not stretch to a full bake, and for households where a full tin would outstay its welcome.

What it is not for is feeding a crowd by accumulation — four loaf tins baked in turn is the hard way to do what two square tins do easily. The small batch is a precision tool: a test, a treat for a small house, a way of keeping flapjacks in the kitchen without keeping them too long. Used for those, it may quietly become the batch you bake most often.
