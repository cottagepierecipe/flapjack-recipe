Yoghurt-Topped Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

This is the classic flapjack with a fresh finish: the bars are baked exactly as usual, cooled completely, and only then spread with a lemon yoghurt icing that sets softly in the fridge. Let us be clear about what this is — the topping is a fresh icing added after baking, never baked on. The result is a sweet, chewy oat bar under a cool, tangy white layer, and the contrast is the whole point.

The short answer: Bake the classic flapjack — 175g butter, 175g golden syrup, 175g light muscovado, 350g porridge oats, a pinch of salt, 20cm tin, 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) — and let it cool completely in the tin. Whisk 150g strained Greek yoghurt with 100g icing sugar, 1 tsp lemon juice and the zest of 1 lemon, spread it over the cold slab, and chill for about 1 hour until just set. Keep the finished bars in the fridge and eat within 2 days.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Yoghurt topping on flapjacks: a fresh icing, added after baking

A flapjack is sweet, buttery and rich, with no contrast built into it — which is exactly what a yoghurt topping supplies. Spread thinly over a cold slab and sharpened with lemon, tangy yoghurt cuts through the caramel sweetness, and served cool from the fridge it adds a temperature contrast too: cold, soft icing over a dense, chewy bar. It turns a sturdy lunchbox bake into something closer to a dessert — a clean white layer over a golden slab cuts into very handsome bars.

What it is not is a baked topping. Yoghurt cannot go through the oven: heat splits it, the smooth texture curdles, and a sweetened yoghurt mixture would slide, bubble and turn grainy long before the oats were cooked. So the order of work is fixed — bake the bars, cool them completely, then ice them. "Completely" is doing real work in that sentence: a slab that is even slightly warm will melt the icing's set, thin the yoghurt and send the whole layer sliding towards the edges. Give the flapjack its full cooling time in the tin, and only then think about the bowl of yoghurt.

Straining the yoghurt: the step that decides whether the topping sets

Everything about this topping depends on how thick the yoghurt is. Icing is mostly a battle against water: icing sugar can absorb a little liquid and still set, but yoghurt is far wetter than the butter in an ordinary frosting, and every spoonful of whey left in it will weep out across your flapjack. That is why the recipe specifies strained Greek yoghurt — the thick kind that holds the mark of the spoon. Bought strained yoghurt is the easy route, and the one this recipe is written for.

If all you have is ordinary yoghurt, strain it yourself: line a sieve with muslin or a clean thin tea towel, set it over a bowl, tip the yoghurt in and leave it in the fridge to drain until properly thick — allow a couple of hours, or overnight if the yoghurt was very runny. You will lose a surprising amount of liquid, so start with more than the 150g you need. Low-fat yoghurts are the wettest of all; if the drained result still slumps off the spoon, it will not hold a layer, and you are better off buying the strained kind. The test is simple: a spoonful should sit up in a soft peak, not spread into a pool.

That is the tested classic, unchanged — a firm, fully set slab matters more here than in a plain flapjack, because it has to be iced and cut without the topping sliding. The topping's ingredients are below. You will also need a sieve and muslin if you are straining your own yoghurt, and fridge space for the tin — check it fits before you start, because the slab is chilled in its tin.

The yoghurt icing: yoghurt, icing sugar and lemon

IngredientAmountNotes
Strained Greek yoghurt150gThe thick kind that holds a spoon's mark; strain ordinary yoghurt first if needed
Icing sugar100gSift it — lumps of icing sugar will not whisk out of a cold yoghurt icing
Lemon juice1 tspFreshly squeezed; a teaspoon is flavour and sharpness, not liquid — do not be tempted by more
Lemon zestZest of 1 lemonFinely grated, yellow part only; the white pith is bitter

The lemon is doing two jobs. The juice sharpens the yoghurt's natural tang so the topping tastes fresh rather than merely sweet, and the zest brings the actual lemon flavour, most of which lives in the skin's oils rather than the juice. One teaspoon of juice is a deliberate limit: the icing has almost no spare capacity for liquid, and a generous squeeze will undo all the good work of straining. If you want more lemon, add more zest, not more juice. The icing sugar is not only sweetness — it is the setting agent that turns flavoured yoghurt into something that will hold a soft layer. Sift it in rather than tipping it, and whisk just until smooth; overworking a yoghurt icing thins it.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, with a little overhang at two sides for lifting the slab out later.
  2. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. In a large pan over a low heat, melt 175g butter with 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado sugar, stirring until smooth and glossy. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil.
  3. Stir in the oats and salt. Off the heat, tip in 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt. Stir until every flake is coated and there are no dry patches hiding at the bottom of the pan.
  4. Press into the tin. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly, working into the corners. A level top matters here — the icing will show every bump and hollow.
  5. Bake until golden and set. Bake following the classic's timing — the top golden brown, the edges a shade darker, the centre set and firm with a slight give when pressed.
  6. Score while warm, then cool completely. After about 10 minutes, mark the portion lines lightly with a table knife — the scoring will guide your knife under the icing later. Then leave the slab in the tin until it is completely cold. Not nearly cold: completely. Warmth is the one thing that will ruin the topping.
  7. Whisk the icing. Put 150g strained Greek yoghurt in a bowl. Sift over 100g icing sugar, add 1 tsp lemon juice and the finely grated zest of 1 lemon, and whisk just until smooth and evenly lemony. It should fall off the whisk in a thick ribbon and hold soft peaks.
  8. Spread the icing over the cold slab. Spoon the icing into the middle of the cold slab, still in its tin, and spread it out to the edges and corners with the back of a spoon or a palette knife. Work in one direction and do not keep going back over it — every extra pass thins the layer and drags crumbs into it.
  9. Chill for about 1 hour. Put the tin in the fridge until the icing has firmed to a soft set — it will hold a clean line from a knife but stay creamy, never hard like fondant.
  10. Lift out and cut into 12. Lift the chilled slab out by the paper overhang. Cut along your scored lines with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down, and wipe the blade clean between every cut — a dirty blade drags white icing down the golden sides.

Spreading the icing: cover the slab in one go

Spreading a soft icing is a confidence job. Put all of it in the middle of the slab at once rather than a spoonful at a time, then push it outwards in long, even strokes, letting it fall towards the edges under its own weight. Chasing a perfectly smooth finish is the beginner's trap: yoghurt icing levels itself to a point and then works against you, picking up oat crumbs and thinning where the spoon has passed too often. Near enough, covered corner to corner, is the right standard — the layer settles as it chills and small marks soften out. Ice the slab in its tin, as the method says: the sides give you something to spread against at the edges, and the slab is far easier to move into the fridge before it is cut.

Cutting and serving a topped flapjack

A topped flapjack cuts differently from a plain one, and it is worth slowing down for it. Chill first — a soft icing cut at room temperature smears, while a chilled one divides cleanly. Follow the lines you scored into the warm slab before icing; they are your map under the white layer. Press the knife straight down rather than sawing, and wipe it between cuts, every cut — the contrast of white top and golden side is the beauty of these bars, and it survives only if the blade stays clean. Serve straight from the fridge or after just a few minutes out, while the topping is still cool against the chewy bar. If fruit is your thing, a handful of fresh berries alongside makes a better partner than anything baked in — and our cherry flapjack recipe is the one to try when you want the fruit inside the bar instead.

Storing yoghurt-topped flapjacks: the fridge, and 2 days

Everything about storing these bars follows from one fact: the topping is fresh yoghurt. Keep the cut bars in a single layer in an airtight box in the fridge — a single layer, because the soft icing sticks to anything stacked on it; if you must stack, put baking paper between the layers and accept a few marks. Eat them within 2 days: the icing is at its best on the first day, still good on the second, and after that it dries at the edges and loses its freshness. This is the one flapjack on the site where the fridge is not optional, so our usual advice in the guide to how to store flapjacks — a tin at room temperature — does not apply here. To get ahead, freeze the baked slab untopped and ice it after defrosting: the icing takes five minutes, and the bars will taste freshly made, because they are.

A yoghurt icing thaws watery and grainy, so it is far better to freeze the baked slab or plain cut bars — wrapped well, as with any flapjack — and ice them after defrosting, once they are back at room temperature and dry on top.
