Blueberry Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

Fresh fruit changes a flapjack more than dried fruit does, and blueberries change it most visibly of all. This version folds 200g of fresh blueberries through the tested classic mixture at the very end. In the oven the berries soften, some burst, and their juice streaks the bars purple and keeps the set softer and jammier than the plain classic. It is the same recipe, the same tin and the same bake — with one juicy ingredient to handle gently and one softer set to expect.

The short answer: Melt 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado sugar. Stir in 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt, then fold in 200g fresh blueberries gently, at the end. Press into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes, allowing a few minutes longer if the centre still looks wet. Cool completely in the tin and cut into 12 bars. The set will be softer than the classic — that is the berries, not a mistake.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

What 200g of blueberries do to the classic flapjack

A blueberry is mostly water held inside a thin skin, and the oven undoes that arrangement. As the bars bake, the berries swell, soften and split, and their juice runs into the surrounding oats. Three things follow. The colour changes: the juice is a deep purple that streaks the golden mixture, so the finished bars come out marbled rather than evenly golden, with the strongest colour around each berry. The flavour changes: against all that butter and syrup, the berries stay bright and slightly sharp, which is why fruit flapjacks taste less heavy than the plain ones. And the set changes: the juice is extra moisture the classic recipe never had to deal with, so the bars set softer, especially in the middle, and cut best when fully cold.

The base underneath all this is our classic flapjack recipe, which is the version we have kitchen-tested: equal weights of butter, golden syrup and light muscovado sugar, twice that weight of porridge oats, baked in a 20cm tin. The ratio is untouched here, because it already has syrup to spare — which is precisely why it can absorb a punnet of juicy fruit without falling apart. The blueberries are an addition, not a reformulation, and everything different about the result traces back to them.

Fresh or frozen blueberries: both work, handled differently

Fresh berries are the first choice for this recipe: they go in whole, hold their shape better in the early part of the bake, and bleed their colour in streaks rather than flooding the whole batch. Frozen blueberries work too, and in one way they are better behaved — a frozen berry that has been tossed in a spoonful of flour or spare oats before it goes in bleeds noticeably less, because the dry coating catches the first juice. What frozen berries must not do is defrost in the mixture: a thawed blueberry is a soft, leaky thing that collapses the moment it is stirred. Straight from the freezer, coated, and into the pan at the last moment is the way.

Whichever you use, the handling rule is the same: the berries go in last, after the oats are fully coated, and the stirring from that point is folding, not mixing — a few slow turns of the spoon, just enough to spread them through. Every extra stir breaks more skins and turns the mixture a uniform mauve before it even reaches the tin. A few broken berries are unavoidable and fine; the aim is streaks, not soup.

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper and a large pan. The 200g of berries is roughly one standard supermarket punnet; if your punnet is 150g, the recipe still works with a slightly plainer bar, and if it is 250g, expect a softer set and use the longer end of the bake. Check the berries over as you tip them out — one mouldy or burst berry in the punnet will flavour and stain far beyond its size, and it takes seconds to pick them over.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides for lifting the slab out later — the paper matters more with this batch than with the classic, because berry juice can glue a slab to a bare tin.
  2. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado in a large pan over a low heat. Stir until everything has melted into one smooth liquid. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil — boiled syrup sets like toffee and makes hard bars.
  3. Add the oats and salt. Tip in 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt. Stir until every oat is coated and shiny and there are no dry patches hiding at the bottom of the pan.
  4. Fold in the blueberries, gently and last. Add 200g of fresh blueberries and fold them through with a few slow turns of the spoon — just enough to spread them through the mixture, no more. Some will break; that is fine. The mixture will streak purple where they have burst. If you prefer your berries sitting in the top of the bar rather than through it, there is an alternative: press the plain mixture into the tin first, scatter the berries over the surface and press them in lightly with the back of the spoon. Both work; the folded version spreads the fruit and colour through every bar, the scattered version keeps the berries nearer whole.
  5. Press into the tin. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working into the corners but not crushing down so hard that you burst every berry on the surface. Firm pressing is still what stops the bars crumbling later; it just wants a slightly lighter hand than the plain classic once the fruit is in.
  6. Bake for 30–35 minutes, checking the centre. The top should be golden where the oat mixture shows, deep purple where the berries have burst, and the centre should look set rather than wet and glistening. Berry juice keeps the middle looking softer than the classic at the same point; if it still looks wet, give it a few minutes longer and check again. The edges, where there is less fruit, are your best guide to how the base itself is doing.
  7. Score while warm. About 5 minutes after the tin comes out, mark the portion lines with a table knife — 12 bars — while the top is soft enough not to tear. Cut around berries where you can rather than through them, and the lines will stay cleaner.
  8. Cool completely in the tin. Leave the slab until it is cold and set before cutting. This matters more here than with any plain batch: a warm blueberry flapjack has juice where set syrup should be, and it will fall apart if it is cut early. An hour at least, and two is better.
  9. Lift out and cut. Use the paper overhang to lift the cold slab onto a board and cut through along the scored lines with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down. Expect a softer, jammier bar than the classic, with whole berries in some bites and purple streaks through all of them.

Why these bars set softer, and how to keep them from going soggy

The moisture maths is simple: 200g of blueberries bring well over 150g of water with them, locked inside skins that the oven then breaks open. That water has to go somewhere, and where it goes is into the oats around each berry. Near a burst berry the mixture stays jammy; in the middle of the slab, where the heat arrives last, it stays softest of all. This is the same reason dried-fruit flapjacks can turn in the tin if they are made with soaked fruit, and our guide to why fruit flapjacks turn soggy works through the moisture problem in full. The defences here are the ones in the method: berries in dry, a bake taken to a set-looking centre rather than a clock time alone, and a complete cool before cutting, because a blueberry slab finishes setting as it cools just as the classic does.

If fruit in flapjacks is your thing generally, our fruit flapjack recipe is the dried-fruit counterpart — mixed dried fruit in the same classic base, with a firmer set and a longer keeping life, because dried fruit brings flavour without bringing water. Fresh blueberries are the opposite bargain: the best flavour and colour of any fruit version, paid for with softness and a shorter prime. Both are worth making; they are just different bars.

Storing blueberry flapjacks

Keep the cut bars in an airtight tin at room temperature with baking paper between the layers — berry juice makes the surfaces tackier than the classic's, and the paper stops the bars welding themselves together. They keep about 5 days, though honestly they are at their best in the first two or three, before the moisture from the berries has spread evenly through the whole bar and softened it throughout. They freeze well — wrap individually, seal in a bag or box and use within 3 months, defrosting at room temperature in the wrapping. Our guides to how to store flapjacks and freezing flapjacks cover the detail.
