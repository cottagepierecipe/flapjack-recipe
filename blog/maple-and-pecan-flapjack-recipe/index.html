Maple and Pecan Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

A maple and pecan flapjack swaps the golden syrup in the classic base for maple syrup, measure for measure, cuts the sugar back a little, and folds chopped pecans through the oats. Maple syrup is thinner than golden syrup, so the mixture looks wetter and the set is softer — press it well, cool it fully, and the bars slice into a soft, nutty, distinctly maple bake.

The short answer: Melt 175g butter, 175g maple syrup and 150g light muscovado sugar in a pan, stir in 300g porridge oats, 100g chopped pecans and a pinch of salt, press firmly into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 30–35 minutes. Cool completely in the tin — these bars set softer than the classic — and cut into 12.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Maple and pecan flapjacks: maple in place of the golden syrup

The classic flapjack on this site is built on golden syrup, and golden syrup is hard to beat as a glue — thick, mild and reliable. Maple syrup plays a different game. It brings a flavour of its own, woody and almost smoky at the edges, that golden syrup never attempts, and pecans are its oldest partner: the two have been baked together in North America for as long as pecan pie has existed. This recipe puts that pairing into the British oat bar. The base is our classic flapjack recipe adjusted in three places: maple syrup in place of golden syrup, 25g less sugar because the pecans and maple carry sweetness differently, and 50g of the oats swapped for chopped pecans.

We already have a maple syrup flapjack on the site, and it is worth saying plainly how this one differs, because they are not the same bar twice. That post is the straight swap — the classic recipe with maple syrup and nothing else changed, the one to make if you want to taste what maple alone does to a flapjack. This one is a pecan recipe that happens to use maple: the nuts are folded through the mixture, the sugar is lower, and the finished bar is softer, nuttier and less purely sweet. Make the first to learn the swap; make this one when you want the full maple-and-pecan effect.

Maple syrup is thinner than golden syrup: what that changes

Pour the two syrups side by side and the difference is obvious. Golden syrup barely moves; it spoons in ribbons and holds them. Maple syrup pours like a thin honey. That thinness has two consequences in a flapjack, and both are manageable as long as you expect them. First, the raw mixture looks wetter than the classic — glossier, looser, more inclined to slump in the tin. It is not a mistake and it does not need more oats; the liquid is the full measure doing its job, and adding oats to compensate gives you a dry, heavy bar. Second, the set is softer. Maple syrup contains more water than golden syrup, and the finished bars stay tender where the classic is firm and chewy.

Two habits handle both points. Press the mixture into the tin really firmly — firmer than feels necessary — because a well-compacted slab holds together even with a softer set. And cool the slab completely in the tin before you cut it, right down to room temperature, however long that takes. A maple flapjack cut warm will bend and tear; the same slab cut cold slices cleanly. The comparison below sets the two syrups side by side so the trade-offs are clear before you start.

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper and a large pan for the oat mixture. On the syrup: buy real maple syrup, the kind whose ingredient list is just maple syrup. Maple-flavoured syrup is a different product — usually a sugar syrup with flavouring — and it behaves and tastes like the golden syrup it is imitating, only thinner. The whole point of this recipe is the genuine flavour, and the genuine article is what the quantities assume.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven, line the tin and chop the pecans. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides. Chop 100g of pecans roughly on a board and set them aside.
  2. Melt the butter, maple syrup and sugar. Put 175g butter, 175g maple syrup and 150g light muscovado in a large pan over a low heat. Stir until everything has melted into one smooth liquid. The mixture will look thinner than the classic melt — that is the maple syrup, and it is right. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil.
  3. Add the oats, pecans and salt. Take the pan off the heat and tip in 300g porridge oats, the chopped pecans and a pinch of salt. Stir until every oat is coated and glossy and the pecan pieces are spread through the mixture.
  4. Press into the tin — firmly. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down hard and evenly with the back of a spoon, working well into the corners. This recipe sets softer than the classic, so firm pressing matters more here than in any other flapjack on the site.
  5. Bake for 30–35 minutes. The top should be golden brown with the edges a shade darker, and the pecan pieces at the surface toasted. The centre should look set but will feel softer than a classic flapjack when pressed — it firms up as it cools, though it stays tender.
  6. Cool completely in the tin. Leave the slab in the tin until it is fully cold, at least an hour and longer if the kitchen is warm. Cutting a maple flapjack warm is the one reliable way to ruin it: the bars bend, tear and crumble at the edges.
  7. Lift out and cut into 12. Use the paper overhang to lift the cold slab onto a board and cut with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down in single, confident cuts rather than sawing.

The pecans toast as they bake: keep the pieces big enough to notice

Pecans are the softest and sweetest of the common baking nuts, and the oven improves them: raw pecans are mild and a little waxy, but baked in a syrup mixture they come out toasted, darker and properly nutty, with a slight crunch the oats cannot give. That is why the chop is rough. Pieces around half a pecan keep their identity in the finished bar; a fine chop disappears, and you would taste maple and sweetness with only a rumour of nut. It is also why there is no need to toast the pecans before they go in — 30–35 minutes in the bake does the job, and pre-toasted nuts would go too far and catch at the edges.

Walnuts are the obvious swap and they work at the same weight, making a slightly more bitter, more robust bar — the coffee-and-walnut combination's nut, doing a different job here. If you bake with nuts often, our mixed nut flapjack uses a blend of whatever you have. One caution that applies to pecans in particular: they stale quickly once a bag is open, and a stale pecan tastes flat and oily. Taste one before it goes in the pan; if the bag has been open a while, the fridge or freezer is the better home for the next one.

Storing maple and pecan flapjacks: about 5 days in an airtight tin

Keep the cut bars in an airtight tin at room temperature, with baking paper between the layers. They keep about 5 days, and because the set is softer than the classic, they stay tender for most of that time rather than firming up. Keep the tin out of a warm spot — a soft-set bar in a hot kitchen slumps at the edges. The bars freeze well for up to 3 months, wrapped individually and sealed; defrost in the wrapping at room temperature. The general rules are in our guide to how to store flapjacks and how long they last.

Questions people ask

It will make a flapjack, but not this one. Maple-flavoured syrup is generally a sugar or glucose syrup with flavouring added; it is thin like maple syrup but tastes mostly of sugar, so you get the softer set without the flavour that justifies it. Real maple syrup — check that the ingredient list says only maple syrup — is what the recipe is built on, and a bottle keeps for months in the fridge once opened.

Because maple syrup is much thinner than golden syrup and holds more water. The loose, glossy mixture is correct — do not add extra oats to stiffen it, or the bars come out dry and heavy to compensate for a problem that was never there. You can scatter a handful of pecan halves over the pressed mixture before baking, and they toast attractively — but keep most of the 100g folded through, or the bars themselves taste of plain maple flapjack with a nut garnish. A good compromise is three-quarters through the mixture and a few halves pressed into the top. Pieces on top scorch faster than pieces inside, so check the edges from about 25 minutes.

Almost always one of two things: the slab was cut before it was fully cold, or the mixture was not pressed firmly enough. A maple flapjack's set is soft by design, and it needs both a hard press and a complete cool to slice cleanly. If a batch has crumbled, chill the slab for half an hour and cut it cold with a large sharp knife in single downward presses — it usually rescues the rest of the tray.
