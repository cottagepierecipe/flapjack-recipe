How to Make Flapjacks in a Silicone Mould

By Michael

A silicone mould turns flapjack mixture into ready-portioned little bars without a knife ever being involved — mini loaves, muffins, hearts, whatever shape the mould holds. It is also genuinely different to bake in: nothing sticks, nothing browns much, and the mould itself is too floppy to carry. Used the way it wants to be used, it is the easiest portioning method there is.

The short answer: Yes — press the classic mixture firmly into the cups of a silicone mould, with no lining and usually no greasing, stand the mould on a rigid baking tray before you fill it, and bake at the usual 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Small individual portions cook faster than a slab, so start checking a few minutes earlier than the slab's time and take them out when the tops are golden and just set. Cool the portions in the mould, then flex the silicone to pop them out. Expect softer, paler edges than a metal tin gives — that is silicone's trade for its easy release.

What silicone changes: release, browning and shape

Silicone is a poor conductor of heat, and nearly everything about baking in it follows from that. A metal tin rushes oven heat into the edges of a flapjack, so the edges colour and firm ahead of the centre. A silicone mould insulates: heat reaches the mixture slowly and gently, the outside of each little bar never gets the fierce contact that browns and crisps, and the finished portions come out pale-edged, soft-sided and evenly tender all through. For some bakes that is a loss. For flapjacks — which plenty of people prefer chewy anyway — it is a fair swap, and the compensation is the release: a cooled flapjack portion flexes out of silicone with no lining, no greasing and no knife work, its shape as crisp as the mould that made it.

The classic mixture itself needs no changes. Our classic flapjack recipe — 175g butter, 175g golden syrup, 175g light muscovado sugar to 350g porridge oats — presses into moulds exactly as it presses into a tin. One full batch fills roughly a dozen muffin-sized cups or a couple of mini-loaf trays, depending on the mould; the arithmetic is the same as any tin, and our tin sizes guide covers the area thinking if you want to scale a half batch for a small mould.

No lining, no paper — and pressing still matters

The pleasure of silicone is what you can skip: no paper circle, no overhang sling, no greasing for plain shapes. The syrup mixture releases from the smooth surface once cold. Two qualifications. First, intricate moulds — deep ridges, sharp lettering, fine detail — can hold onto a syrupy mixture in the crevices; a whisper of oil brushed into a detailed mould is cheap insurance there, and our guide to why flapjacks stick explains the glue-like behaviour of hot syrup that makes crevices risky. Second, pressing matters more in a mould, not less. Each cup must be filled firmly and evenly, corners and edges worked with the back of a spoon or your fingertips, because a loosely packed cup bakes into a loosely held portion that crumbles as it flexes out. Fill each cup level — the mixture does not rise or spread much, so the surface you press is the surface you serve.

The tray rule: the mould goes on a tray before it is filled

A filled silicone mould is a floppy, heavy, wobbling thing with no safe way to grip it, and a flapjack mixture is dense. The rule that prevents the one disaster this method has is simple: put the empty mould on a rigid baking tray first, fill it standing on the tray, and move the tray — into the oven, out of the oven, across the kitchen — never the mould alone. A mould carried by its edges sags in the middle, and sagging spills mixture, tips portions into each other, and in the worst case folds a hot mould over your hand. The tray comes out of the oven with the mould still on it and goes straight onto a heatproof surface, where the portions cool undisturbed. Treat the tray as part of the mould from start to finish and silicone never gives you a moment's trouble.

Small portions cook faster — check early

A slab of flapjack is one thick piece; a mould of portions is a dozen small ones, and small pieces heat through sooner. As guidance, start checking individual portions a good few minutes before the classic slab's 30–35 minutes is up — smaller cups sooner, deep mini-loaf shapes closer to the full time — and judge them by the same signs as any flapjack: tops golden, edges looking set, centres just set and still slightly soft, because they firm as they cool. Our guide to telling when flapjacks are done works through those cues. Do not wait for browned edges in silicone; they are not coming. A portion judged by slab rules stays in too long and dries, which is how silicone gets its unfair reputation for pale, tough little bars. Pale, yes. Tough only if they were left in waiting for a colour that silicone never gives.

Cooling and release: patience, then a flex

Turn-out discipline is the same as for a slab, only more so: portions come out of the mould when they are cold, not before. A warm flapjack portion is soft syrup and oats with no structure of its own, and flexing it out of its cup warm tears it or leaves half behind in the mould. Let the tray sit until the portions are fully cool and firm — the mould insulates, so this takes a little longer than you expect — then turn the mould over and flex it gently, pressing the base of each cup to pop the portion out. They release whole, with the mould's pattern on them. If a detailed mould does grip in one crevice, ease that corner with the tip of a knife rather than pulling; the next batch wants the whisper of oil mentioned earlier.

Choosing a shape: what each mould does best

Mini loaf moulds are the closest thing to a classic bar — a small loaf shape that slices or serves whole, and the best choice if the portions are for lunchboxes. Muffin and cupcake moulds give round, deep portions that eat more like a cake than a bar; they suit softer mixtures and are the easiest shape for small children to hold. Shallow novelty shapes — hearts, stars, gingerbread figures — are the gift and party option, and their thinness means they bake quickest and driest, so they reward the earliest checking of all. Deep, detailed moulds look wonderful and grip the hardest, as the release section warned: save the cathedral-window mould for firmer bakes, and let flapjacks keep the simpler shapes they release from cleanly.

Looking after the mould, and storing the portions

Syrup leaves its mark on silicone over time — a faint tackiness and darkening in the cups is normal service, not damage. Wash moulds in hot soapy water, soak a stubborn syrup film rather than scouring it, and dry them thoroughly before storing; store them flat or nested, not folded, as a permanent crease weakens the spot it sits on. The portions themselves store exactly as bars do: an airtight tin at room temperature for about 5 days, or wrapped and frozen for up to 3 months — their ready-made portioning makes them natural freezer stock, each one defrosting on its own. Our guide to how to store flapjacks covers the details.
