Raspberry Flapjack Recipe

By Michael

Fresh raspberries do something in a flapjack that dried fruit never can: they collapse in the oven into soft, jammy pockets, and their sharpness cuts straight through the butter and golden syrup. This version keeps the tested classic exactly as it is and folds 175g of fresh raspberries through it — half mixed gently into the oats, half pressed over the top — and it is honest about the price. Raspberries are wet, fragile fruit, so these bars set softer than the classic and need real patience while they cool.

The short answer: Melt 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado sugar. Stir in 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt, then fold in half of 175g fresh raspberries as gently as you can. Press into a lined 20cm square tin, press the remaining berries over the top, and bake at 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 35–40 minutes. Cool completely in the tin, then cut into 12 bars.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Raspberry flapjacks: the classic recipe with jammy pockets

A plain flapjack is sweet all the way through, and that is its charm and its limit. Raspberries break the sweetness up. In the heat of the oven the berries soften and slump, their juice bleeding a little into the oats around them, and when the bars cool you get two textures in one bite: the chewy, caramel-bound oats of the classic, and then a soft pocket of fruit that tastes almost like jam but sharper, because nothing has been added to sweeten it. The contrast is the whole point — the fruit tastes more raspberry-like, not less, for sitting inside something this sweet.

The base here is our classic flapjack recipe, the version we have kitchen-tested: equal weights of butter, golden syrup and light muscovado sugar, twice that weight of porridge oats, a pinch of salt, baked in a lined 20cm square tin. That skeleton does not change. The same syrup mixture coats the oats and sets as it cools, and the same firm pressing holds the slab together. What changes is only what the flavour needs — the fruit itself, how it is added, and a little more time and care at the baking and cooling stages to deal with the water the berries bring with them.

Raspberries are wetter and more fragile than blueberries

It is tempting to treat all soft fruit as interchangeable in baking, and raspberries punish that assumption. A raspberry is not a solid berry. It is a cluster of tiny juice-filled sacs — drupelets — held loosely around a hollow core, which is why a ripe raspberry crushes between finger and thumb with almost no pressure. Blueberries, by contrast, sit inside a tough skin that keeps their juice contained until they burst late in a bake. Fold blueberries through a flapjack mixture and most of them survive as whole fruit. Fold raspberries through and a good number of them break down on contact with the warm oats, streaking the mixture pink and releasing their juice into it.

That juice is the honest difficulty with this recipe. Extra liquid in a flapjack mixture means a softer set, and a slab that feels fragile until it is completely cold. It does not mean a failed bake — raspberry flapjacks are meant to be softer and moister than the classic — but it does mean the usual rules matter more, not less: weigh the fruit rather than guessing, do not be tempted to add extra, and give the slab its full cooling time before you cut. Our guide to why fruit flapjacks turn soggy explains the moisture problem in full, and most of it comes down to exactly this: fresh fruit gives up water as it bakes, and the oats around it have to absorb the lot.

Fresh, frozen or freeze-dried raspberries

Three forms of the fruit turn up in shops, and they behave differently enough in a flapjack that the choice matters. Fresh is what this recipe is written for. Frozen raspberries are picked ripe and freeze well, but freezing breaks their cell walls, so they leak far more juice as they thaw and bake — in a mixture that is already coping with fresh-fruit moisture, that pushes the set softer still. Freeze-dried raspberries are the opposite extreme: the water has been removed entirely, leaving intense, tart, crunchy little pieces that add flavour and colour while asking nothing of the bake at all.

Firmer fruit behaves differently again: cherries hold their shape in a way raspberries never will, which is why our cherry flapjack recipe can carry its fruit in bigger pieces. With raspberries, small and scattered beats big and whole.

Freeze-dried raspberries: the intense, dry alternative

Freeze-dried raspberries deserve a section of their own because they solve this recipe's only real problem. Sold in small tubs in the baking aisle, they are whole or crumbled raspberries with the water removed, and they taste startlingly strong — tart enough to make your mouth water dry. Folded through the oat mixture they give you the raspberry flavour and the pink flecks without a drop of extra liquid, so the bars press, bake and set exactly like the classic, in the classic's time. Nothing about the bake changes.

The trade-off is texture. You lose the jammy pocket entirely; what you get instead is a chewy bar with crunchy, sherbet-like bursts of fruit. It is a different flapjack, not a lesser one, and it is the version to make if you want raspberry bars that travel well in a lunchbox or keep their firmness all week. A middle course works too: make the fresh recipe as written, and scatter a crumbled tablespoon of freeze-dried berries over the top with the fresh ones, for colour and an extra hit of tartness where the bake has softened the fresh fruit most.

You will also need a 20cm square tin lined with baking paper and a large pan. Check the raspberries over before you start and pick out any that are mouldy or collapsing — one soft, fermenting berry taints everything near it, and in a warm oat mixture there is nowhere for that flavour to hide. If the berries are dusty, leave them unwashed if you can; washing adds surface water to fruit that is already bringing plenty. If you must wash them, do it early and let them dry spread out on kitchen paper.

The method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Set the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a little overhang at two sides for lifting the slab out later.
  2. Pick over the raspberries. Tip 175g of raspberries onto a plate and remove any that are mouldy, mushy or pale. Keep them dry. Set the firmest, best-looking half to one side for the top.
  3. Melt the butter, syrup and sugar. Put 175g butter, 175g golden syrup and 175g light muscovado in a large pan over a low heat. Stir until everything has melted into one smooth liquid. Keep the heat gentle and never let it boil — boiled syrup sets like toffee and makes hard bars.
  4. Add the oats and salt. Take the pan off the heat and tip in 350g porridge oats and a pinch of salt. Stir until every oat is coated and shiny and there are no dry patches at the bottom of the pan.
  5. Fold in half the raspberries, gently. Add roughly half the berries and fold them through with a spoon in a few slow strokes — no more than it takes to distribute them. Some will break and streak the mixture pink; that is expected. Crushing them to a purée is not: you want recognisable fruit left.
  6. Press into the tin. Spoon the mixture into the lined tin and press down firmly and evenly with the back of a spoon, working well into the corners. Firm pressing matters even more with a wet mixture, because the syrup alone cannot hold a loosely packed slab together.
  7. Press the rest of the berries over the top. Scatter the reserved raspberries across the surface and press each one lightly into the mixture with the back of the spoon, so they sit half-sunk in the oats rather than resting on top where they would dry out and fall off after baking.
  8. Bake for 35–40 minutes. That is a few minutes longer than the classic, to drive off some of the berry juice. The edges should be golden brown, the top berries slightly darkened and shrivelled at the edges, and the centre set but still a little soft to a fingertip — it firms as it cools, and with this much fruit it firms later than the classic does.
  9. Score while warm. About 5 minutes after the tin comes out, mark the portion lines with a table knife — 12 bars — while the top is soft enough not to crack. Cut around the top berries where you can rather than straight through them.
  10. Cool completely in the tin. Leave the slab until it is fully cold and set before cutting — at least 2 hours, and longer does no harm. A raspberry slab cut warm will slump and tear, because the fruit pockets are still soft even when the oats feel set.
  11. Lift out and cut. Use the paper overhang to lift the cold slab onto a board and cut through along the scored lines with a large sharp knife, pressing straight down. If the bars still feel delicate, half an hour in the fridge firms them without drying them out.

Keeping raspberry flapjacks from going soggy

Most disappointing raspberry flapjacks fail for one of three reasons, and all three are about water. The first is too much fruit: the classic's syrup ratio binds 350g of oats comfortably, but every extra handful of berries adds juice without adding anything to hold it, and past about 200g the slab stops setting properly. The second is wet fruit — berries washed at the last minute, or frozen berries tipped in with their thawing juices. The third is impatience: a slab taken out of the tin, or worse cut up, while the middle is still warm will tear at the fruit pockets and then weep juice into the storage tin, softening every bar it touches.

Store the cut bars only once they are completely cold, and put baking paper between the layers so the jammy tops do not stick to the bars above them. If your kitchen is warm, these keep better in the fridge than the classic does — the fruit pockets stay firmer and the bars cut more cleanly — though they are best eaten at room temperature. If a batch does come out softer than you like, our guide to why flapjacks fall apart works through the binding problems in order, and nine times out of ten with fruit the answer is cooling time, not the recipe. For a drier, firmer fruit bar altogether, our fruit flapjack recipe builds on dried fruit instead, which brings sweetness without the water.

Storing raspberry flapjacks

Keep the cut bars in an airtight tin at room temperature with baking paper between the layers. They keep about 5 days, though the fruit pockets soften the bars gradually, so they are at their best in the first two or three days. They freeze well — wrap the bars individually, seal them in a bag or box and use within 3 months, defrosting at room temperature in the wrapping so the fruit stays where it belongs. There is more detail in our guides to how to store flapjacks and whether you can freeze flapjacks.

Work through three checks. First, the fruit weight: more than about 200g will keep the centre wet no matter how long you bake. Second, the bake: this version needs its few extra minutes beyond the classic, and an oven running cool will leave the middle underdone.
