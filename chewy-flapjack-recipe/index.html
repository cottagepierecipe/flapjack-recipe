Chewy Flapjack Recipe with Condensed Milk

By Michael

A chewy flapjack needs enough syrupy binding, a firm press and a short bake that leaves the centre soft. This version uses sweetened condensed milk as well as golden syrup, then comes out of the oven while the middle still looks slightly underdone; it sets into soft, bendy bars as it cools.

The short answer: Melt 150g butter with 100g light brown sugar, 3 tablespoons golden syrup and 150g sweetened condensed milk. Stir in 325g porridge oats, press the mixture firmly into a lined 20cm square tin and bake at 170°C (150°C fan, Gas Mark 3) for 20–22 minutes. Cool completely in the tin before cutting into 12 bars.

The chewy flapjack recipe

  • Prep: 10 min
  • Cook: 22 min
  • Total: 32 min
  • Makes: 12 bars

Oven: 170°C (150°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Tin: 20cm square, lined with baking paper. The total time covers preparation and baking; leave the slab to cool and set in the tin for at least 1 hour before cutting.

Draft recipe: worked out from cross-checked published recipes and not yet kitchen-tested by us. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Heat the oven to 170°C (150°C fan, Gas Mark 3). Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a little paper overhanging two opposite sides. The overhang works as handles later and makes the cooled slab much easier to lift out without breaking the corners.

  2. Melt the butter mixture gently. Put the butter, light brown sugar, golden syrup and condensed milk in a large saucepan. Warm over a low heat, stirring often, until the butter has melted and the sugar no longer feels gritty against the spoon. Take the pan off the heat as soon as the mixture looks smooth and glossy. Do not let it boil: boiling drives off water, concentrates the sugar and is one of the most reliable ways to turn a chewy flapjack hard.

  3. Stir in the oats and salt. Add the porridge oats and salt to the warm mixture. Stir slowly and thoroughly, turning the oats over from the bottom of the pan, until every flake looks coated and shiny. Dry pockets matter. An uncoated spoonful of oats has nothing sticky holding it to its neighbours, so it becomes a crumbly patch in the finished bar.

  4. Tip the mixture into the tin. Scrape the mixture into the lined tin and spread it into the corners. Use the back of a spoon or a spatula to level the surface. Work it right to the edges rather than leaving the centre piled high; an even thickness bakes evenly and gives bars with the same texture from edge to middle.

  5. Press firmly, then press again. Press the surface down hard with the back of a spoon, the base of a glass or a flat spatula. Pay particular attention to the corners, which are often the loosest parts. Smooth the top, then press firmly once more. This second press pushes out air gaps and packs the coated oats close enough for the syrup mixture to bind them as the slab cools.

  6. Bake while the centre is still soft. Bake on the middle shelf for 20–22 minutes. The edges should look golden and set, while the centre still looks soft and may move very slightly if the tin is nudged. That soft-looking middle is the point: it firms up during cooling. Baking until the whole surface looks dry and deeply coloured usually takes the bars past chewy and into firm or hard.

  7. Cool completely in the tin. Put the tin on a wire rack and leave the flapjack to cool completely, for at least 1 hour. The condensed milk and syrup mixture becomes firmer as its temperature falls, and this cooling period is part of the recipe rather than a waiting extra. Cutting too soon can make even a well-made slab tear, bend and fall apart.

  8. Lift out and cut. Lift the cooled slab out by the paper. Use a large, sharp knife and press straight down to cut it into 12 bars. Avoid sawing backwards and forwards, which can drag at the edges. If the knife sticks, wipe it clean between cuts. The centre bars should feel soft and slightly bendy rather than crisp.

What makes flapjack chewy?

Chew is not produced by a single magic ingredient. It comes from four things working together: the amount of liquid sugar binding, the kind and proportion of oats, the pressure used before baking, and the point at which the tin leaves the oven. Condensed milk helps because it brings sweetness, milk solids and body in a thick liquid that coats the oats and sets softly. It gives more margin than a lean syrup mixture, but it cannot rescue a slab that has been boiled hard or baked for too long.

Porridge oats are the right choice for this texture because their smaller flakes pack closely and present plenty of surface for the mixture to coat. Jumbo oats make a more open, rustic slab, but the gaps between the larger flakes can make the bars more fragile. Pressing is the third part of the system: a loose layer has air spaces where a firm layer has contact. Finally, cooling completes the set. A flapjack that looks perfect and sliceable the moment it leaves the oven will usually be hard once cold; one that looks a fraction too soft often lands exactly where you want it.

The chew dial: choose your finish

Use the same recipe and tin, then adjust the bake by only a few minutes. Judge the edges and centre as well as the clock, because ovens vary and a dark metal tin can colour the base faster than a pale one. The condensed milk mixture also continues to firm as it cools, so the hot slab always feels softer than the finished bar.

Crunchy rather than chewyBake for about 25–27 minutesThe whole slab is deeper golden and feels firm; expect snap at the edges, not a bendy centre.

If your first batch is firmer than expected, reduce the next bake by 2 minutes before changing the ingredients. Time is the easiest variable to correct. If the first batch is still loose after complete cooling, bake the next one for 2 minutes longer; do not judge either result while the slab is warm.

Why flapjacks go hard, crumble or stay soggy

Most texture problems can be traced to a small number of causes. Boiling the sugar mixture changes it before the oats are even added. Overbaking dries the slab. Too little pressing leaves the oats insufficiently packed. Cutting while hot interrupts the setting process. The table below separates the likely causes so you can change one thing at a time and learn what your oven does to this recipe.

Can you make chewy flapjacks without condensed milk?

Yes, but the method becomes less forgiving. The classic flapjack recipe uses butter, brown sugar and golden syrup without condensed milk and can still be chewy if the syrup is not boiled, the oats are pressed firmly and the bake stops while the centre is soft. What condensed milk adds is body and a wider margin for error. If you leave it out without replacing its weight, there is less mixture coating the oats and the slab is more likely to crumble. A closer swap needs the missing liquid and sugar made up carefully; simply deleting the tin from this ingredient list is not the same recipe.

For a firmer, more traditional result, compare the classic on the homepage and the guidance on the best flapjack recipe page. For this soft version, stay with porridge oats and make one change at a time. It is tempting to add chocolate chips, dried fruit and extra oats all at once, but dry additions change the ratio of coating to oats. Every handful of extra dry ingredient makes binding harder unless the basic slab is already working.

Keeping chewy flapjacks soft

Cool the bars completely, cut them, then store them in an airtight container at room temperature. Separate layers with baking paper if the tops are sticky. Keep the container away from the heat of the hob and out of direct sun. Chewy flapjacks are at their best soon after baking because exposure to air gradually firms and dries the cut edges; an airtight container slows that change. Avoid storing them beside strongly flavoured foods, as buttery bars can pick up other smells.

Do not put warm bars into the container. Trapped steam can make the tops damp and undo the neat surface you worked to create. If a batch has firmed up after a few days, a very brief warm-through can soften a piece for immediate serving, but let the rest cool before returning them to storage. For the wider keeping guidance, including freezing and packing bars for later, see the questions on our flapjack FAQs page and the storage-led variations on the flapjack variations hub.
