Classic British Flapjack Recipe with Golden Syrup

A proper British flapjack: porridge oats, butter, light muscovado sugar and golden syrup, baked until the edges are golden and the middle stays chewy. Four main ingredients, plus a pinch of salt — one tin, twelve bars.

This website is devoted to that one bake: the classic recipe, the variations worth knowing, and the small details that decide whether yours turn out chewy and neat or hard and crumbly. Exact quantities, times and oven temperatures are in the full recipe further down this page — read on to understand the bake first, or jump straight to it.

What is a flapjack?

In Britain, a flapjack is a baked oat bar: rolled oats bound with butter, sugar and golden syrup, pressed into a flat tin, baked and cut into squares or rectangles. It is the bake of school fairs, lunchboxes and walking trips — sweet, chewy and sturdy enough to travel.

If you have arrived here from North America, one warning saves a lot of confusion: in the United States and Canada, "flapjack" means a pancake. The two could hardly be more different. Nothing on this page is a pancake — there is no batter, no frying and no maple syrup at the table.

PointBritish flapjackAmerican flapjack
What it isA baked oat barA pancake
Main ingredientPorridge oatsFlour batter
How it is cookedBaked in a flat tinFried in a pan
How it is eatenCold, cut into squaresHot, stacked, with syrup poured over

The word is old — it was recorded centuries ago for a flat tart, and Shakespeare mentions flap-jacks in Pericles, Prince of Tyre — but the oat-based bar itself is a twentieth-century bake, first recorded under this name in 1935 and built on golden syrup, which dates from the 1880s. The classic recipe below is the plain, traditional version: no egg, no flour, nothing fancy.

The classic flapjack recipe

  • Prep: 10 min
  • Cook: 30–35 min
  • Total: about 45 min, plus cooling
  • Makes: 12 bars

Oven: 160°C / 140°C fan / Gas Mark 3. Tin: 20cm square, lined with baking paper.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Adjust servings (ingredient quantities only)
Scaling changes ingredient quantities only. It does not change oven temperatures, cooking times or the tin size.

Equipment you need

  • A 20cm square baking tin.
  • Baking paper (parchment) to line it, with overhang on two sides.
  • A large saucepan for the melting, and a big bowl for the oats.
  • A wooden spoon, kitchen scales and a wire rack.

The tin size matters. Too large, and the mixture spreads thin and bakes hard and dry; too small, and the slab is deep and stays soggy in the middle. In a 20cm tin this mixture sits about 2cm deep, which is what gives the classic its chew.

Method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Heat the oven to 160°C (140°C fan, Gas Mark 3) before you do anything else, so it is ready the moment the mixture is. Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper: cut one sheet a little wider than the tin, press it into the corners, and leave the paper hanging over two opposite sides. Those overhangs are the handles you will lift the whole slab out with later, so leave them long — do not trim them flush. If the paper will not sit flat, scrunch it up in your hands, open it out and press it in again; it settles much better the second time.

    Cue: the paper overhangs are your handles — leave them long.

  2. Weigh everything out. Weigh the butter, golden syrup and sugar straight into a large saucepan — one big enough to hold the oats later as well, because everything gets mixed in this same pan. Weigh the oats into a big bowl with the pinch of salt on top. A small tip for the syrup: weigh it directly into the pan on the scales, or dip the spoon in hot water first, and it will slide off instead of staying stuck to the spoon. Have everything weighed and within reach before the pan goes on the heat — once the melting starts, flapjacks move fast and there is no spare minute for weighing.

    Cue: once the pan is on the heat there is no time to weigh — everything ready first.

  3. Melt, but never boil. Put the pan on a low heat and stir slowly with a wooden spoon, moving the butter around so it melts evenly. Over about 3 to 4 minutes the butter melts, the sugar dissolves and the mixture turns into a smooth, glossy, golden liquid. Drag the spoon across the bottom of the pan: if it still feels gritty, there is undissolved sugar left, so give it another half minute. The moment it is smooth, take the pan off the heat — and do not let it bubble or boil. Boiled syrup sets like toffee, and toffee is what makes finished bars hard instead of chewy. If it starts to bubble at the edges, lift the pan off the heat and stir; that alone is enough to save it.

    Cue: glossy and smooth, with no bubbles — boiling is what makes bars set hard.

  4. Stir in the oats. Take the pan off the heat and tip in all the oats, with the pinch of salt. Stir slowly from the bottom up, turning the mixture over on itself again and again — the dry oats hiding at the very bottom are the ones that cause crumbs later, so scrape right into the edges of the pan. Keep going until every single oat is coated and shiny and the mixture looks even, with no pale, dry patches anywhere. It will feel thick, heavy and sticky on the spoon; that is exactly right. And if it looks loose and sloppy, do not add more oats — the balance is deliberate, and it firms up as it cools and sets.

    Cue: no dry, pale oats left anywhere — every oat coated and shiny.

  5. Tip it into the tin. Scrape all the mixture into the lined tin, getting as much out of the pan as you can — a flexible spatula earns its keep here. Push the mixture into the corners first, then spread it outwards with the back of a spoon until it sits level and even across the whole tin, about 2cm deep everywhere. Take a moment over the corners and edges: thin patches bake hard and dark, and a thick middle stays soggy — an even layer now is what gives you an even bake later.

    Cue: an even layer, right into the corners.

  6. Press down firmly. Now press the surface down — hard. Work over the whole tin with the back of a spoon or the flat base of a glass, pushing the mixture down and out into the corners until the top is flat, tight and even, with no loose oats sitting on the surface. Press firmer than feels natural: you are squeezing out the air pockets, and it is this pressing — not anything in the ingredients — that holds the baked bars together. A loosely pressed flapjack crumbles when you cut it; a firmly pressed one slices into clean, sturdy bars.

    Cue: firmer than feels natural — this pressing is the difference between bars and crumbs.

  7. Bake until the edges are golden. Slide the tin onto the middle shelf and bake for 30 to 35 minutes. Judge it by the edges first: they should be turning golden brown and just starting to pull away from the paper. The centre should still look paler, softer and slightly underdone — that is not a mistake. The slab keeps cooking gently in the hot tin and firms up as it cools, so a centre that looks fully baked in the oven will be hard by the time it is cold. If the edges colour much faster than the middle, your oven heats unevenly — turn the tin round halfway through, this time and next time.

    Cue: golden edges and a centre that still looks slightly soft — it firms up as it cools.

  8. Score the portions while warm. Leave the slab in its tin for 5 minutes — no longer, because it must still be warm for this. Then take a table knife and score light lines across the top where the cuts will go: for twelve bars, that is three lines one way and two lines the other. Press the knife in just far enough to mark the surface; do not cut through to the bottom. Scoring now, while the slab is soft, is what gives you clean edges later — a cold slab cut without score lines tends to crack and shatter along the cut.

    Cue: light score lines now mean clean cuts later.

  9. Cool completely in the tin. Put the tin on a wire rack and leave the flapjack completely alone until it is cold right through and firmly set — about 1 hour. This is the step people rush, and it is the main reason bars fall apart: a warm flapjack has not finished setting, because the syrup holding it together is still soft. Do not lift it out, do not cut it, and do not be tempted by a warm corner. When the base of the tin feels cool against your hand, it is ready for the knife.

    Cue: completely cold and set — a warm flapjack has not finished.

  10. Lift out and cut. Lift the cold slab out of the tin using the paper overhangs and set it on a chopping board. With a large sharp knife, cut along the scored lines, pressing straight down in one firm movement — do not saw backwards and forwards, which drags at the edges and makes crumbs. If the knife gets sticky, wipe it on a damp cloth between cuts. You should have twelve neat, chewy bars with clean edges. And if one corner piece crumbles, it was almost certainly a loose corner back at the pressing stage — it will still taste exactly as good.

    Cue: press straight down with a long knife, and you get twelve neat bars.

Recipe notes. Judge the bake by the cues, not only by the clock — ovens vary by more than the dial says. The two mistakes that ruin flapjacks are boiling the melted mixture and leaving the tin in too long; both are covered in the tips further down this page.

The Classic Flapjack Ratio: Equal Butter, Syrup and Sugar, Twice the Oats

The classic flapjack ratio is easy to remember because three of the four main ingredients match: equal weights of butter, golden syrup and sugar, with twice that weight of oats — 175g, 175g, 175g and 350g. Each part does a job. The butter gives richness and helps the bars set firm when cold. The golden syrup is the glue: it coats every oat and sets into that chewy bite. The sugar dissolves into the syrup for sweetness and a slight caramel edge. The oats are the body — too few and the bars are greasy and floppy; too many and there is not enough syrup to bind them, so they crumble.

Once you know what each part does, the common failures explain themselves. Hard bars were usually boiled or overbaked — the syrup cooked too far. Crumbly bars were under-pressed, under-syruped or cut while warm. Greasy bars had too much butter for the oats. Every variation on this site starts from this ratio and changes one thing at a time, so you can see what the change does.

Flapjack Tips: Keep Your Bars Chewy — Not Hard, Not Crumbly

Do not boil the syrup mixture. Melt it gently and stop. Boiling drives the syrup towards toffee, and toffee sets hard — this is the single most common cause of flapjacks you could use as doorstops.

Press harder than you think. A loose, airy layer bakes into crumbs. A firmly pressed layer bakes into a bar. Work the corners as carefully as the middle.

Take it out soft. The bar sets as it cools, so a centre that looks slightly underdone in the oven is exactly right. Wait for a deep golden centre and you have baked a biscuit.

Cool before you cut. Score while warm by all means, but the knife only goes through once the slab is cold and set.

Storing Flapjacks: 5 Days in the Tin, 3 Months in the Freezer

Flapjacks are one of the best bakes for making ahead: they keep in an airtight tin at room temperature for up to 5 days and, if anything, soften and improve over the first day or two. There is no need to refrigerate them — the fridge dries them out. Keep the tin somewhere cool and out of direct sunlight, with a sheet of baking paper between layers if you stack them.

You can also freeze flapjacks. Wrap the cut bars individually or layer them with baking paper in a freezer box; they keep for up to 3 months. Defrost at room temperature for a couple of hours — no reheating needed. The uncooked mixture does not freeze well, so always bake first, then freeze.

Flapjack Tin and Temperature: A 20cm Tin at 160°C, Middle Shelf

The recipe is written for a 20cm square tin, which gives bars of a good thickness. A smaller tin makes thicker bars that need a few minutes longer; a larger tin makes thin, crisper bars that need a few minutes less and are easier to overbake. No square tin? A 20cm round tin or a small roasting tin of a similar area both work — judge by depth: you want the pressed mixture about 2cm thick.

Bake on the middle shelf, where the heat is most even. The temperature that matters most is restraint: flapjacks baked gently at 160°C stay chewy, while a hot oven races them towards hard. If your oven has no markings you trust, an inexpensive oven thermometer removes the guesswork.
