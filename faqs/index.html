Flapjack Questions, Answered

By Michael

The questions people ask most about British flapjacks — what they are, why they go hard or fall apart, how long they keep, and what you can swap. Every answer is short enough to use and honest about what we do not know.

Questions people ask

A baked oat bar: porridge oats bound with butter, sugar and golden syrup, pressed into a flat tin, baked and cut into squares or rectangles. It is a lunchbox and bake-sale staple — sweet, chewy and sturdy. The full classic version is our flapjack recipe on the homepage.

Geography. In Britain a flapjack is the oat bar described above. In the United States and Canada, "flapjack" is another word for a pancake — a fried batter cake. Same word, two completely different foods; everything on this website is the British oat bar.

Standard porridge oats (rolled oats) give the classic chewy texture and bind well. Jumbo oats make a chunkier, more crumbly bar; instant oats turn soft and pasty. If you only have jumbo oats, briefly blitz about a third of them so the syrup can hold the mix together.

Usually because the butter, syrup and sugar boiled while melting, or the bake ran too long or too hot. Boiled syrup sets like toffee. Melt gently, never boil, and take the tin out while the centre still looks soft — it firms up as it cools.

The three usual causes: cutting while warm, pressing the mixture too lightly, or too many oats for the syrup to coat. Press firmly, score while warm, and wait until the slab is completely cold before cutting. Our chewy flapjack recipe explains the binding in more depth.

Classic flapjacks keep for up to 5 days in an airtight tin at room temperature and often taste better on the second day. Banana and fresh-fruit versions keep for about 3 days because of their extra moisture. There is no need to refrigerate any of them — the fridge dries them out.

Yes. Freeze baked, cut bars in a box with baking paper between layers for up to 3 months, and defrost at room temperature for a couple of hours. Always bake first — the raw oat mixture does not freeze well.

Yes, swapped like for like by weight. Honey is runnier and browns faster, so check the bake a few minutes early, and remember honey is still a sugar nutritionally. Our honey flapjack recipe is built around the swap — and note that babies under 12 months must not have honey at all.

Golden syrup is made from sugar, water and a little citric acid — it contains no animal products, so it is suitable for vegans (honey is not; see our vegan flapjack recipe). It is also naturally gluten-free as an ingredient, though you should check the label of your own tin if you are cooking for someone with coeliac disease.

Not automatically. Oats themselves do not contain gluten, but most porridge oats are grown and milled alongside wheat, so standard oats are not safe for people with coeliac disease. Flapjacks made with certified gluten-free oats — and checked labels on every other ingredient — can be gluten-free. Always check your own packets.

The classic recipe contains neither. It is oats, butter, sugar, golden syrup and a pinch of salt — one of the simplest bakes there is. Some modern variations add flour for a cakier texture, but ours keep to the traditional make-up unless a page says otherwise.

It depends entirely on the recipe and the size of the bar, so we will not quote a made-up number. The honest way to work it out: add up the calories printed on the labels of the ingredients you actually used, then divide by the number of bars you cut. When a recipe on this site shows a figure, it will have been calculated that way from the tested recipe and labelled as a calculation.

Yes. Coconut oil or a dairy-free baking block can replace butter in the same weight; the bars set a little differently and coconut oil adds a faint coconut note unless you use the refined kind. The vegan flapjack recipe compares the options. Oil by itself (liquid) makes a softer, greasier bar because there is no milk solid and the fat never fully sets at room temperature.

Yes — honey is the closest swap, and maple syrup works too, though it is runnier and less sweet, so the bars set softer. Treacle gives a darker, more bitter bar used in smaller amounts. What you cannot do is simply leave the syrup out: it is the glue that holds the oats together.

Most recipes here are written for a 20cm square tin, which gives a pressed layer about 2cm thick. Any tin of a similar area works — a 20cm round tin or a small roasting tin — as long as the depth is roughly right. Thinner layers bake crisper and faster; thicker layers stay chewier and need a few minutes longer.

Score warm, cut cold. Mark the portion lines with a table knife about 5 minutes after the tin comes out of the oven, while the top is soft enough not to crack — then wait until the slab is completely cold and set before cutting through along those lines.

No — do not share them. Flapjacks are loaded with sugar and fat, which are bad for dogs in quantity, and fruit versions often contain raisins or sultanas, which are genuinely toxic to dogs even in small amounts. Some peanut butters also contain xylitol, which is extremely dangerous to dogs. Keep flapjacks for people, and if your dog has eaten any with raisins in, call your vet for advice straight away.
