Honey Flapjack Recipe with Brown Sugar

By Michael

A honey flapjack is the classic British oat bar with honey doing the work of both the golden syrup and most of the sugar: porridge oats bound in melted butter, runny honey and a little light brown sugar, pressed into a tin and baked low and slow. Honey browns faster than syrup and sets a touch softer, so this version bakes at 160°C for 25 to 30 minutes and comes out golden, fragrant and chewy in the middle.

The short answer: Melt 175g butter with 200g runny honey and 75g light brown sugar, stir in 325g porridge oats, press firmly into a lined 20cm tin and bake at 160°C (140°C fan) / Gas Mark 3 for 25 to 30 minutes, until the edges are deep golden and the centre still looks slightly soft. Score while warm, cool completely in the tin, then cut into 12 bars. One honest note before you start: honey is still a sugar, so these are a treat, not a health bar.

The honey flapjack recipe

  • Prep: 10 min
  • Cook: 30 min
  • Total: 40 min
  • Makes: 12 bars

Oven: 160°C (140°C fan) / Gas Mark 3 — middle shelf. You need a 20cm square tin, lined with baking paper.

Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this recipe exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

Method, step by step

  1. Heat the oven and line the tin. Heat the oven to 160°C (140°C fan) / Gas Mark 3. Line a 20cm square tin with baking paper, leaving a little paper hanging over two opposite sides so you can lift the slab out later. Honey flapjacks stick more readily than syrup ones, so do not skip the paper.

  2. Weigh everything before you start. Weigh the oats into a large bowl and have the salt and lemon zest ready. Weigh the honey straight into the saucepan if your scales allow it — honey clings to jugs and spoons, and weighing it into the pan means you lose none of it on the way.

  3. Melt the butter, honey and sugar. Put the butter, honey and light brown sugar in a large saucepan over a low heat. Stir gently until the butter has melted and the sugar has dissolved into the honey. Take the pan off the heat as soon as the mixture is smooth and liquid. Do not let it boil: boiled honey loses its delicate flavour and the finished bars set hard.

  4. Stir in the oats. Tip the oats, salt and lemon zest, if using, into the pan. Stir well, turning the oats over and over until every flake is coated and glossy and there are no dry pockets left at the bottom. The mixture will look looser than a syrup flapjack at this stage — that is normal for honey.

  5. Tip the mixture into the tin. Scrape everything into the lined tin, including the sticky honey mixture clinging to the spoon. Spread it out roughly with the back of the spoon so it reaches all four corners before you press it.

  6. Press down firmly. Press the mixture down hard and evenly with the back of a spoon or the flat base of a glass. Work into the corners and edges. Firm pressing is what stops the bars crumbling later, and it matters even more with honey, because a honey mixture binds a little less tightly than a syrup one.

  7. Bake low and slow. Bake on the middle shelf for 25 to 30 minutes. The bars are done when the edges are a deep golden brown and the centre still looks slightly soft and pale by comparison — it firms up as it cools. Honey browns faster than golden syrup, so check from 25 minutes and do not wait for the middle to colour.

  8. Rest and score while warm. Leave the tin on a wire rack for 10 minutes, then score the top into 12 bars with a sharp knife, cutting about halfway down. Scoring now, while the slab is warm and giving, means clean cuts later instead of shattered edges.

  9. Cool completely in the tin. Leave the flapjack to cool fully in the tin — about an hour. This is when the bars set. Honey flapjacks set a little softer than syrup ones, so be patient: cutting too early is the usual reason they fall apart.

  10. Lift out and cut. Lift the slab out by the paper and cut along the scored lines into 12 bars. Wipe the knife between cuts if it drags. The bars should be chewy in the middle with firmer, deeper-flavoured edges.

Honey or golden syrup: what actually changes?

Plenty of flapjack recipes treat honey and golden syrup as interchangeable, and up to a point they are — both are liquid sugars that melt with butter and bind oats. But they behave differently enough in the oven that a straight swap in the classic recipe often disappoints: the edges catch, or the middle never quite sets. This page is built around those differences rather than pretending they do not exist. If you want the original, our classic flapjack recipe uses golden syrup throughout.

The practical lessons are simple. Bake honey flapjacks lower and check them earlier. Expect a softer bar and store it somewhere cool. And if flavour is the reason you reached for the honey — perhaps a jar from a local beekeeper — you will taste it here, because honey is the main sweetener rather than a spoonful lost in syrup.

Which honey should you use?

Ordinary clear, runny honey from the supermarket is exactly right for this recipe. It melts evenly, measures easily and its flavour survives baking well enough to be noticed. Expensive single-origin or raw honeys, the kind sold for drizzling on toast, are largely wasted in a hot oven — their delicate aromas fade in the heat, and you would taste little difference in the finished bar. Save the special jar for the topping of your porridge and bake with the everyday one.

Set honey works too, with one adjustment: it will not pour or weigh as easily, so warm the jar briefly in a bowl of hot water until it loosens, or scoop it into the pan and let the butter melt around it. Either way, melt gently and never let the mixture boil, whichever honey you choose.

The honest word on honey and sugar

This page exists partly for readers who like honey's flavour, and partly for readers who reach for honey because it feels like the healthier choice. It is only fair to be straight: honey is a sugar. Your body treats the sugars in honey much as it treats other sugars, and a honey flapjack is a sweet, energy-dense treat in the same way the classic is. Swapping syrup for honey changes the flavour and the baking behaviour far more than it changes the nutrition.

If cutting refined sugar is your actual goal, honey alone will not get you there — the banana flapjack recipe and our healthy flapjack recipe take that further by sweetening with fruit. Think of this page as the flavour-lover's version, and enjoy it as what it is.

One safety point belongs here too. Babies under 12 months must not have honey at all — not stirred into food, not baked into bars. NHS guidance is that honey can contain bacteria that a baby's immature gut cannot handle, causing infant botulism, a rare but serious illness, and baking does not remove the risk. These flapjacks are fine for children over one; for babies under one, keep honey out of their food completely.

A no-added-sugar honey version

Searches for honey flapjacks often include "no sugar", and there is a workable version: leave out the 75g light brown sugar, add one ripe mashed banana (about 100g) to the melted mixture and keep the honey as it is. The banana brings its own sugars and a little extra moisture, and the bars come out softer, more cake-like and less firmly set — closer in spirit to our banana flapjacks. Eat them within two days, and do not expect the tidy edges of the main recipe. What we will not do is call it sugar-free: between the honey and the banana, there is plenty of sugar in it — just none of it from the sugar bag.

Keeping honey flapjacks

Cool the bars completely, then store them in an airtight tin at room temperature. They keep well for up to a week, and the texture arguably improves on day two, when the honey has settled into the oats and the bars turn uniformly chewy. Because honey sets softer than syrup, a warm kitchen can make the stack slump and stick together; if your kitchen is hot, separate the layers with baking paper or keep the tin somewhere cooler. They freeze well for up to three months — wrap the bars individually, freeze, and defrost at room temperature for an hour or so before eating.
